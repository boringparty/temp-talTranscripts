  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:11.60" data-timestamp="11.6"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. I want to play something for you today. And before I do, I want to get in front of something. The story is about Guantánamo. And I think lots of us hear "Guantánamo" at this point, and we think, ugh, it's heavy, and it's complicated. And is it really still going on? And don't I really know everything I already need to know about Guantánamo?</p><p begin="00:00:33.99" data-timestamp="33.99">Maybe you've heard our co-workers Sarah Koenig and Dana Chivvis over at <i>Serial.</i> They've been rolling out season four of <i>Serial,</i> and it's all about Guantánamo. And the premise of the show is, enough time has passed that all these people who were there in its heyday can finally talk publicly about what really happened-- what it was like to work there, what it was like to be imprisoned there.</p><p begin="00:00:53.73" data-timestamp="53.73">And you hear all these ordinary people who were thrown into this extraordinary situation where there were guards and interrogators and commanders and detainees and this invented offshore prison with its own weird, brand-new rules. And you finally get a very clear sense of what it was like in there.</p><p begin="00:01:08.39" data-timestamp="68.39">But there are two episodes that they made that are about stuff that's just happened down there in the last couple of years, and about stuff that is going to happen, that's going to be in the news. And honestly, what I heard these two episodes, I thought-- I know this sounds kind of grand. I thought, really, every American should hear these. They're the kind of stories that, when you hear them, it totally changes how you understand the news after that. And they're also just amazing stories.</p><p begin="00:01:33.64" data-timestamp="93.64">And so what we're going to do this week and next, we're going to do something we've never done. We're going to run these two episodes from <i>Serial,</i> two full episodes on our show. And so, right now, today, here is the first of those two episodes. Dana Chivvis, co-host this season of <i>Serial,</i> and she does this one.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:01:50.99" data-timestamp="110.99"> Majid Khan was 16 when his family moved to Baltimore from Pakistan. He went to Owings Mills High School, smoked weed, worked at his dad's gas station, and thought about becoming a DJ under the stage name "Bob Day-si." He listened to Eminem and watched <i>Law</i> <i>and</i> <i>Order</i> and paid attention in Ms. Sanford's social studies class, where he learned about checks and balances and due process. So when Majid Khan found himself locked away in a cold, dark cell at a black site in 2003, he told the CIA he wanted to see a lawyer.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:02:21.93" data-timestamp="141.93"> CIA is just shocked. It's like, what do you thinking, who you are and where you are? It's like, you're talking about rights? Son, the gloves came off a long time ago. That's what they tell me.</p><p begin="00:02:35.33" data-timestamp="155.33">I was like, no. No, I still believe in due process. I still believe in due process. Right or wrong, whatever I did, I believe in due process.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:02:45.40" data-timestamp="165.4"> What he did-- instead of becoming a DJ, Majid joined al-Qaeda, reported directly to Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, the admitted mastermind of 9/11. Naturally, the CIA had questions for him, but the way they asked those questions, they tortured him at black sites for years. And when they were done, they sent him to Guantánamo in shackles and a diaper.</p><p begin="00:03:08.96" data-timestamp="188.96">Majid did bad things. He joined al-Qaeda, wore a suicide vest, tried to kill the Pakistani president. He came up with plots targeting the American government to blow up gas stations and poison the water supply near military installations, to sell poisoned medication in the United States. He delivered $50,000 to another terrorist, money the United States says was used to fund a suicide bombing that killed 11 people and injured at least 81 others.</p><p begin="00:03:37.61" data-timestamp="217.61">And then we did bad things back to Majid. We disappeared him for three years, locked him away for 20, tortured him.</p><p begin="00:03:47.53" data-timestamp="227.53">This story is about two ledgers-- on one side, what Majid did to us; on the other, what we did to Majid. How do you weigh those against each other? In the historical accounting to come, which is worse? Majid's case is the first time at Guantánamo those ledgers are compared, and someone has to render a verdict.</p><p begin="00:04:10.80" data-timestamp="250.8"></p><p begin="00:04:18.99" data-timestamp="258.99">Majid's story starts with his radicalization. Unlike so many former Guantánamo detainees, there's clear public evidence that he was in al-Qaeda. He admits it. So this is a rare chance to hear how someone made the leap from self-described normal American teenager to self-described terrorist. It's actually quite simple, to hear Majid tell it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:04:41.46" data-timestamp="281.46"> The reason I joined al-Qaeda was the Palestine issue.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:04:46.20" data-timestamp="286.2"> It begins with Palestine.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:04:48.27" data-timestamp="288.27"> I had nothing against America. I didn't have it then. I don't have it now. It's your fucking stupid foreign policy that your leaders make-- right?-- that I criticized.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:00.36" data-timestamp="300.36"> Some people might remember President George Bush explaining September 11th, saying that al-Qaeda attacked us because they hate our freedoms, our liberty. Actually, what al-Qaeda really hated was our support for Israel, that we had troops in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, that we had invaded Iraq the first time in 1991.</p><p begin="00:05:20.14" data-timestamp="320.14">When I visited Majid last year in Belize, where he now lives with his wife and two kids in a bright-green cottage not far from the sea, it was November 2023, just weeks since the October 7 terrorist attacks on Israel and the start of the relentless bombing of Gaza in return. He had been watching a lot of cable news coverage of the war and TikTok videos.</p><p begin="00:05:41.29" data-timestamp="341.29">Like so many people, Muslims and non-Muslims alike, he was fired up about the atrocities there, the same kinds of atrocities that had spurred him to join al-Qaeda two decades ago. What he was seeing was Americans making the same mistakes all over again, supporting Israel.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:05:58.45" data-timestamp="358.45"> You guys are so powerful, so smart, and you guys so dumbass at the same time. No offense. [LAUGHS] I mean, I'm not an American, OK, by citizenship. But you guys amaze me every single time.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:06:17.16" data-timestamp="377.16"> What's the dumbass part?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:06:19.63" data-timestamp="379.63"> Not getting the basics! And I'm thinking, there could be another Majid Khan somewhere right now. Now, imagine that, back then, we didn't have YouTube, we didn't have Instagram.</p><p begin="00:06:32.99" data-timestamp="392.99">And I'm thinking, what are they thinking, these kids? And they're so stupid kids. So they could be easily misled. And I'm thinking, how many Majid Khan out there right now that will become like me?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:06:51.10" data-timestamp="411.1"> What led Majid to extremism was a confluence of events. Growing up, he wasn't very religious. He and his parents, his seven siblings, they didn't pray regularly. He says his mother was anti-religion. When he grew a beard, she made him shave it off.</p><p begin="00:07:06.70" data-timestamp="426.7">But in his late teenage years after his family moved to Baltimore, he struggled with his identity. He wanted to fit into American high school life, to be cool, but he wasn't sure that being cool was very Majid. He felt guilty that he didn't pray, that he was a bad Muslim.</p><p begin="00:07:22.30" data-timestamp="442.3">He started learning more about Islam from a group of proselytizers called the Tablighi Jamaat, who would visit him at work and at the mosque.</p><p begin="00:07:30.40" data-timestamp="450.4">And then, when he was 21, Majid's mother died suddenly. She was the most important person in his life, the center of their family. He says he was devastated.</p><p begin="00:07:40.48" data-timestamp="460.48">He started asking the big questions about life and death. He felt like he had sinned up until that point, had wasted his life. He decided to devote himself to Islam. No more partying, no more smoking weed.</p><p begin="00:07:53.83" data-timestamp="473.83">And it was at this point that he started getting more political. At the mosque, they talked about Palestine. The world seemed black and white to him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:08:02.66" data-timestamp="482.66"> At some point in my life, I had to choose a path. Do you want to be siding with the oppressed or oppressor? I chose to be siding with the oppressed.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:08:16.10" data-timestamp="496.1"> Nine months after his mother's death, he went to Pakistan for a bunch of family weddings. His family wanted to arrange a marriage for him, too. And secretly, he also had a fantasy of joining the jihad, maybe training at the camps in Afghanistan.</p><p begin="00:08:31.26" data-timestamp="511.26">Growing up in Pakistan, Majid heard a lot about the mujahideen, how honorable they were, how brave-- like the Navy SEALs in the United States, he says. He didn't particularly care which jihadi group he joined. He talks about the victims of the Jakarta bombing, who've been off stage for most of the hearing, and reads their names out loud.</p><p begin="00:52:58.65" data-timestamp="3178.65">Colonel Foster plays down the torture, saying, quote, "I would concur that Mr. Khan received extremely rough treatment by the individuals that detained him, and it is right for you to consider that conduct. However, despite what Mr. Khan experienced, he is still alive and with us here today, a luxury that the dead and the 11 victims of the JW Marriott Hotel bombing do not have."</p><p begin="00:53:22.49" data-timestamp="3202.49">The jury can choose a sentence anywhere from 25 to 40 years. Colonel Foster asked them to sentence him in the upper range of the spectrum. Major Michael Lyness, one of Majid's defense attorneys, gives a rebuttal. He asks the jury to give Majid 25 years.</p><p begin="00:53:38.99" data-timestamp="3218.99">And then the judge sends him off to deliberate. They're gone for three hours. When they come back, the judge asks Majid to stand, and the foreman reads a sentence. Quote, "Majid Shoukhat Khan, this military commission sentences you to be confined for 26 years, zero months, zero days."</p><p begin="00:53:59.12" data-timestamp="3239.12">Again, Majid doesn't have to serve all this time. His sentence is already set. But 26 years, it's only one year more than the minimum. It's proof that Majid was right to bet that a jury of Americans would give him a lighter sentence once they heard about the torture.</p><p begin="00:54:17.16" data-timestamp="3257.16"></p><p begin="00:54:20.96" data-timestamp="3260.96">The judge thanks the jury, reminds them not to talk about their deliberations. And then, just as he's about to excuse them, the foreman indicates he has something else to say. He has a letter for the defense team. He hands the letter to the bailiff, who walks over and gives it to Majid's attorney, Major Lyness, who leaves it there on the desk, tantalizingly folded in half while the judge wraps up the session.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:54:43.91" data-timestamp="3283.91"> We wanted to open right away. Right? And everybody is acting like it's not a big deal, nonchalant.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:54:52.46" data-timestamp="3292.46"> The judge finally says, Mr. Khan, I wish you all the best in the future. This court is adjourned.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:54:58.77" data-timestamp="3298.77"> And then once the judge left the building, like a pack of dogs, attacked that letter. I was like, let me read it. It's my letter. It's like, let me hold it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:55:12.57" data-timestamp="3312.57"> The letter is written in tidy handwriting on lined paper. It reads, quote, "The panel members listed below recommend clemency in the case of Majid Shoukhat Khan." It's a letter to the so-called "Convening Authority." That's the person who's the final arbiter of Majid's case. It's a military justice thing. I'm not going to explain.</p><p begin="00:55:32.27" data-timestamp="3332.27">It's a letter asking the Convening Authority to go easy on Majid. It says Majid should get leniency because he was denied due process, because he was a young man when he committed his crimes and is no longer a threat for future extremism, and because he was so badly abused by the United States.</p><p begin="00:55:51.23" data-timestamp="3351.23">Quote, "This abuse was of no practical value in terms of intelligence or any other tangible benefit to US interests. Instead, it is a stain on the moral fiber of America." It's signed by seven of the eight jury members, all officers of the United States military, all serving during the global war on terrorism, and who, having compared the two ledgers, rendered their verdict on this era of American history. What Majid did was bad, but what we did to him was punishment enough.</p><p begin="00:56:24.39" data-timestamp="3384.39">I think for lots of people in the military, this conclusion will not come as a surprise. This idea of torturing prisoners isn't some abstract moral debate for them. It's personal, practical.</p><p begin="00:56:36.39" data-timestamp="3396.39">If they get captured by the enemy, they don't want to be tortured. They don't want other Americans to be tortured. They want that kind of thing to be out of bounds. They need the US to hold the line.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Lyness</h4><p begin="00:56:48.01" data-timestamp="3408.01"> The clemency letter is framed on my wall in my office.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:56:52.18" data-timestamp="3412.18"> That's Major Lyness, one of Majid's defense attorneys.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Lyness</h4><p begin="00:56:56.29" data-timestamp="3416.29"> And the original document is in my closet. And I still don't know what to do with it. I don't know who-- does the National Archives want this original document? Because it's currently just in my closet.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:57:10.67" data-timestamp="3430.67"> Why didn't you frame the original on your office wall? Why is that the one in the closet?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Lyness</h4><p begin="00:57:13.88" data-timestamp="3433.88"> Because it's not mine.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:57:14.41" data-timestamp="3434.41"> Oh.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Lyness</h4><p begin="00:57:15.16" data-timestamp="3435.16"> To me, it's the public's. It's a public document. It should be put somewhere where everybody can see it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:57:22.99" data-timestamp="3442.99"> It's the kind of historical document that could, should, end up in a textbook, but it probably won't. It will probably stay in Major Lyness's closet.</p><p begin="00:57:32.88" data-timestamp="3452.88">I reached out to the CIA for comment on this story, for comment on Majid's treatment. In response, a CIA spokesperson sent me a statement-- quote, "The CIA's detention and interrogation program ended in 2009. The CIA has committed and remains committed to never again operating such a program of enhanced interrogation techniques," end quote.</p><p begin="00:57:54.92" data-timestamp="3474.92">So many of the players in this story, in all these Guantánamo stories, just want to put that place behind them. The American government certainly wants us to forget about Gitmo, the black sites, the torture.</p><p begin="00:58:07.58" data-timestamp="3487.58">And Majid, too. He finally got out of prison in February 2023, 11 months after his sentence officially ended.</p><p begin="00:58:15.86" data-timestamp="3495.86">His attorneys met him in Belize, helped him set up his new house. Two months later, Rabia and Manaal joined him. He helped Manaal hang rainbow-colored lights around her room. He spotted her as she put a ladder on a chair on her bed to reach the ceiling. In some photos he and Rabia took shortly after they were reunited, they're both beaming, like newlyweds.</p><p begin="00:58:39.44" data-timestamp="3519.44">Majid spent the first 20 years of adult life in prison. Now, suddenly, at the age of 44, he's a husband and a father. He has a 20-year-old daughter and a new baby. He's got to learn to raise a kid, find a job, convince the banks to give him a bank account.</p><p begin="00:58:54.77" data-timestamp="3534.77">He doesn't want to be talking about any of this stuff, doesn't want to relive it. He couldn't sleep the week I was in Belize interviewing him. He just wants to move on.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Majid Khan</h4><p begin="00:59:04.28" data-timestamp="3544.28"> I don't give a fuck about history. Like, I don't want it to be a part of history-- with all due respect, I just want to be forgotten. You know, who cares? Who's Majid Khan? If I die and bury someone someplace, I will make sure nobody knows where I am. When I die, I just want to make sure only two people come-- my two loved ones. That's it, my daughter and my wife. I'm happy. That's what I prefer.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:59:30.00" data-timestamp="3570"> Majid told me the sentencing hearing, the clemency letter, they were validating in the moment, but writ large, the American public? He doesn't think anyone noticed. And he's probably right.</p><p begin="00:59:42.69" data-timestamp="3582.69">But you know who did notice? The accused masterminds of 9/11-- or their lawyers, anyway. If Majid got a more lenient sentence because he was tortured, could they? Next time.</p><p begin="00:59:59.65" data-timestamp="3599.65"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="01:00:04.62" data-timestamp="3604.62"> That'll be next time on <i>Serial</i> and on our show as well. We'll be running one more episode from the Guantánamo series next week.</p><p begin="01:00:12.56" data-timestamp="3612.56">[BECK, "MODERN GUILT"]</p><p begin="01:00:15.48" data-timestamp="3615.48"></p></div>  </div>

  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.57" data-timestamp="0.57"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:00:03.24" data-timestamp="3.24"> Mr. Goodman and I get these regularly. Sometimes people think we make them up.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:08.66" data-timestamp="8.66"> Vincent Homenick and his boss, Norman Goodman, have to enlist 1,800 jurors a day for the state courts of New York City. And they have heard every possible excuse, real and fake, for avoiding jury duty. And they've saved some of them in this very thick folder.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:00:21.99" data-timestamp="21.99"> Believe it or not, we still get the old, my cat threw up on the summons and I could not send it in.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:29.08" data-timestamp="29.08"> Here's a summons with cat throw-up on the back. It's red and sort of a mustard color.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:00:34.79" data-timestamp="34.79"> We get used tubes of Anusol, stating that the woman has hemorrhoids and can't sit. We get pictures. Instead of a doctor's note, they send in pictures of their condition as if we know it's the actual person.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:52.36" data-timestamp="52.36"> Oh, this is just a random photo of a man in a wheelchair.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:00:55.16" data-timestamp="55.16"> That's right. And he says, as you can see, I'm not able to serve on jury duty.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:59.56" data-timestamp="59.56"> Actually, using a wheelchair, or blindness-- or deafness, for that matter-- will not get you out of jury duty in New York.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Norman Goodman</h4><p begin="00:01:05.11" data-timestamp="65.11"> I got a letter the other day and it was not signed by the doctor.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:09.76" data-timestamp="69.76"> This is Vincent's boss, Norman, who's been in this job since 1969.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Norman Goodman</h4><p begin="00:01:13.63" data-timestamp="73.63"> It was signed by a nurse in the doctor's office. And the nurse said that this prospective juror has a chronic condition. Well, so have we all.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:28.06" data-timestamp="88.06"> Wait. It didn't say what the condition was?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Norman Goodman</h4><p begin="00:01:30.05" data-timestamp="90.05"> Didn't say what the condition was. Just said she's got a chronic condition that prohibits her from serving on jury service.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:37.23" data-timestamp="97.23"> An unspecified medical condition is one they've seen a lot in their jobs as county clerk and chief clerk. They also encounter a certain amount of rudeness. "Dear County Jerk," one letter opens. "Are you brain-dead?" Many New York citizens send back things we cannot broadcast on their official jury forms.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:53.05" data-timestamp="113.05"> Now here's one where someone has just scrawled in black pen to F off.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:02:00.08" data-timestamp="120.08"> We, unfortunately, get a lot of these. They seem to think that they can write anything on an official court document, which is not the case. One particular gentleman, during a noncompliance proceeding, blamed his seven-year-old sister for sending in pornography that was attached to our juror qualification questionnaire. The judge did not believe him. The judge did fine him, and he ended up serving.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:32.29" data-timestamp="152.29"> Serving his jury duty, that is. One man wrote in saying that he was simply too racist to be on a jury. But the single most common excuse? People can't afford the time. They're single moms with no childcare. They're people who will not be able to pay the bills if they take off work. They think they are simply too important for jury duty. None of this cut it with Norman and Vincent, by the way. Court clerks talk to these people, try to accommodate their schedule somehow. And part of the formula is the court clerks appeal to their idealism, their sense of duty.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vincent Homenick</h4><p begin="00:03:03.08" data-timestamp="183.08"> You make it a personal thing. People want to serve. They're generous with their time.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:33.90" data-timestamp="213.9"> And-- not to put too fine a point on it-- they are threatened with fines and jail time if they don't show up. But most do show up. Only 9% of the people who are summoned here for jury duty fail to show. Another 15%, Norman and Vincent say, try to contest or postpone their eligibility.</p><p begin="00:03:48.45" data-timestamp="228.45">And there is one curious thing that the court's own exit questionnaires show, and that is that people who actually get to sit on a case and hear evidence and get to a verdict say they liked the experience way more than the people who just show up and don't get a case and then actually get to go home earlier. Something kicks in for the people who actually get on to juries. Suddenly, it seems important to do the right thing.</p><p begin="00:04:08.71" data-timestamp="248.71">Which brings us to today's program. Today we're going to devote our entire show to the story of somebody who gets called for a job that he does not want-- that nobody would want, really. The kind of family obligation all of us face at some point or another, in some form. Though, this guy faces a more extreme version of it than most of us do. And he goes. Because, corny as it sounds, sometimes duty calls. And sometimes it calls you to Florida. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:40.40" data-timestamp="280.4"> When Josh Bearman grew up in suburban Pasadena, his dad worked for NASA-- was a physicist. He had a stepmom, and he had a brother named Ethan.</p><p begin="00:04:48.91" data-timestamp="288.91">That's not his whole family. When we first ran this next story in 2007, his mom was living in Florida along with his half-brother, David. Josh had gone for a while, not spending much time with them, but one day, he had no choice.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Bearman</h4><p begin="00:05:03.40" data-timestamp="303.4"> Here's how different the two halves of my family are. My brother Ethan, who grew up with me in California, left high school early for a conservatory, where he studied French horn. He has a house, a wife, a son, and fills in with the LA Philharmonic. But my brother David in Florida, well, here he is.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Parks</h4><p begin="00:05:21.39" data-timestamp="321.39"> Yeah, I just got this police report for my DUI, and I'm gonna read it here. But I can already see a couple of things that are absolute bull-[BLEEP]. This wasn't even the original officer, and he's gonna talk about what he saw.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Bearman</h4><p begin="00:05:33.41" data-timestamp="333.41"> David's been arrested a bunch of times-- not for anything violent, just a DUI, a shoplifting charge or two. Then there's my father, the nuclear physicist. He sometimes launches at Caltech's Athenaem, surrounded by Nobel laureates. Here's my mom describing someone she might have lunch with.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Josh's Mom</h4><p begin="00:05:52.91" data-timestamp="352.91"> OK, Terron-- I used to call him the creeper. He's a street hustler, and that's all he'll ever be. He's waiting for a liver transplant.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Bearman</h4><p begin="00:06:06.84" data-timestamp="366.84"> The last time I lived with my mother, I was nine. After my parents' divorce, my half-brother David-- he was just an infant at the time-- stayed with my mother, and my father got custody over me and Ethan. That's when my mom started drinking seriously. As she got worse, we only caught glimpses of her. Occasional drunken phone calls late at night, a few sightings on family holidays.</p><p begin="00:06:29.45" data-timestamp="389.45">For several years in there, I didn't even know where she was. Once, in an effort to connect with her, I arranged for me, my brother Ethan, and my girlfriend Ronnie to all fly across the country to meet my mother and David for a visit at my grandparents' house. My mother didn't show.</p><p begin="00:06:52.42" data-timestamp="412.42">A few years ago, my grandparents staged a rescue. They brought my mom and David to Florida, where they all lived among the palm trees and strip malls.

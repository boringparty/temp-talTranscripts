  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.58" data-timestamp="0.58"> So John, you're from Minnesota. Right?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:00:02.27" data-timestamp="2.27"> I am from Minnesota.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:03.52" data-timestamp="3.52"> John Biewen's a public radio guy, makes documentaries. And he says growing up in Minnesota, there was a way that people usually talked about the history of the state.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:00:11.72" data-timestamp="11.72"> And it's often told in a kind of light and self-deprecating way that northern Europeans came over, and they staggered out onto the tundra and said, oh, this reminds us of home. And they didn't know enough to keep going to some more pleasant place. And so they built their sod huts and laid out their farms.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:33.49" data-timestamp="33.49"> Sometimes there's an oh, by the way, Indians were here when we got here.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:00:38.34" data-timestamp="38.34"> It's either an afterthought or it doesn't come up at all. And there was always a sense, too, growing up in a place like southern Minnesota, that nothing had ever happened there.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:48.97" data-timestamp="48.97"> Which, John points out, is completely weird. Southern Minnesota-- he's learned since he grew up there-- was the location of one of the major wars with the Plains Indians, the US-Dakota War. It happened during the same period as the Civil War.</p><p begin="00:01:01.66" data-timestamp="61.66">More people died in that war than died at Little Big Horn or Wounded Knee. In fact, John's own hometown-- Mankato-- was the site of the largest mass execution in US history of Dakota warriors. 38 of them were hanged by the order of Abraham Lincoln, a little more than 150 years ago, the day after Christmas, 1862.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:01:21.22" data-timestamp="81.22"> And the place where that hanging happened, it's right in the heart of downtown. I would've hung out at the mall not far from there, a few blocks down the street. I would have ridden past that spot on my way to Pizza Hut with my buddies, who knows how many times. And never-- it just never-- I didn't hear about it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:41.89" data-timestamp="101.89"> He says he doesn't remember it being taught in school or being mentioned at all his entire childhood by anybody, even though this war is arguably the biggest event in the state's history. It paved the way for Minnesota to become the place it is today, led directly to all the Dakota being kicked off their own land and expelled from the state under conditions that killed hundreds of them.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:02:02.05" data-timestamp="122.05"> And if anybody should have heard about it, it should have been me. My parents worked on the McGovern campaign. They admired Martin Luther King. My dad was a high school English teacher who taught <i>To</i> <i>Kill</i> <i>a</i> <i>Mockingbird.</i> And every Sidney Poitier movie that came on TV, they would sit us down and watch it. And I was raised to have a social-- look, I went on to be a public radio reporter.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:27.29" data-timestamp="147.29"> I was going to say. Really, how did you end up in public broadcasting?</p><p begin="00:02:30.52" data-timestamp="150.52">[LAUGHTER]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:02:32.13" data-timestamp="152.13"> I was raised to be aware of the nation's racial injustices. It's just that they all happened someplace else, not in Minnesota.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:50.80" data-timestamp="170.8"> For the last 17 years, John's lived down south, in North Carolina. And he says that at some point the contrast in how people saw their own history down south really hit him.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:03:00.41" data-timestamp="180.41"> Not that people down south are sitting around reminiscing about Stonewall Jackson all day long. I mean, that's really not the case. But there is a general and pervasive understanding in the South that something very big happened in the 1860s that shaped the place right up to the present. And that's what's missing in Minnesota.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:26.52" data-timestamp="206.52"> So a while back John Biewen started traveling around the state with two questions-- first, what exactly happened back in 1862? And, second--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:03:36.08" data-timestamp="216.08"> Why don't we talk about it? Why haven't we internalized this story in Minnesota into our understanding of the place?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:43.92" data-timestamp="223.92"> And so, today, the week after Thanksgiving, we bring you this story of Indians and settlers. I found so many of the details of what John learned to be completely surprising. Maybe you will too. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> Stay with us.</p><p begin="00:04:05.95" data-timestamp="245.95">So now I turn things over to John Biewen.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:04:10.05" data-timestamp="250.05"> In setting out to understand what happened in southern Minnesota in 1862, I called up Gwen Westerman. She agreed to spend a few days with me driving around the state to the spots where it all occurred, so I could tell the story here on the radio.</p><p begin="00:04:24.14" data-timestamp="264.14">We meet up in Mankato. I grew up there but haven't lived there for 30 years. She's lived and taught there for the last 20 years.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:04:32.12" data-timestamp="272.12"> More people probably know about the town of Mankato than realize it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:04:36.46" data-timestamp="276.46"> That's us, in Gwen's SUV.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:04:38.60" data-timestamp="278.6"> Because in the television series <i>Little</i> <i>House</i> <i>On</i> <i>The</i> <i>Prairie,</i> the Ingalls family lived in Walnut Grove. And whenever Ma and Pa wanted to get away from the kids, they came to Mankato.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:04:52.47" data-timestamp="292.47"> Gwen's an English professor at Minnesota State University in Mankato and a member of the Dakota tribe. She's in her 50s like me. She grew up in Kansas, part of the Dakota diaspora. A lot of her family was banished from Minnesota after the 1862 war-- not that she's always known that herself.</p><p begin="00:05:11.75" data-timestamp="311.75">See, I grew up in Mankato, a white kid knowing next to nothing about the bloody history that happened beneath my feet. Gwen got a teaching job and moved to Mankato, also knowing nothing about that history. She found out later that some older people in her family did know.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:05:28.37" data-timestamp="328.37"> Then, when I took the job here at MSU, my father and my uncle would say, well, we'll see how long you last there. But they never did explain why they had questions about whether I would stay here or not.</p><p begin="00:05:48.80" data-timestamp="348.8"></p><p begin="00:05:55.73" data-timestamp="355.73">So, December 26, 1993, I had a friend who worked at the university. She said, there are Indians coming. She was also Indian. She said, there are Indians coming. Let's go see what they're doing.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:06:12.71" data-timestamp="372.71"> That's all she said?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:06:13.51" data-timestamp="373.51"> That's all she said. So I said, OK. She came and picked me up.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:06:18.22" data-timestamp="378.22"> Gwen didn't know it, but she and her friend Kimberly were going to see the annual ceremony for the Dakota warriors who were hanged in the town square in 1862.</p><p begin="00:06:27.11" data-timestamp="387.11">They arrived late for the ceremony and stood in the back for the prayers and songs. They still didn't know what the event was about. And then it was over.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:06:37.07" data-timestamp="397.07"> Kimberly and I got back in the car and she was driving. And she started to pull around the corner of the building. And I don't know if your parents drove fast on back country roads when you were small, up and down the hills.</p><p begin="00:06:54.50" data-timestamp="414.5">We used to love it when my parents would do that because you'd go over the top of the hill and then your stomach would kind of come up. But as we rounded the corner of the building, my stomach came up just like that. And I started to sob.</p><p begin="00:07:14.00" data-timestamp="434">And Kimberly stopped the car and said, what's wrong? What's wrong? And I told her I had no idea what was wrong. It was incredible sadness. I didn't really say anything to anybody about that until a few days later when I called my uncle and explained to him what had happened.</p><p begin="00:07:36.38" data-timestamp="456.38">After I finished my story, he was quiet for a long time. Gwen and I went.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gwen Westerman</h4><p begin="00:53:25.78" data-timestamp="3205.78"> Well, we're standing here today in the midst of cornfields on all sides of this intersection, under an incredibly beautiful blue sky, at the state line between South Dakota and Minnesota, as we make that walk back into Minnesota 150 years after we were forced to leave.</p><p begin="00:53:58.92" data-timestamp="3238.92"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:54:03.57" data-timestamp="3243.57"> The highway patrol has closed off a section of road, where South Dakota's Highway 34 meets Minnesota Highway 30.</p><p begin="00:54:10.05" data-timestamp="3250.05">[HORSE NOISE]</p><p begin="00:54:10.74" data-timestamp="3250.74">There are a dozen Dakota on horseback and many more on foot. A handful of white Minnesotans greet them with signs saying "Welcome home."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:54:19.38" data-timestamp="3259.38"> Thank you! Thank you for coming back! Thank you.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:54:23.76" data-timestamp="3263.76"> About 40 yards shy of the state line--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:54:26.52" data-timestamp="3266.52"> That's about as far as I can let you guys go.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:54:31.41" data-timestamp="3271.41"> --organizers ask reporters to turn off our equipment if we want to witness the ceremony up close.</p><p begin="00:54:38.39" data-timestamp="3278.39">Maybe 100 people form a big circle around eight older Dakota women. And that he repudiated Governor Ramsey's words about extermination and exile.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:55:22.81" data-timestamp="3322.81"> Minnesota's governor, Mark Dayton, said he was appalled by Governor Ramsay's words in 1862. And here it is.</p><p begin="00:57:05.66" data-timestamp="3425.66"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:57:12.55" data-timestamp="3432.55"> The history of every place is more complicated than the people who live there like to believe. And that's why we have a hard time about who we are.</p><p begin="00:58:49.43" data-timestamp="3529.43">So that the kind of self-congratulatory history that passes for heritage, it keeps us from seeing ourselves and doing better.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>John Biewen</h4><p begin="00:59:03.47" data-timestamp="3543.47"> One place to see fake history-- on a state flag of Minnesota.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.93" data-timestamp="0.93">When it comes to teenagers, we always get it wrong. Even in the most widely scrutinized, widely reported stories, we get it wrong. Take Tiananmen Square. This week is the 10th anniversary of the crackdown and the massacre there. And you think you know the story. Idealistic students trying to democratize their government. But in fact, it was more complicated than that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:00:22.69" data-timestamp="22.69">The majority of students were like me. I was a very typical student at that time. And our knowledge of what democracy was was really limited.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:34.02" data-timestamp="34.02">Wen Huang was part of the student movement, first in Shanghai, where he was a graduate student at the time, then in Beijing at Tiananmen Square right up until the crackdown on June 4, 1989. And he says that for him and most of the students he knew, the movement was not about turning China into more of a democracy. He says students wanted the government to cutdown on corruption, do something about inflation, allow them to criticize the government without being thrown in jail or kept from good jobs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:01:01.90" data-timestamp="61.9">It never occurred to us that we wanted to overthrow the Communist government. It was like the Communist party was a God-given thing. And we were born with it. We're going to die with it. And the only way to make it work for us is just to have some modest reforms within the government.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:20.23" data-timestamp="80.23">But Wen says that for most of the students he knew, most of the students in the movement, he believes, there was another motivation besides politics, an equally compelling, if not more compelling, motivation.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:01:30.46" data-timestamp="90.46">It was really like a big party. I never heard about Woodstock until I came to the US.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:36.84" data-timestamp="96.84">Woodstock, yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:01:37.49" data-timestamp="97.49">Woodstock. And then later on, I watched a video tape. And then suddenly, I realized that the student movement in 1989 was just like a big-- similar to the Woodstock experience.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:50.41" data-timestamp="110.41">Why? What were you seeing that was similar in 1989 in China, in Tiananmen Square, and in Shanghai that was similar to what was going on at Woodstock?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:01:59.78" data-timestamp="119.78">The festive atmosphere and the playfulness. And we were singing pop songs, and people playing guitars.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:18.74" data-timestamp="138.74">It was exciting, Wen says. Midterms were coming up. Papers were due. And everybody would skip class together to go to demonstrations. This is not to say that there was not political idealism behind all this. There was. But as one of the best known student leaders, Chai Ling, said in an article in <i>The</i> <i>New</i> <i>Yorker</i> magazine commemorating the 10th anniversary of Tiananmen Square, the demonstrations, even the hunger strikes weren't primarily political as far as she was concerned. It was about a kind of sheer pleasure in living, she said, a rock and roll bravado. Wen Huang agrees.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:02:51.74" data-timestamp="171.74">I think that was kind of accurate statement. I think there was more of an honest assessment of what's motivated us to take part in this movement. Because I noticed when I first came out here, the student leaders who escaped China--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:09.53" data-timestamp="189.53">The student leaders who escaped, uh-huh.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:03:10.82" data-timestamp="190.82">The student leaders who escaped China, when they came over here, they exaggerated the motivation. They portrayed themselves as such democracy fighters, as if they knew so much about democracy. And that has a lot to do with how Americans have this romantic vision of the Tiananmen Square movement.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:33.22" data-timestamp="213.22">Of course, it goes further than this. We Americans have a romantic vision of what it means just to be a student, what it means to be young. And we ascribe a lot of motivations and meaning to what young people do that simply don't bear much relation to reality. Today on our program, we try to set the record straight with a few typical case examples. From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:03:55.87" data-timestamp="235.87">Today's program, The Kids Are Alright, stories in which people try to set the record straight about actions that adults seem to widely misinterpret. Act One, Where The People Walk Around Upside Down. A story about what you think you know about the 1989 student uprising in China versus what really happened. Act Two, When Czechs Bounce. The true tale of how young people's music in Czechoslovakia in the 1940s and '50s seemed to pose a threat first to the Nazi occupation, then to communist authorities.</p><p begin="00:04:28.11" data-timestamp="268.11">Act Three, When We Were Animals. Someone who helped perpetrate high school violence when he was a teenager tries to find the blurry line that divides childhood prank from social pathology. Act Four, When We Were Angels, in which we hear the purest possible student uprising imaginable, the most innocent, documented by an actual student using the crude tools of telephone answering machine and shiny, red boom box. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Where The People Walk Around Upside Down</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:03.31" data-timestamp="303.31">Act One, Where the People Walk Around Upside Down. Growing up in China, Wen Huang believed that China was an advanced country, a civilized country, which provided for all its citizens, not like those countries with poor people in them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:05:16.93" data-timestamp="316.93">I remember when I was growing up, each time I couldn't finish my meal, my mom always say, &quot;Oh, you gotta finish your meal. Just imagine how lucky you are. People in America are starving.&quot; And of course, I heard the same thing.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:33.42" data-timestamp="333.42">And of course, once you came to this country, everybody told you, &quot;My mom would say to me, 'Yes, finish your meal because there are children starving in China.'&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:05:40.87" data-timestamp="340.87">Yeah, I actually believed that. I remember that was our impression of America. We thought China was very strong. We felt very lucky to be under the socialist system.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:49.73" data-timestamp="349.73">In a more advanced system, where that wouldn't happen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:05:51.10" data-timestamp="351.1">In a more advanced system, everybody's equal. And then suddenly, in the 1980s, when China opened up to the outside world, we suddenly felt that we were left behind. And there was the phrase saying that we were at least 30 or 40 years behind the United States and England. And we felt humiliated in a way.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:09.73" data-timestamp="369.73">Students like Wen had friends who'd gone overseas, who'd seen that in Europe and America, the grocery stores had 30 different kinds of cookies, that it was fantastic prosperity abroad. Meanwhile, opening to the West meant the end of wage and price controls back home in China, and prices were skyrocketing. Wen remembers living on $10 a month American and watching the price of tomatoes climb from $0.01 a pound to $0.30 a pound. It just didn't seem like there was any kind of future to be had in China, anything to be idealistic about. That was what sent students to the streets, Wen says, not some idea of American-style democracy, which they didn't know much about anyway.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wen Huang</h4><p begin="00:06:45.31" data-timestamp="405.31">We really had a mentality of &quot;We could save this world.&quot; We were young. There was a Chinese phrase, saying, &quot;A newborn calf doesn't know the danger ahead.&quot; That is exactly what we were. We felt like the whole future-- we could change the future. We could change the country. As how we're going to change it, we didn't know.</p><p begin="00:07:02.94" data-timestamp="422.94">So we grew up watching these movies about the martyrs, for example, during the early communist movements, how people would face the bullets to die for a cause. And that really had the impact. We felt like we wanted to be part of it. I remember during the Cultural Revolution, thousands of Red Guards-- like my uncle and my aunt, they were Red Guards-- thousand Red Guards, they traveled hundreds of miles to Beijing to see Chairman Mao Zedong, who was the leader of the communist movement. He was like a living god.</p><p begin="00:07:46.19" data-timestamp="466.19">And my aunt and my uncle told me that they were young, and they would walk hundreds of miles. And sometimes, when they took the train, the train was so crowded that people had to stand in the toilets for 7 or 8 hours or 20 hours. And also, people have to cram into the luggage rack just to go to Beijing to see Chairman Mao. And later on, during the 1980s, we found that was kind of stupid. The Red Guards, they really did a lot of damage.</p><p begin="00:08:13.23" data-timestamp="493.23">But on the other part was the fantasy. I felt like, I wish I could live in that time to experience the excitement that my aunt and my uncle, they lived. Because each time they reminisced that, their voice started changing. They got excited. It's like part of the experience. I always felt like I wanted to be that part of experience.</p><p begin="00:08:43.07" data-timestamp="523.07">That was why, when I was in Shanghai after the students' hunger strike, I felt like I really wanted to go where the action was. So I took the train to Beijing. Gordon and Jack tell the story.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gordon</h4><p begin="00:38:17.03" data-timestamp="2297.03">Chris was in the back of the truck. And he saw these chains. And as we were driving by the power plant, he had the idea that we should go and try to blow it up.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:38:31.75" data-timestamp="2311.75">I remember Chris took the chain, which was the bigger of the two, and looped it in half and just tried to wing it over the fence at one of the transformers.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gordon</h4><p begin="00:38:44.01" data-timestamp="2324.01">And honestly, I didn't think that-- I thought this was a futile effort. I didn't think that it was going to do anything.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:38:53.95" data-timestamp="2333.95">I couldn't have been looking directly at it when it connected, when the chain connected with the transformer. And I was blinded for a minute there. And the next thing I noticed was how dark it was in that already dark, rural area. But it was so dark because every street light was out. Every house light was out. It was real dark.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gordon</h4><p begin="00:39:22.32" data-timestamp="2362.32">That's when the fear set in on me. I thought, &quot;I really want to get out of here because whatever we've done, it's serious. And this is really bad.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:39:31.27" data-timestamp="2371.27">All I remember is Chris begging us to get going. &quot;Let's get going.&quot; I don't remember anyone saying that they felt bad for having done untold-- I don't know how much damage-- how much that would have cost to repair. That must have been huge. But yeah, I certainly don't remember anybody expressing any remorse for it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gordon</h4><p begin="00:40:04.23" data-timestamp="2404.23">And the whole way back, we were just talking about how crazy it was, and how people on life support systems could be suffocating, and that kind of thing. It was exciting.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:40:24.75" data-timestamp="2424.75">I point out here that most of us weren't seen as problem kids. When I wasn't out with my friends, I collected Chinese stamps, went to Hebrew school, practiced the viola, not that a sociopath can't play the viola. Then there came this point when their talk about pranks, if you could still call them that, shifted from property to people. This was the beginning of the end of us as a group of friends. Here are Philip and Jack.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:40:47.18" data-timestamp="2447.18">We were trying to decide one evening what would be our next step in what we were going to be doing violent-wise. And objects-- destroying public property or private property was just not exciting enough anymore. And so we went out one night. We were driving around downtown. And we saw this staggering drunk.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Philip</h4><p begin="00:41:10.74" data-timestamp="2470.74">And Chris got out of the car with his Taser. And he was acting really sweet to this guy and just chatting with him, being chummy. And the guy was smiling. And then Chris took the Taser out of his pocket or wherever and showed it to him.</p><p begin="00:41:32.31" data-timestamp="2492.31">And he said, &quot;Look at this. Do you know what this is?&quot; The guy shook his head. &quot;Well, it's for doing this.&quot; And he put the Taser on the guy's arm or something. And it didn't knock him down. But it was, obviously, extremely painful. And the guy shrieked and pulled away.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:42:00.54" data-timestamp="2520.54">There was another time when Chris attacked a vagrant that mirrored a scene from the film <i>A</i> <i>Clockwork</i> <i>Orange,</i> which we watched and rewatched during those years.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:42:07.55" data-timestamp="2527.55">He kicked him. I remember. And he did it in this overly dramatic way. And I remember laughing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Philip</h4><p begin="00:42:20.04" data-timestamp="2540.04">We were all exhilarated, and we were all laughing. I think we were in awe of what we had done. But because we didn't know how to talk about it, we were just laughing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:42:28.24" data-timestamp="2548.24">And then all of a sudden, I realized how twisted all that was.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:42:42.69" data-timestamp="2562.69">I was there in the car for the first of these incidents. I remember feeling trapped. I remember wanting out. That was it for me. I dumped my friends. I was surprised to hear it wasn't long after that everyone left for the same reason. The strange thing was that each of us thought he was the only one who reacted adversely to this new violence, that the others somehow enjoyed it.</p><p begin="00:43:12.85" data-timestamp="2592.85">When I started to hear about the Littleton shootings and when I started to hear about the shooters, the kind of kids they were, I found myself thinking about this ugly slice of my personal past, things I hadn't thought about in years. I found myself asking questions about who we were exactly. Here's Jack.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:43:28.23" data-timestamp="2608.23">I turn on the TV, and I saw what was happening at Littleton and the descriptions of the kids that were coming out to the media were-- I remember thinking, &quot;My god, that was us.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:43:43.49" data-timestamp="2623.49">This is Philip.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Philip</h4><p begin="00:43:44.73" data-timestamp="2624.73">When I first heard about Littleton, there were these kids in trench coats. And I wore a trench coat. And there were these kids that called themselves The Trench Coat Mafia. And I don't think we had a name for ourselves, but we had that attitude. And I won't go so far as to say, &quot;It could have been me.&quot; Because I never would have killed anybody. Maybe.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack</h4><p begin="00:44:10.78" data-timestamp="2650.78">Definitely, I remember thinking that it's way too close to home.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:44:28.90" data-timestamp="2668.9">Philip.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Philip</h4><p begin="00:44:29.88" data-timestamp="2669.88">I don't think there's any difference between what they did in Littleton and what we were doing. When Eric Harris decided to take a gun to school and kill his classmates, he was thinking the exact same way we were when we decided to stay out at night and mash people's mailboxes. Or when we decided to take that monument and destroy it. It was that same anger, inarticulate anger.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:44:55.72" data-timestamp="2695.72">Here's Gordon.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gordon</h4><p begin="00:44:57.10" data-timestamp="2697.1">I guess in some sense, what we were doing wasn't all that different. But I suppose, if the circumstances in my life were a little different, I suppose that I could have walked over that line. But the circumstances of my life weren't that extreme.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Paul Bravmann</h4><p begin="00:45:48.57" data-timestamp="2748.57">How are we supposed to think about Littleton? Either they see it as a kind of innocent boys-will-be-boys joyride. Or they see it as social pathology. And they feel they have come down on one side or the other. Based on personal experience, I think I can say, it's a little of both.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:46:17.16" data-timestamp="2777.16">Paul Bravmann is a freelance writer living in New York City.</p><p begin="00:46:21.12" data-timestamp="2781.12">[MUSIC - &quot;TOO SWEET TO DIE&quot; BY THE WACO BROTHERS]</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Act Four: When We Were Angels</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:46:49.39" data-timestamp="2809.39">Act Four, When We Were Angels. Let us end our program today with an example of the most innocent childhood prank, the most innocent student uprising imaginable. Hillary Frank is in graduate school, studying drawing at the New York Academy of Art. A few months ago, she mailed our radio show a story that she wanted to get onto the program.</p><p begin="00:47:05.95" data-timestamp="2825.95">She had recorded the interviews using her microcassette answering machine. And then she edited the interviews and recorded the parts that she said, her script, by dubbing and recording onto her shiny, red boombox. For all the crude sound quality, the story sounded remarkably like a story on <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> But for one reason or another, and time considerations, that unsolicited story never made it onto the radio.</p><p begin="00:47:29.51" data-timestamp="2849.51">This week, though, she has put together another story using the same style, which we added music to here at the radio station to make it fully sound like a story on our show. It is about an incident which happened to her back when she was an undergraduate.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:47:45.17" data-timestamp="2865.17">Tufts University is a pretty straight place. Entertainment for most people means fraternity keggers. It's not the sort of place you'd expect people to watch a guy sitting on another guy's shoulders pretending to be a giant. It happened by accident in 1994. My friend, Scott, was the top half.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:48:05.64" data-timestamp="2885.64">It was beginning of the school year. And we were bored one night. And we decided to go up to the quad. And I guess, basically, Jeff got up on someone's back. And he started yelling and screaming about how giant he was and how magnificent he was. And I think, actually, right after that, I might have gotten on someone's back and said, &quot;Yeah, I'm also giant.&quot;</p><p begin="00:48:31.80" data-timestamp="2911.8">And I guess that really struck a chord in me. I thought that was pretty amusing. I guess I thought a lot about it. It actually did start me thinking along a particular path.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:48:57.14" data-timestamp="2937.14">Scott talked about the idea one night in the dining hall. The next day, on the way to class, he saw signs all over campus that said in bold print, &quot;I am nine feet tall. Come see Giantman. 8:00 PM on the quad.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:11.98" data-timestamp="2951.98">Scott had no idea who put them up. He learned later that the signs were posted by a guy who had overheard him talking at dinner. Scott decided he would go to the quad at the specified time and undertake the challenge. To pull this off, he would need to create a character and a costume for the giant. He gave Giantman a booming voice.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:49:32.39" data-timestamp="2972.39">I don't even know exactly. It was something like, &quot;Behold, I am giant.&quot; Not even loud, but just weird and suggesting man-ness in a vague way. I don't know.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:49:57.92" data-timestamp="2997.92">Scott asked his tallest friend, Podo, to act as Giantman's legs. They grabbed some props before heading up to the quad, a blanket to wrap around their middle to hide Podo, a long wooden staff, and a black, curly wig, like the guys in Kiss.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:50:14.78" data-timestamp="3014.78">We had picked a point in the bushes, Podo and I. We really couldn't see what was going on out on the quad. And so the time came, and I got on his shoulders, basically, and tied the blanket around my waist. And we walked out.</p><p begin="00:50:28.46" data-timestamp="3028.46">And I remember there just being maybe 10 people, 15 people. And I remember them being way on the other side and running. What was so ridiculous about it was there was just a handful of people, and they were so spread out. But they were all coming towards me. It was like, what the hell am I doing here?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:50:54.32" data-timestamp="3054.32">They decided to plan another Giantman appearance the following week. They posted more signs and told everyone they knew, &quot;Go see Giantman. It'll blow your mind.&quot; Word spread quickly. And amazingly, they were able to keep it secret that they, themselves, were Giantman. Most students believed there was an actual nine-foot man come to Tufts for some mysterious reason. I was friends with these guys, and I didn't even know yet.</p><p begin="00:51:20.17" data-timestamp="3080.17">At the next appearance, almost 200 people were waiting for Giantman and chanting his name. It was like a political rally. Some of them carried signs that said things like, &quot;We love you Giantman. Why are you here?&quot; &quot;Save us from ourselves, Giantman.&quot; &quot;Nine feet of loving.&quot; And &quot;Giant freak, go home.&quot;</p><p begin="00:51:41.38" data-timestamp="3101.38">Giantman made his way onto the quad, and the quad went wild. People came rushing out of their dorms to see what was going on. When Giantman reached his fans, he made a small speech. &quot;I am giant,&quot; he boomed. &quot;I am huge, and I have brought you butterscotch.&quot; He then threw cellophane-wrapped butterscotch to the crowd, and they dove for it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:52:06.27" data-timestamp="3126.27">On the walk up, I just stopped in the bookstore and saw some candy. And I was trying to think, &quot;What was the most ridiculous candy that nobody every ate?&quot; There was butterscotch there in some kind of cellophane wrapper. It looked like nobody ever was eating it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:52:32.74" data-timestamp="3152.74">Butterscotch became Giantman's trademark treat. When he ran out of things to say, he would revert to throwing candy. The fact of the matter is Giantman had very little to tell the Tufts community other than &quot;My strength is amazing. My girth is enormous. And my height is unequaled.&quot; He would brag like this for only two or three minutes and then retreat back to the bushes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:52:57.66" data-timestamp="3177.66">Problem was, Podo would get really tired really quickly. He walked out really fast. He almost was running out. And he got really tired and didn't even know where he was going. It must have just looked absolutely idiotic.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:53:15.28" data-timestamp="3195.28">So he couldn't see?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:53:16.68" data-timestamp="3196.68">Yeah, his eyes were covered. I remember I would sit on his head, and I would put both my hands on his head, kind of give him direction by maybe forcing his head in a certain direction.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:53:27.71" data-timestamp="3207.71">Giantman became a phenomenon. Enthusiasts wore &quot;I love Giantman&quot; t-shirts, which had silhouettes of a huge man with a bulging middle. There was once a parade across campus with noise makers and a trumpet to greet him. Another time, there were torch jugglers and bodyguards. Letters were written to the student newspaper pro and con Giantman. Teachers were mentioning Giantman in class. There was a discussion in an ethics course in which people who hadn't seen Giantman argued about whether or not we were exploiting a freak of nature.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:54:07.73" data-timestamp="3247.73">Do you think that if Giantman had been an actual political cause that you would have gotten such a big turnout and there would have been such a big deal about it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:54:15.71" data-timestamp="3255.71">Yeah, I doubt that. That's one of the things, I think, that was a big draw about it was that it didn't have any meaning. And for whatever reason, people were really drawn to that. If it had meaning or was trying to pitch some idea or something, it would seem less real, I think. There was really something fundamentally interesting and truthful about Giantman, I guess. There is something that people are drawn to that absurdity for whatever reason.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:55:10.60" data-timestamp="3310.6">By the end, Giantman's following had grown to about 350 people. I don't think any of us have had any experience like it since. When you're a student, it still feels like something exciting might happen at any moment. Life feels full of all this potential. But when you get out of school, that potential just doesn't seem to be there.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:55:32.86" data-timestamp="3332.86">What do you know now?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:55:35.45" data-timestamp="3335.45">Well, I'm an engineer.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:55:37.90" data-timestamp="3337.9">What kind of engineer?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:55:39.13" data-timestamp="3339.13">Computer engineer, designing computer circuits and things like that.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:55:45.88" data-timestamp="3345.88">And do you have Giantman-like experiences today?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Scott</h4><p begin="00:55:53.08" data-timestamp="3353.08">No, not really. I'm not parading around, talking about my magnificence.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:56:11.85" data-timestamp="3371.85">There's actually a recording of Giantman's final public appearance. There was a band called The Electric Fun Machine that dedicated a song to him. And he appeared with them at a concert on the quad.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>The Electric Fun Machine</h4><p begin="00:56:23.98" data-timestamp="3383.98">[SINGING] The Giantman. Giantman. Nine feet tall. Giantman.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Giantman</h4><p begin="00:56:41.64" data-timestamp="3401.64">Students of Tufts, fear me not for I am the benevolent Giantman. I have come to show love. As a symbol of my benevolence, I shall, once again, shower you with--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Hillary Frank</h4><p begin="00:56:57.76" data-timestamp="3417.76">And then Giantman threw butterscotch candy.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:05.30" data-timestamp="3425.3">Hillary Frank in New York.</p></div>  </div>

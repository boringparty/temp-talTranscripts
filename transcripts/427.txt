  <h3>Act One: Message In a Bottle</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.73" data-timestamp="0.73">Hey there, everybody. So I'm not going to beat around the bush. For this week's radio program, we think we may have found the original recipe for Coca-Cola. And I am not kidding. I am not kidding. One of the most famously guarded trade secrets on the planet, I have it right here, and I'm going to read it to you. I'm going to read it to the world. And I'm going to make my case for why I think it's real, despite whatever Coca-Cola might say. And that is just one of the two stories on today's program.</p><p begin="00:00:29.39" data-timestamp="29.39">From WBEZ Chicago, it is <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act Two: Ask Not What Your Handwriting Authenticator Can Do for You; Ask What You Can Do for Your Handwriting Authenticator</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:34.77" data-timestamp="34.77">Probably the best story about how protective Coca-Cola is of its secret formula comes from this book that was written by Charles Howard Candler. His Dad, Asa, did not invent Coca-Cola, but founded the Coca-Cola company back in 1892.</p><p begin="00:00:48.41" data-timestamp="48.41">And Charles, the son, wrote this. &quot;One of the proudest moments of my life came when my father initiated me into the mysteries of the secret flavoring formula, inducting me into the holy of holies.&quot; Charles then says, &quot;Incredibly there was no written formula and the labels had been removed from all the containers of the ingredients.&quot; So they were, quote, &quot;identified only by sight, smell, and remembering where each was put on the shelf. And I thereupon experienced the thrill of making up, with his guidance, a batch of merchandise 7x.&quot; Merchandise 7x. Merchandise 7x is the cartoonishly super secret, cloak and dagger name they give the flavoring mixture in Coca-Cola.</p><p begin="00:01:29.00" data-timestamp="89">Charles' dad, Asa, was so paranoid about anybody else getting hold of the secret formula for 7x that, even though he was president of Coca-Cola-- he's president of the company-- he would go through the company mail himself and remove the invoices for any ingredients that had been purchased, so that nobody in the accounting department could read what ingredients had been bought for the product. I learned all this from a history of Coca-Cola, a book written by investigative journalist and historian Mark Pendergrast.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mark Pendergrast</h4><p begin="00:01:56.04" data-timestamp="116.04">The company has always said, and as far as I know it's true, that only two people at any given time know how to actually mix the 7x flavoring ingredient. And that these people never travel on the same airplane in case it crashes. It's this carefully passed on secret ritual. And that the formula is kept in a bank vault at Sun Trust, which used to be the Georgia Trust Company.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:22.77" data-timestamp="142.77">Of course this makes no sense at all. Why can't the two guys fly on a plane if the formula is sitting inside a bank vault? Coke's official line on this these days is that they simply do not talk about how many people know the recipe. Though the reason that most of us think that of it as one of the biggest trade secrets in the world is probably because that's the story Coca-Cola likes to tell.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Announcer</h4><p begin="00:02:43.26" data-timestamp="163.26">Coke's secret formula. Only two guys in the world know it. If something happened to one, the formula would be lost forever. And then, cookouts would be catastrophic.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:54.95" data-timestamp="174.95">In this particular Coke commercial, I guess the premise is that each of the two guys only knows half the formula. It's just one of several commercials that have come out recently that poke fun at the myth of the super secret formula, while at the same time bolstering and selling that myth.</p><p begin="00:03:09.66" data-timestamp="189.66">Coke even has this thrill ride at its museum in Atlanta, The World of Coca-Cola, where you follow two characters who are trying to unlock the secret formula for Coca-Cola. So with all this money, all this energy, all these millions of dollars spent over the years to convince us of just how impossibly, importantly, iconically secret the formula to Coca-Cola is, it was, a surprise when we stumbled across an article in Coke's hometown paper, the <i>Atlanta</i> <i>Journal and</i> <i>Constitution</i>, February 18, 1979. Not on the front page, but buried. Buried back on page 2B. One of their columnists, Charles Saulter, without any fanfare at all, published what looks like the original recipe for Coca-Cola.</p><p begin="00:03:51.92" data-timestamp="231.92">He found it in a pharmacist's old book of recipes. All the recipes, including the one for Coca-Cola, are written by hand. Most of them are for various ointments and remedies.</p><p begin="00:04:00.75" data-timestamp="240.75">And if that seems like a strange and random place to find this kind of thing, well, Coca-Cola was invented by a pharmacist, and it was originally sold at pharmacy soda fountains. The article says that the book passed from one pharmacist to another until it fell into the hands of one Everett Beal, who owned a drug store in Griffin, Georgia, 40 miles from Atlanta. He is mainly identified in the story as a fishing buddy of the columnist.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:04:24.53" data-timestamp="264.53">Hello?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:25.44" data-timestamp="265.44">Judy?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:04:26.51" data-timestamp="266.51">Yes, you're late.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:27.84" data-timestamp="267.84">I know I am.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:04:30.26" data-timestamp="270.26">Did you have a nice flight back home?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:32.16" data-timestamp="272.16">Everett Beal passed away July 2010. I called his widow Judy at her home in Georgia so many times asking for an interview that at one point she said to me, &quot;This is how you people won the war, isn't it?&quot; &quot;You people&quot; meaning northerners.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:04:45.43" data-timestamp="285.43">And you wanted to know how Everett got this book?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:49.26" data-timestamp="289.26">Yes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:04:50.00" data-timestamp="290">Well RR Evans was a good friend of Pemberton's.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:53.37" data-timestamp="293.37">OK, let's just pause the tape right there to explain who these guys are. The Pemberton that she's talking about is John Pemberton, the guy who invented Coca-Cola in Atlanta back in 1886. Like I said, he was a pharmacist. But he was also a maker of patent medicines. He made something called Globe Flower Cough Syrup. He made a blood purifier called Extract of [? Stalengia. ?]</p><p begin="00:05:13.39" data-timestamp="313.39">RR Evans-- that's the second person that she mentioned-- Judy says, was another pharmacist in Atlanta at the time.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:05:18.62" data-timestamp="318.62">And they were best friends and blah, blah, blah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:21.85" data-timestamp="321.85">This book of recipes, Judy says, originally belonged to Evans. She says his name is all over the book. Apparently after his friend Pemberton invented Coke, Evans copied down the formula in this book, calling it Cocoa-Cola</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:05:34.67" data-timestamp="334.67">That's right.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:35.53" data-timestamp="335.53">And after Evans died, the book started its travels.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:05:38.86" data-timestamp="338.86">Evans gave it to RM Mitchell from Griffin, Georgia. And then Mitchell's widow was a friend of my husband's and told him, after he passed way, to come over and get any books he wanted to out of the library. So Everett picked out the book.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:57.92" data-timestamp="357.92">What's it look like?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Judy</h4><p begin="00:05:59.45" data-timestamp="359.45">Oh gosh, it's 133 years old. It's just leather bound.

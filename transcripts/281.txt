  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.28" data-timestamp="0.28"> It took not one, not two, but three, three big breaks to get Marisela and Yadira to college. Every time they got a break, they thought it was a done deal, nothing more to say, and then they just had to start over from scratch and wait for the next bolt of lightning. They were honors students, in the top 10% of their class, AP courses, all that stuff. They were ambitious. They wanted to go to college. But they didn't have the money for it.</p><p begin="00:00:25.32" data-timestamp="25.32">Then one day, in their junior year, a man comes to their high school talking about a scholarship program called the Daniels Fund, which sends students to special college prep classes for months. And then if they do well in those classes, they get their entire tuition paid at the college of their choice. Great, right? It's a first big break.</p><p begin="00:00:41.82" data-timestamp="41.82">They get in the program. They start the special classes. They're happy. They're doing great. And then they hear a rumor that despite what they had been told by that man who came to visit their class, the scholarships only go to US citizens. Marisela and Yadira-- they live in the US, but they are not citizens. Their parents brought them to the US from Mexico when they were little kids. So they went to their adviser. And they were told, yeah, there was a misunderstanding. They weren't going to be able to get any scholarship money when these special classes were finished.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela</h4><p begin="00:01:07.41" data-timestamp="67.41"> And I remember she said that they didn't want us in the prep program because we were taking up the space of the students that could actually get it. That's when it really hit me. Like woah. And I remember getting very depressed and I even started skipping some classes. Because I remember thinking that why do so much work for nothing? So that's where I would just go into my room and just stay there the whole day. I would talk on the phone. And then when there was no one to talk to, I would just sit there and think.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:50.39" data-timestamp="110.39"> Here's how harsh this was. Marisela and Yadira had two friends in advanced placement classes whose families also came over from Mexico. Except the friends were born in the United States. They could get the scholarships. They had social security numbers, which you need before you can get most financial aid. And suddenly, it felt kind of hard to be friends with them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela</h4><p begin="00:02:09.55" data-timestamp="129.55"> We were all the same, and we all had the same type of parents and the same type of living. And it was just like-- I was mad that they had their papers and that we didn't when, in fact, we were the same. It was just like luck or something, that they got more lucky than we did. And it was just not fair because it's not like you could-- we couldn't buy luck. We could buy a fake social security, but we can't buy the luck to get a real one.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:43.34" data-timestamp="163.34"> And then, Marisela and Yadira got their second big break. They found a scholarship program that helps undocumented students. But again, there was a catch. Yadira got a full scholarship. Marisela, she didn't. They both cried when they got the news.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela </h4><p begin="00:02:59.96" data-timestamp="179.96"> And that whole day it was really hard for us to talk to each other.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:03.40" data-timestamp="183.4"> This is Marisela.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela</h4><p begin="00:03:04.53" data-timestamp="184.53"> Not because-- I wasn't mad at her. But just because everything came tumbling on me. Oh, I felt like less, like less than her. I saw my life in an instant, what it was going to be like. And I saw bad things coming, like me working, something that I don't want to do, or marrying young just to get out of a situation.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:32.34" data-timestamp="212.34"> Later that day, Marisela told her mom.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela</h4><p begin="00:03:35.19" data-timestamp="215.19"> I cried so hard. And she didn't know what to do. She was like, oh, don't worry. She started crying too. She was all like, I'll raise enough money, you could go to beauty school, like to do your hair and to do your nails. You could go there. I'm like, oh mom. I didn't know what to tell her, because for her it was a big thing. But to me, that's not what I wanted.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:00.64" data-timestamp="240.64"> This happens more often than you'd think. Public schools in America have to accept undocumented kids up through high school. But once they graduate-- this is over 50,000 kids a year-- they have no more rights. They can't work because they're not citizens. And though, theoretically, they can go to college as international students from another country, in practice, most undocumented kids are like other kids. They need financial aid, which is a problem because most scholarships follow what the federal government does. And federal student aid, Pell Grants, can't go to undocumented students.</p><p begin="00:04:31.13" data-timestamp="271.13">Given all that, it was especially amazing that Marisela got her third big break. A private donor heard of her situation and ponied up most of the money that she would need for college. The school put up the rest. At first, Marisela thought, OK, three big breaks-- three huge big breaks-- now they're set. They are set. But actually, as time has passed, other problems have become clearer to them. For instance, it's a long shot that they're ever going to become citizens. Even if they marry Americans, it would take years to make that happen legally. And in the meantime, they're scared of getting caught by the INS. They worry about the future as much as they ever did.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marisela</h4><p begin="00:05:06.83" data-timestamp="306.83"> I'm working really hard in college. But I still don't know what's waiting for me. It seems like I'm just living life to the day. I have goals. But I don't have ways of how I'm going to get there.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:27.29" data-timestamp="327.29"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Today on our program, My Big Break, stories of people who get their shot, their chance, their once in a lifetime opportunity that they think can solve all their problems, and then, of course, after the big break they discover all sorts of other problems that they never imagined before. Act One of our program today, Take My Break, Please. In that act, two entertainers get the biggest possible chance anybody ever could to make the big time, and they blow it. Act Two, What Happens in Baghdad Stays in Baghdad. Two young men decide that it would be interesting to be part of the rebuilding of Iraq, and so they take a bus to Baghdad during the war and the bombings and the kidnappings and the danger, and they try to make their mark. Act Three, Oedipus Hex, the true story of a third grader who stumbles upon a rare opportunity at making his mother happy, and driving his abusive father out of the picture forever. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Take My Break, Please</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:32.25" data-timestamp="392.25"> Act One, Take My Break, Please. Well, we begin our program with a classic big break which results in an equally classic result. David Segal tells the story.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Segal</h4><p begin="00:06:45.24" data-timestamp="405.24"> Mitzi McCall and Charlie Brill were a sketch comedy act back in the early 1960s, playing small clubs around the country, mostly in Los Angeles where they lived. They were married. They still are actually. And they were struggling. Then one day they got a phone call that changed their lives.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:01.75" data-timestamp="421.75"> We were sitting at home, and I don't know what we were--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mitzi Mccall</h4><p begin="00:07:04.26" data-timestamp="424.26"> Starving.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:04.74" data-timestamp="424.74"> Starving. No, we weren't starving.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mitzi Mccall</h4><p begin="00:07:07.22" data-timestamp="427.22"> Yes. I was starving.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:08.25" data-timestamp="428.25"> Well, you were hungry that day.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mitzi Mccall</h4><p begin="00:07:10.21" data-timestamp="430.21"> Oh, was that it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:11.83" data-timestamp="431.83"> Yeah. And the phone rang and it was our manager, Mace Neufeld, and he said, guess what? What? I got you on <i>The</i> <i>Ed</i> <i>Sullivan</i> <i>Show</i>. And we let out a scream because that was THE show. It's like today, young comics want to get on--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mitzi Mccall</h4><p begin="00:07:25.43" data-timestamp="445.43"> Dave Letterman.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:26.28" data-timestamp="446.28"> Jay Leno, Dave Letterman. Well, this was--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mitzi Mccall</h4><p begin="00:07:29.17" data-timestamp="449.17"> The ultimate.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charlie Brill</h4><p begin="00:07:29.98" data-timestamp="449.98"> Bigger. If you got a shot on <i>Ed</i> <i>Sullivan</i>, you had a shot at stardom. We were just so thrilled.

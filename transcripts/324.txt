  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.40" data-timestamp="0.4"> Michael had been in the business for over 35 years, and he never had a customer like this one. Michael designs and sells tombstones. And this customer, who we're not going to name for reasons that will become clear the further into the story I get-- we'll just call him Mr. X-- Mr. X is what they call in the funeral business a pre-needs customer. Pre-needs is basically a polite funeral business way to say he wasn't dead. He was buying a plot and a tombstone before he needed them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:00:29.35" data-timestamp="29.35"> You know, he explained that this was for himself, and he said he had some special requests that he wanted on the memorial. So I obviously asked what they were, and we started going through it. He wanted his picture on there. He also wanted to know if he could put his son's names on and their pictures, and he would also like to put their dates of death.</p><p begin="00:00:52.11" data-timestamp="52.11">And at that point, I say, "oh, I'm very sorry for your loss." And that's when he says, "no, they're not dead." And I was slightly speechless for a moment because I'm trying to figure out what's go on. Is he trying to tell me that he's going to kill them? Or is it-- what's happening? You know.</p><p begin="00:01:19.26" data-timestamp="79.26">And so we proceeded slightly further in the conversation, and he basically takes out a sheet of paper and hands it to me. And I start reading it, and he said, "this is what I want on the monument."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:30.93" data-timestamp="90.93"> And what did it say?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:01:32.66" data-timestamp="92.66"> Well, I wish I had it and saved it. It was really very blunt, to the effect that, "my two sons stole money from me." And it kind of broke his heart. In his eyes, they were not alive any longer.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:51.94" data-timestamp="111.94"> They were dead to him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:01:53.03" data-timestamp="113.03"> They're dead to him because of something they did to him.</p><p begin="00:02:03.79" data-timestamp="123.79">When I first read it, it didn't even click. And then I read it again, and I looked at him and said, "are you serious?" And he said, "yes." I said, "OK." I said, "it's pretty strong." And he said, "that's what I want."</p><p begin="00:02:21.26" data-timestamp="141.26">And the cemetery wouldn't allow him to put it on there in that way. And you need to, you know, kind of soften it up a little bit.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:29.40" data-timestamp="149.4">And I should say now, this monument is actually standing. The guy still is alive. The monument is standing in a cemetery right now, and it has his picture-- kind of a big picture of him at the top, and his name, and date of birth, and then blank for the date of death-- and then underneath it there are pictures of two of his sons. Then it says, "in memory of." And it says each of their names and the date of death. And I guess those are the dates when they broke his heart and became dead to him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:02:54.50" data-timestamp="174.5"> Exactly.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:55.50" data-timestamp="175.5">And then underneath-- let me just ask you to read the text of what it says underneath. And leave out their names.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:03:00.66" data-timestamp="180.66"> OK. It says, "I'll never forget"-- and someone's name-- "and"-- someone's name. "Even in death, these two people put an everlasting mark on my heart. Money will come between family no matter how close you are. Even in death, I still feel the pain."</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:28.74" data-timestamp="208.74">I called this guy. I talked to him. In fact, I've talked to him a couple times, and he didn't want to come on the radio or anything. And he explained that in his mind, this was the perfect plan. Because basically, he could finally get the last word.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:03:43.21" data-timestamp="223.21"> Yeah, and it's cut in stone. That's a saying around here. It's permanent. It's there forever, for eternity.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:57.01" data-timestamp="237.01"> Now, when I talked to Mr. X about all this, he told me that he carries around photos of the monument, and he shows those photos to people. He likes seeing their reactions. He says that he visits the monument every five or six weeks, polishes it up. He has a fantasy of his sons coming to his funeral and seeing the monument for the first time. He pictures the looks on their faces.</p><p begin="00:04:17.77" data-timestamp="257.77">But the problem with this plan-- and Mr. X is the very first person to admit this-- the problem with this plan is, who wants to wait until they're dead to get the satisfaction? You know? On the other hand, if they find out now, while he's alive, then they might get mad, they might argue. And that would defeat the whole point of the whole thing. He wouldn't get the last word. And Michael sees another problem with Mr. X's plan.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:04:42.66" data-timestamp="282.66"> Maybe they're just going to look at it and go, "oh, he's crazy. Who cares?" You know what I mean?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:49.10" data-timestamp="289.1">Oh, wouldn't that be horrible for him?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:04:50.49" data-timestamp="290.49"> Yeah. And then they'd just walk away.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:54.34" data-timestamp="294.34">That would be the worst case scenario, that they would see it and just shrug.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:04:57.59" data-timestamp="297.59"> And it wouldn't surprise me if they spotted the monument-- I'm almost thinking that if the sons saw that, they would probably try to remove their photographs, at least, which they could do. They could break them or remove them. Because I think he was even concerned about that.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:13.53" data-timestamp="313.53">Oh, he was?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael</h4><p begin="00:05:13.91" data-timestamp="313.91"> Yeah. I think he might have been going to leave some money somehow set up in a trust or something to take care of replacing them or something like that if something happened to them.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:22.87" data-timestamp="322.87"> You know, in the end, this is the problem with Mr. X's plan: all of eternity is stretching in front of Mr. X, and in that time, somebody can deface the tombstone, or a great, great, great, great, great, great grandson can decide that it is embarrassing, this tombstone, and have it removed.</p><p begin="00:05:38.68" data-timestamp="338.68">Well, today on our show, we have stories of people who put together what they think are brilliant plans, and then all sorts of things that they didn't really account for finally get to them. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> Life, distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:05:53.91" data-timestamp="353.91">Our show today, My Brilliant Plan, in two acts. Act One, Mr. Adam's Neighborhood. That act is a simple story involving a money-saving, information-getting, life-embracing plan that just had one thing, one little thing going against it, and that is where it was happening. Act Two, Tragedy Minus Time Equals Happily Ever After. In that act, an 11-year-old child makes up a plan, a completely unlikely kid's plan, the kind of plan that no adult would ever think could work. And then the kid sticks with that plan into his 20s, into his 30s, into his 40s, into his 50s. He sticks with this plan for so long that somehow this fantastically unreasonable plan becomes, decades later, somehow suddenly rather reasonable. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Mr. Adam's Neighborhood</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:48.71" data-timestamp="408.71"> Act One, Mr. Adam's Neighborhood. This story takes place in Iraq. But it's not a big story. Nobody is dying in the story. Democracy doesn't hang in the balance in this story. But it illustrates some things about what it's like to be an American trying to get anything at all done in Iraq, even something simple and ordinary, like rent a house, and how naive a person can be, how much you can stumble even doing that.</p><p begin="00:07:13.25" data-timestamp="433.25">One of our regular contributors here at <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> Adam Davidson, moved to Baghdad back in 2003. This wasn't long after the US invasion. He was a reporter for the public radio show <i>Marketplace.</i> He was reporting there, by the way, especially on corruption in the American reconstruction projects. Eventually, he won the Daniel Schorr Journalism Prize, and was a finalist for the duPont-Columbia Award.</p><p begin="00:07:32.49" data-timestamp="452.49">But back when he first arrived in Baghdad, he had to figure out where to live. And he noticed that not only was it expensive to live in the big hotels where Western journalists stayed, those hotels were just starting to get bombed. So he came up with a plan-- it seemed like a very clever plan at the time-- to rent a house. He was going to rent a house. He was going to get a bunch of roommates to share the costs, save some dough. And they were going to live in a regular neighborhood. Not the green zone, which is the protected zone that Americans control. This was out in the city, in a regular, unprotected neighborhood.</p><p begin="00:08:03.23" data-timestamp="483.23">One of our show's producers, Nancy Updike, was in Iraq reporting for a month back then, and she stayed at that house, and recently talked with Adam about the saga of what came to pass there. DeWitt's statement was a validation to me of my life, because here was this great man saying, "your father would be proud of you." Maybe he'd be able to stir up time inside the machine, but if Ron followed his own equations to their logical conclusion, the furthest he'd ever be able to go back is the moment the machine was first switched on. And finally, in his 50s, without him ever really noticing, the plan he made as a boy got replaced with a more adult plan, one based on an adult's understanding of what was actually possible. So by the time he found out he'd never see his father again, he didn't need to.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:42.37" data-timestamp="3462.37"> Josh Gleason in Portland, Maine.

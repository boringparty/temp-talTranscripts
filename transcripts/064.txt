  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:54.10" data-timestamp="414.1"> Well, Act One and Two. Most of our ideas about the start of summer come from school, from that moment when we would get out of school in May or June and feel that flush of freedom. Even as adults, I think we yearn for that feeling to hit us again come Memorial Day, come the start of summer. The first of these two stories that we'll be hearing interlaced throughout the hour is by Ron Carlson, a story of somebody on summer break from college. Ron Carlson is a writer who lives in Arizona.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ron Carlson</h4><p begin="00:07:21.09" data-timestamp="441.09"> In 1967, the year before the year that finally cracked the 20th century once and for all, I had as my summer job delivering medical oxygen in Phoenix, Arizona. I was a sophomore at the University of Montana in Missoula, but my parents lived in Phoenix and my father, as welding engineer, used his contacts to get me a job at Air and Oxygen Company. I started there doing what I called &quot;dumbbell maintenance,&quot; the kind of make work that's assigned to college kids. I cleared debris from the back lot, mainly crushed packing crates.</p><p begin="00:07:56.04" data-timestamp="476.04">These were good days for me. I was 19 years old, and this was the hardest work I had ever done. The days were stunning, starting hot and growing insistently hotter. My first week, two of the days had been 116. And in that first week, I learned what not to touch, where not to stand. And I found the powerhouse heat simply bracing. I lost some of the winter dormitory fat and could feel myself browning and getting into shape.</p><p begin="00:08:34.25" data-timestamp="514.25">I was having a hard ride through the one relationship I had begun during the school year. Her name was Linda Enright, a classmate, and we had made the mistake of sleeping together that spring. Just once, but it wrecked absolutely everything. I watch myself arrive at his terrible house and wheel the tank toward the door, and I search myself for compassion, the smallest shred of fellow feeling, kindness, affection, pity. But all I found was repulsion, impatience. Give me the truck keys and a job to do. I could get up and hit the phone booth in less than 10 minutes and make that phone ring, have her reach for it. But I didn't. I'll let her sit there until the last sunlight rocked through the den, broke, and disappeared.</p><p begin="00:41:16.36" data-timestamp="2476.36">The last day of my job in the summer of 1967, I thought-- and this is the truth-- I thought for the first time of what I was going to say last to Elizabeth Rensdale. I tried to imagine it, and my imagination failed. When I climbed from her bed the nights I'd gone to her, it was just that-- climbing out, dressing, and crossing to the door. She didn't get up. This wasn't <i>Casablanca</i> or <i>High</i> <i>Noon</i> or <i>Captain</i> <i>Blood</i>. This was getting laid in a hot summer desert town by your father's oxygen delivery man. There was no way to make it anything else.</p><p begin="00:42:04.80" data-timestamp="2524.8">Some of my customers knew I was leaving and made kind remarks or shook my hand or had their wife hand me an envelope with a twenty in it. I smiled and nodded gratefully, and then turned, business-like, to the dolly and left. These were strange goodbyes because there was no question that we would ever see each other again. It had been a summer, and I had been their oxygen guy.</p><p begin="00:42:27.72" data-timestamp="2547.72">But there was more. I was young and they were ill. I stood in the bedroom doors in Sun City and said, take care. And I moved to the truck and felt something, but I couldn't even today tell you what it was. To the people who didn't know, who said, &quot;See you next week, David,&quot; I didn't correct them. I said, &quot;See you,&quot; and I left their homes too. It all had me on edge.</p><p begin="00:43:02.96" data-timestamp="2582.96">The rain moved in for the day, persistent and even, and the temperature stalled and hovered at about 100. I thought Gil would be pleased to see me so soon in the day. But I surprised him, knocking at the door for five full minutes before he unlocked it, looking scared. Though I had told him I would eventually be going back to college, I hadn't told him this was my last day.</p><p begin="00:43:28.58" data-timestamp="2608.58">There was no chatter right off the bat, no sitting down at the table. He looked like the victim of a fire.</p><p begin="00:44:53.03" data-timestamp="2693.03">&quot;Gil,&quot; I said. &quot;I'm late.&quot; I looked at him, but he did not look at me. His eyes were still floating, and it looked like he was grinning, but it wasn't a grin. I kicked my door open and jumped down into the red mud and went around the front of the truck.</p><p begin="00:45:23.33" data-timestamp="2723.33">When I opened his door, he did not turn or look at me, which was fine with me. I lifted Gil like a bride, and he clutched me, his face wet against my face. I carried him to the front door. He was light and bony like an old bird. And I was strong, and I felt strong, but I could tell this was an insult the old man didn't need. When I stood him there, he would not let go, his hands clasped around my neck. And I peeled his hands apart carefully, easily, and I folded them back toward him so he wouldn't snag me again.</p><p begin="00:45:57.49" data-timestamp="2757.49">&quot;Goodbye, Gil,&quot; I said. He was an old man alone in the desert. He did not acknowledge me.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Scott Carrier</h4><p begin="00:46:30.42" data-timestamp="2790.42"> It happened in Desolation Canyon, a wilderness, a place of natural forces. A child. This man, an instructor, who came to fly the body out of the canyon.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:47:20.91" data-timestamp="2840.91"> The boy who died was a 16-year-old student. I couldn't speculate.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Scott Carrier</h4><p begin="00:48:09.41" data-timestamp="2889.41"> What's it like? Over.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:50.52" data-timestamp="2990.52">I waited a minute to over and out to Nadine while the news subsided in me. It was on Scottsdale Road at Camelback where I turned right. Over,&quot; I said.</p><p begin="00:50:05.71" data-timestamp="3005.71">In front of the Rensdale's townhouse, I felt odd going to the door with the empty dolly.

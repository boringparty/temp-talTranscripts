  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.46" data-timestamp="0.46"> Andrea was working at her first temp job at Bank of America in San Francisco, learning how to use the computers. And she made this chart with Microsoft Excel. It's very professional-looking. The line that goes across the bottom of the chart lists the years between 1987 and 1997. The left side of this chart, what you might call the y-axis lists numbers of people, going from 0 to 7. There's a legend, and the title at the top of the chart, &quot;My Love Life: A 10-Year Span.&quot;</p><p begin="00:00:38.99" data-timestamp="38.99">It's basically all the people Andrea made out with or slept with over 10 years. And once you laid it out this way, in numbers, it's hard not to start thinking about your stats. Like the huge jump she made in 1994 three to seven.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrea</h4><p begin="00:00:56.53" data-timestamp="56.53"> So seven was my first jump. That was definitely my highest year, but then '95 followed up with a four, which was higher than all the previous year. So I thought at that time that I'd broken a barrier and that it was just going to be smooth from here on out. And then '96, I hit another high with five. So '97 is unexplainable in terms of trends of the chart.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:21.17" data-timestamp="81.17"> In '97, her number is 0.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrea</h4><p begin="00:01:23.80" data-timestamp="83.8"> I think what explains it is I spent the first six months living at home with my mom.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:32.91" data-timestamp="92.91"> Then there are the dramatic increases in 1994 and 1996-- very big jumps. Andrea's phrase for what happened in those years? Her slutty years abroad.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrea</h4><p begin="00:01:42.38" data-timestamp="102.38"> Well, definitely leaving the country brings my numbers up immediately. Me and David Hasselhoff do better in Europe.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:55.54" data-timestamp="115.54"> So it all started as this funny thing to just try on the computer, kill time with, but as Andrea looked at the chart, it was actually kind of reassuring. First of all, it made her look like such a player, which she wasn't really feeling at the time.</p><p begin="00:02:11.86" data-timestamp="131.86">And the second thing was that, when you reduce people to just numbers, everything that was painful about your relationships with them-- it just vanishes. The person you kissed who never called you again. The really embarrassing person you'd rather just forget ever happened. It's all gone. The trauma's gone.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrea</h4><p begin="00:02:27.86" data-timestamp="147.86"> It is just numbers. Looking at it this way, the people totally go away. I can't look at a year and think, oh, that was that person, or whatever. Because so many of these things on their own, I would normally classify as failures. They were rejections or something painful. But when I look at it in the context of all this, now these are all my scores. These are my successes. So it's a way to turn things around, I guess.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:57.00" data-timestamp="177"> This is the thing about numbers. They're easier to deal with than feelings, than the thousand ambiguous and difficult things that happen in life, real life. And so today on our program, we bring you stories of people who have tried to take reality, take emotional situations, take things that never should be expressed in numbers, and render them in numbers. From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it is <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:03:24.46" data-timestamp="204.46">On today's program, Act One, the story of one couple who decided to put a corporate annual report about their own relationship. Act Two, Paint by Numbers, two artists who decided what to paint by hiring a polling firm to ask people what they wanted to see in art. Act Three, When Days are Numbered. Jerry Davidson of Worcester, Massachusetts has been keeping a list of everything he has done since 1955. What does not make it onto the list turns out to be a lot bigger than what does. Act Four, The Salesman. A Husband tries to market himself to his own wife using all the tools of corporate brand marketing. Act Five, Break It Down, calculating the cost of love. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Corporate Culture</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:20.44" data-timestamp="260.44">Act One.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:04:21.63" data-timestamp="261.63"> It was called &quot;Bi-Annual Report on Status of Relationship with Significant Other.&quot;</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:25.86" data-timestamp="265.86"> And so why would any couple choose to analyze their relationship using the tools and graphs and methods of a corporate annual report?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:04:35.20" data-timestamp="275.2"> I think because it seemed the most emotionless. I mean, it seemed the most opposite of a relationship.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:45.42" data-timestamp="285.42"> So once a week, for half a year, Travers Scott and his boyfriend, David Eckard, sat down and separately filled out questionnaires to measure the status of their relationship. They tried to be as scientific as possible. They had six categories of evaluation: efficiency, feedback, profitability-- which is what they each got out of the relationship-- security, mergers-- which was their jargony word for their sex life-- and loyalty, which in their case meant brand loyalty to the relationship.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:05:13.10" data-timestamp="313.1"> Yeah, how hemmed in you felt by the relationship. Did you fantasize about being single? Did you have sex outside the relationship? Things like that. Most of the structural things you see applied to relationships are very touchy-feely, escape from intimacy, co-dependency, psychobabble kind of stuff. And I wanted to use something that didn't have that quite Montessori warm, fuzzy, huggy feel to it, but something that was very cold and removed. And so I wanted to take that a step further in applying it to people, and apply it to a relationship, apply something that cold to love and see what would happen.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:55.66" data-timestamp="355.66"> And does that work?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:05:57.63" data-timestamp="357.63"> Oh, no, it's completely futile. It doesn't work at all. I mean, in all these pieces, we were always trying to apply the most antithetical structures of scientific, masculine, scientific method, hyper-analytical, to the most irrational, chaotic, emotional things in our lives.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:17.06" data-timestamp="377.06"> And you're saying it doesn't work? Why? What falls apart? What do you lose?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:06:20.81" data-timestamp="380.81"> Because it's chaos. It has benefits, but you have to fudge the science a lot. You're trying to quantify things that are really hard to quantify. Like one thing that happened was when we were separated, and we were rating the sexual area, some weeks I would give us a really, really high rating because I was thinking about Dave a lot sexually, and I was having all these warm, fuzzy thoughts and everything. So I gave it a really high rating. Dave gave it a complete zero because we were separated and we weren't having sex, therefore it was a zero.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:06.53" data-timestamp="426.53"> But it does get you into that problem of, how do you even quantify how much love is occurring between people?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:07:13.07" data-timestamp="433.07"> Yeah. I mean, it was easy with things like the phrase &quot;I love you.&quot; I could easily count how many times that happened. But trying to determine exactly what was a non-verbal expression of love. Like, I would count gifts, things like that, but do you count an affectionate look? Do you count a punch in the shoulder?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:32.77" data-timestamp="452.77">And how would you count something like, OK, you're at dinner and he reaches over and just puts his hand on your hand as you talk and then takes it away a moment later?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:07:41.96" data-timestamp="461.96"> That would be a debatable non-verbal expression. But then, that gets really hard, because if you count every instance of physical expression, is that every single time someone touches you? How do you determine between, is he squeezing your hand because he loves you so much or is he squeezing your hand because you're saying something really stupid and embarrassing?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:08:06.36" data-timestamp="486.36">So while you were doing this particular project, did you feel that things just got extremely self-conscious in a way that wasn't perhaps the most desirable outcome?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>D. Travers Scott</h4><p begin="00:08:15.32" data-timestamp="495.32"> It did at first. In the first few months especially, it was very self-conscious. And I knew Dave was keeping track himself of how many times he was saying &quot;I love you,&quot; and things like that. But after a while, you just forget about it and it just becomes a habit. Just every day, OK, how many times did this happen, how many times did that happen? I had a little notebook stuck in my jacket pocket that I'd whip out like a irritating reporter or something. &quot;You said I love you,&quot; and I'd be scribbling it down. And people would kind of glare at me. It made me look really, really insecure, I think.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:08:54.53" data-timestamp="534.53"> Yeah, I mean, I've got to say, if I were involved with somebody and I knew that they were counting how many times I said &quot;I love you,&quot; I would feel constrained to keep my average up.

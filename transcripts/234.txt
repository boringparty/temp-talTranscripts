  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.41" data-timestamp="0.41"> So Neil Chesanow, do you have a copy of your book, <i>Please</i> <i>Read</i> <i>This</i> <i>for</i> <i>Me,</i> there with you?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:00:05.00" data-timestamp="5"> Yes, I do, right in my greasy little claw.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:06.98" data-timestamp="6.98"> Perfect. Can I ask you to open up just to the opening page of the preface to read from the preface?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:00:14.24" data-timestamp="14.24"> "When you have something very important but really tough to tell the man in your life, wouldn't it be great if you could just reach for a book that starts the conversation for you? Imagine being able to turn to the appropriate page, give an open book to the man you love, and ask, 'Please read this for me.' 'What is it?' 'Just read it, OK? Page 73. It's only a few lines.'</p><p begin="00:00:37.37" data-timestamp="37.37">Once the man you love has the book in his hands, a glance will do the job. Each page is an emotional telegram."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:44.24" data-timestamp="44.24"> Neil Chesanow's book is now long out of print. But back in the 1980s, when it was published, it represented, I think, a kind of utopian endgame for what self-help books could accomplish. Instead of explaining in a general way how you should handle this situation or that situation in your life, the book, <i>Please</i> <i>Read</i> <i>This</i> <i>to</i> <i>Me,</i> cut to the chase, went to the next logical step. It actually gave you a script. You're in this situation? Say this. Here are the actual words you can use to kick off the conversation you need to have.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:01:12.38" data-timestamp="72.38"> I tried to come up with a book that would not just tell you how to communicate, but actually spark conversation.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:22.63" data-timestamp="82.63"> Let me just read some of these titles to give people a sense of what some of these are like. Page 111, "It's time to admit you don't have a drinking problem. You're an alcoholic." Page 82, "You act like Cary Grant in public and Archie Bunker at home." Page 134, "I think about marriage all the time." Page 148, "Maybe I'm not ready to have a baby." Page 136, "You'd probably prefer if I were an orphan." Let me ask you to read the one on page 135.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:01:52.53" data-timestamp="112.53"> OK. This one is entitled, "It could be that I'm falling out of love." A difficult subject. "Once I thought I loved you. Now I'm not so sure. Yes, we've changed over time, but that's only part of it. I feel confused. I don't know what I feel.</p><p begin="00:02:17.06" data-timestamp="137.06">One minute I say to myself, stick it out. Make it work. Don't be a quitter. And I feel guilty. It's not in my power to change you. That much I've learned. So I'm giving some serious thought to the only alternative left that I can think of-- a trial separation. If you have another suggestion, I'd like to hear it."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:42.32" data-timestamp="162.32"> Over and over in this book, Chesanow and his co-author, Gareth Esersky, take some of the most painful situations that people can have with each other and give surprisingly graceful one-minute speeches that a person could say or write or show to somebody else. There are a dozen about problems that people might have together in bed. 10 are about dealing with each other as friends. 10 are about the fights that couples have over money. One of the entries even ends with the sentence, "Let's become husband and wife." All of these are written for women because, Chesanow says, men don't buy these books.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:03:13.58" data-timestamp="193.58"> We specifically interviewed women to come up with the most difficult kinds of subjects to discuss.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:22.46" data-timestamp="202.46"> Let me ask you to read another one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:03:24.08" data-timestamp="204.08"> I'd be happy to.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:25.56" data-timestamp="205.56"> Let me ask you to read on page 105.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:03:29.34" data-timestamp="209.34"> This one is titled, "Let's Get Religion." "There's something missing in our lives. We're so focused on relating to each other that we've overlooked an important aspect of relating to our world. I'm talking about religion, spirituality, God. A belief in God and a spiritual life can add--"</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:47.51" data-timestamp="227.51"> When you read one of these after another, the book as a whole seems to have this almost touching faith in the idea that getting the words right might actually solve something or help something. But of course, if you want religion in your life and your partner doesn't, or if you think you've fallen out of love, or if you don't want a baby and your spouse does, I've got to say, your main problem is not what words to use. Your main problem is the situation itself. Neil Chesanow says that plenty of times he heard from women who used the words in the book and it didn't fix the problem.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil Chesanow</h4><p begin="00:04:14.69" data-timestamp="254.69"> So there may not be magic words. There aren't always magic words, but sometimes that in itself becomes something vital for a woman to understand. Now she can tell herself, well, I really have given it my best shot. This message in <i>Please</i> <i>Read</i> <i>This</i> <i>for</i> <i>Me</i> really sums up my feelings. He's still not willing to respond. I think we're reaching one of those landmark moments where we have to decide whether we want to go on or not.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:48.47" data-timestamp="288.47"> Which brings us to today's radio program. Today, during this week in which talking is failing on an international level, from Washington and Moscow to Paris and Baghdad, we bring you stories that ask the question, what is talking good for, anyway? When does it work? When doesn't it work? From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Our program today in four acts.</p><p begin="00:05:12.45" data-timestamp="312.45">Act One, How to Write a Note. In that act, a guy who thinks that words and reason might help his friend from trying to hurt himself. Act Two, The Battle of Words Versus Fear. In that act, one man decides to conquer his fears by listing them, all of them, one after another, all 183 of them.</p><p begin="00:05:31.41" data-timestamp="331.41">Act Three, When a City Opens Its Big Mouth. The story of just how easy-- how astonishingly easy-- it is to get people of all ages and races and economic levels to open up and chat. You just need three little words. Act Four, Wedding Bells and Telephone Bells. In that act, Jonathan Goldstein and Liz Gilbert bring us stories of words failing-- in one case, in the worst wedding toast that any of us has ever heard of occurring at a real wedding. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: How To Write A Note</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:09.35" data-timestamp="369.35"> Act One, How to Write a Note.</p><p begin="00:06:12.32" data-timestamp="372.32">This first story on our show today is unusual. It wasn't originally made to be broadcast on any radio show. It was a tape made by somebody who had never put anything together for radio. He made it to give to a friend. Here's what happened.</p><p begin="00:06:25.22" data-timestamp="385.22">Back in July of 1999, Jake Warga heard that somebody who he was close to in college, his friend Brian, tried to kill himself. Jake went out to visit Brian and he took with him this little MiniDisc recorder that he had just bought for himself that had this little clip-on microphone. During his visit with Brian, they recorded this long conversation they had. And then when Jake got home, he decided to edit this down and give it to Brian as a present.</p><p begin="00:06:48.83" data-timestamp="408.83">He had no idea how to actually edit sound or do anything like that, so at some point, he jumped onto the internet and found a website-- a website, actually, that we've mentioned on our show in the past, called transom.org that teaches beginners how to edit and mix audio with links to free editing software. And Jake put together this story you're about to hear. And he sent it to Brian. His hope was that if Brian heard this tape, heard himself talking, heard his own words, it might convince him that he shouldn't try to kill himself again. That didn't work. Here's the tape that Jake put together.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jake Warga</h4><p begin="00:07:21.65" data-timestamp="441.65"> Last year, my friend Brian tried to commit suicide. He had checked himself into a new hotel that runs alongside the interstate, which happens to pass through the small college town in which he lives. And without ceremony or note-writing, he took a combination of drugs he thought sufficient enough to quietly end his life.</p><p begin="00:07:58.13" data-timestamp="478.13">As people often do, Brian and I started getting lazy about communicating after I left college and moved out of that same college town. Emails became rare, and phone calls rarer. After the longest period yet of not hearing from Brian, I got a call from an old mutual friend of ours asking if I had seen or heard from him. I said I had not.</p><p begin="00:08:26.15" data-timestamp="506.15">We made calls and eventually found him safe in the hospital. A few weeks later, I arranged a visit to see how my old school-- and Brian-- had changed in the years I had been away.

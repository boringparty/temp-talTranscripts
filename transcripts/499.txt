  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.68" data-timestamp="0.68">It's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. One of our producers, Nancy Updike, remembers the first time that she met Kirk Johnson.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:00:08.00" data-timestamp="8">It was 2007. And he told me about a crazy plan that he was considering.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:15.22" data-timestamp="15.22">What was the plan?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:00:16.65" data-timestamp="16.65">The plan involved Iraqi refugees. At the time, it was a brand new, very raw issue. These were Iraqis who had worked for the US during the Iraq War, interpreting or doing other jobs.</p><p begin="00:00:29.20" data-timestamp="29.2">And as the war got worse, they were being targeted for having worked with the US. People were being killed. They were being tortured, kidnapped. A lot went into hiding or went on the run.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:40.81" data-timestamp="40.81">And so what was he thinking about doing to deal with this?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:00:44.01" data-timestamp="44.01">So the plan he was mulling was, OK, he was going to gather a small group of these Iraqis who'd worked with the US-- around 50-- and somehow get them to Jamaica, somewhere in the Caribbean, at which point he would charter a high-speed boat and race them over to the United States, hoping to outrun the Coast Guard so that he could get them onto American soil where they could claim asylum.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:09.21" data-timestamp="69.21">Wow.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:01:10.45" data-timestamp="70.45">That's what I said. That's exactly what I said. Wow.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:21.18" data-timestamp="81.18">So who is Kirk Johnson? Well, he had worked in Iraq with USAID, the agency that gives out America's foreign aid. He spoke Arabic. And by 2007, when Nancy met him, he was still just this guy in his mid-20s, but he had kind of become the public face of this issue-- of Iraqis who had worked with the US forces and were now in danger because of it and had to get out of the country.</p><p begin="00:01:41.72" data-timestamp="101.72">He was writing about this. He was appearing on TV. He was talking to people in Congress. He set up a nonprofit organization to help these Iraqis.</p><p begin="00:01:49.09" data-timestamp="109.09">And Nancy says that when he ran this proposal by her in 2007, she thought to herself, OK, well, here is just somebody who is up against a problem that is so big and intractable, I guess he's mulling over some crazy schemes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:02:01.52" data-timestamp="121.52">But that's not even what was going on. I talked to him a few weeks ago and found out that that wasn't even his idea. He didn't come up with that.</p><p begin="00:02:10.19" data-timestamp="130.19">He told me that this boat chartering, outrunning the Coast Guard scheme had actually been suggested to him by someone pretty high up in the government's refugee resettlement bureaucracy.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:21.07" data-timestamp="141.07">Wow.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:02:22.25" data-timestamp="142.25">Right. Another wow. Kirk had met with him to ask for help-- regular, above board, inside the bounds of the law help. And the man had told him, in all seriousness, I can't help you from my lofty position. But I've done some research into maritime law. And here's what I think you should do.</p><p begin="00:02:47.82" data-timestamp="167.82">And in the years since then, it's been so hard to get these Iraqis who worked with the US to safety that Kirk has thought back on that conversation many, many times, he says. This is Kirk talking about this.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:03:01.82" data-timestamp="181.82">And as depressing as it was that this was his recommended strategy, I actually still think he's right. And I sometimes wonder, if I had done the boat thing, maybe this issue would have been solved a long time ago.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:18.72" data-timestamp="198.72">Wait. Nancy, didn't we solve this problem? Am I remembering wrong? Didn't they pass a law or something that was going to allow exactly these people into the country, people who had worked with US forces in Iraq and their lives were in danger?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:03:33.18" data-timestamp="213.18">Yeah, more than one law. And I think a lot of people, when they hear about this, have the same reaction. Didn't we fix this? And this issue-- what to do with these Iraqis-- is this zombie problem. It has seemingly been dealt with over and over only to rise again and keep staggering forward.</p><p begin="00:03:57.21" data-timestamp="237.21">We've passed laws starting in 2008. It's not a partisan issue. Republicans and Democrats came together to create a special track for these Iraqis in the visa process to speed them through. But the problem is still not fixed. There are still thousands of Iraqis who worked with the US and have applied to come here out of fear for their lives and their families' lives, and they're still just waiting, often for years.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:30.32" data-timestamp="270.32">Well, today on our radio program, yes, for once, a national problem where we all agree that we should fix it and how to fix it. And yet, somehow we have not gotten the job done. Kirk Johnson gives us the incredible behind the scenes stories of what has happened to fix this problem, what has not happened, and why. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Reluctant Sailor</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:53.55" data-timestamp="293.55"> Kirk himself, I should say, had no special interest originally in refugees or anything like that. He sometimes says that he stumbled into this issue. Nancy Updike tells what happened.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:05:06.99" data-timestamp="306.99">Kirk's story starts in kind of a weird place-- on a family vacation in the Dominican Republic, his first vacation after his first year in Iraq. And on his first night there, he sleepwalked up to the open window in his hotel room, woke up, and thought he was still in Fallujah.</p><p begin="00:05:23.89" data-timestamp="323.89">He shouted and ducked. He says there had been a sniper or snipers in Fallujah around that time who killed more than a dozen Marines. And he thought for a second that he was standing in front of an open window in Fallujah with no body armor.</p><p begin="00:05:37.10" data-timestamp="337.1">This was early in the war. And it didn't occur to him that he might have PTSD. He thought it was just some bad, weird dream. The next night, he sleepwalked again, right out the second floor window of the hotel, about a 17-foot fall to concrete. He broke both his wrists, his upper jaw, his nose, cracked his skull, needed about 70 stitches in his face.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:06:00.54" data-timestamp="360.54">The good part is that I-- most of doctors that I've had, when they take in the extent of the injuries, have told me that there's no reason I should be walking.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:06:12.97" data-timestamp="372.97">Is the explanation for you not being more injured that you were asleep and your body was just limp? Or is it just dumb luck--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:06:21.20" data-timestamp="381.2">Dumb luck.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:06:21.64" data-timestamp="381.64">--and youth?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:06:22.83" data-timestamp="382.83">Yeah. I just think maybe-- I started taking Centrum multivitamins about a year before the accident. And maybe my bones were sufficiently strong. But I just know I lucked out.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:06:38.05" data-timestamp="398.05">You were spared to be in a Centrum commercial later in life?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:06:40.68" data-timestamp="400.68">(LAUGHING) Yeah, right.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:06:47.51" data-timestamp="407.51">Kirk's plan was eight weeks' recovery and then right back to Iraq. Instead, he spent the next several months in his hometown in Illinois, getting a series of painful surgeries, fighting with his health insurance company, struggling to do basic things like eat or hold a pen, and running out of money.</p><p begin="00:07:05.24" data-timestamp="425.24">Kirk's an ambitious guy. His work in Iraq was something he'd been building toward this whole life. He started learning Arabic at 16 years old, got a Fulbright to Cairo at 21, and at 24, was hired by USAID to go to Iraq. All he wanted was to go back, and he couldn't. He had to restart his adult life from scratch.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kirk Johnson</h4><p begin="00:07:25.22" data-timestamp="445.22">I started studying for the law school admissions test. And I really did feel at that point that I was moving on. I was having the nightmares less. I was thinking about Iraq less. I stopped reading the news from Iraq.</p><p begin="00:07:46.01" data-timestamp="466.01">But one day, one of my good Iraqi friends sent an email-- he was one of the highest ranked Iraqis that worked for USAID. I think it was a $130 million program that he was working on.

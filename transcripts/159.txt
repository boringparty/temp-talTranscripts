  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.20" data-timestamp="0.2"> It's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, I'm Ira Glass. Should she go in? What is there even to say about her daughter's life? Here's Tillie Olsen, reading.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tillie Olsen</h4><p begin="00:00:25.30" data-timestamp="25.3"> Even if I came, what good would it do? You think because I'm her mother, I have a key? Or that, in some way, you could use me as the key? She's lived for 19 years. There's all that life that has happened outside of me, beyond me.</p><p begin="00:00:49.33" data-timestamp="49.33">And when is there time to remember, to sift, to weigh, to estimate, to total? I'll start, and there will be an interruption, and I'll have to gather it all together again. Or I'll become engulfed with all I did or did not do, with what should have been, and what cannot be helped.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:15.73" data-timestamp="75.73"> And then, as she stands there ironing, she reviews her daughter's life, point by point, from the baby she was through all sorts of troubles and mistakes. A working mother trying to be there for her children, trying and learning as she goes, making it up as she goes. I'll never come in to say she was a child seldom smiled at. Her father left me before she was a year old. I had to work her first six years when there was work, or I sent her home and to his relatives. She was dark, and thin, and foreign-looking, in a world where the prestige went to blondness, and curly hair, and dimples. She was slow where glibness was prized.</p><p begin="00:02:16.56" data-timestamp="136.56">She was a child of anxious, not proud love. We were poor and could not afford for her the soil of easy growth. I was a young mother. I was a distracted mother. There were the other children pushing up, demanding. Her younger sisters seemed to be all that she was not. There were years she did not let me touch her.</p><p begin="00:02:49.33" data-timestamp="169.33">She kept too much in herself. Her life was such that she had to keep too much in herself. My wisdom came too late. She is a child of her age, of depression, of war, of fear. Let her be. So, all that is in her will not bloom, but in how many does it? There is still enough left to live by. Only help her to know, help make it so there is cause for her to know that she is more than this dress on the ironing board, helpless before the iron.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:40.70" data-timestamp="220.7"> Being a parent is an improvisation and things go wrong. She is a psychologist in Baltimore.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:03.25" data-timestamp="243.25"> Mom, I was wondering if you've noticed that every year since we started <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> four and a half years ago we've done a Father's Day show, but we've never done a Mother's Day show.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:13.86" data-timestamp="253.86"> Actually, I never noticed it until you pointed it out to me.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:18.64" data-timestamp="258.64"> OK.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:19.79" data-timestamp="259.79"> I guess I've become accustomed to ignoring those kind of slights.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:24.32" data-timestamp="264.32"> [LAUGHING] Oh, really?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:32.32" data-timestamp="272.32"> Well, I have a generic answer for that.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:34.89" data-timestamp="274.89">You do?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:35.31" data-timestamp="275.31"> Yeah, why you've done Father's Day shows and you haven't done a Mother's Day show is because mothers are taken for granted.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:41.45" data-timestamp="281.45"> Like, how do you mean?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:42.57" data-timestamp="282.57"> Well, for example, I knew somebody who told me that the summer that he was at camp and his mother wrote to him every single day. And he didn't keep any of the letters that his mother sent.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:07.13" data-timestamp="307.13"> Well, is that because it was so surprising that his father would be emotionally expressive? Whereas his mother was always emotionally expressive?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:14.26" data-timestamp="314.26"> Exactly. The child is so secure with the mother that he doesn't need to hold onto a souvenir or a symbol that she cares about him.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:27.37" data-timestamp="327.37"> Hm.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shirley Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:28.68" data-timestamp="328.68"> That's what I just said, that we take the mothers for granted.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:35.31" data-timestamp="335.31"> Well, obviously, some mothers believe that mothers are taken for granted. Act One, &quot;She Said, She Said,&quot; in which Alex Blumberg conducts an investigation, perhaps the first ever, with people who compulsively imitate their mother's voices in everyday conversation, well into adulthood. <div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: She Said, She Said</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:42.88" data-timestamp="402.88"> Act One, &quot;She Said, She Said.&quot; There are, as far as we can tell, no scientific studies proving what we're about to assert to you. Our evidence is anecdotal and preliminary. We invite the scientific community to pick up the thread where we leave it. Our findings, thus far, are these. We have discovered that there is a vast subset of American families that crosses all social classes in which the children do imitations of their mothers all the time, even into adulthood. What does it mean? Alex Blumberg tells all.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:07:17.85" data-timestamp="437.85"> In my un-scientific survey I can tell you this, it's always people who have close relationships with their mothers, whose mothers are still powerful forces for them, who end up imitating them. What's always striking is how vivid these imitations are.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gionofer</h4><p begin="00:07:30.61" data-timestamp="450.61"> My name is Gianofer. I'm from Milwaukee. And the typical conversation with my mother, or my favorite thing to imitate her, is in the morning, when we were children, she'd come in the room to wake us up. And there were three girls in the room. And so she would come in every morning and she would first wake up my sister, Sharon. And Sharon was the hardest one to wake up.</p><p begin="00:07:47.78" data-timestamp="467.78">And so, every morning, she would come in the room and she'd go, &quot;Sharon. Sharon? Sharon? Sharon. Sharon. Sharon! Sharon, I know you hear me talking to you, Sharon. Sharon, get up. Sharon, I'm going to wake up the rest of the babies trying to wake you. Sharon, get up. Lord, I don't know how you don't want to get-- Sharon. I know you-- Sharon? I wish you would get out of that bed! Girl, get up!&quot; And that was every morning. And it would always start off very sweet. &quot;Sharon? Sharon. Sharon. Sharon.&quot; And it's not Sha-ron, it's, Sharn. Sharn Sharn.</p><p begin="00:08:26.05" data-timestamp="506.05">She talked all the time. If you would say that, you know, &quot;I'm studying now.&quot; &quot;Oh, you studying? What you studying? Let me look at that book. And we didn't have books. See, we didn't have books like this when I was in school, Jonna. We had a little, old slate. And you'd put your name on there with some chalk. And then, when you were done, you'd just wipe it right off.

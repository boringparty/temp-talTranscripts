  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.85" data-timestamp="1.85">What will make you change? For most of us, it is not self-help books, courses at the learning annex. We are immune to any number of long, sincere talks with our friends about the problems that do not go away. We are immune to resolutions made at the last minute of the last hour of each December 31.</p><p begin="00:00:26.28" data-timestamp="26.28">Consider the story of Evan Harris. She didn't like her job. She wanted to leave her boyfriend. She was living in a city she didn't like.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:00:34.34" data-timestamp="34.34">I was well-entrenched in my life and pretty much sleepwalking.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:40.83" data-timestamp="40.83">And weeks went by. And months went by. And then one day, she and a coworker were in the mail room at the extremely low-level office jobs that they held, and there amongst the envelopes, stacks of envelopes everywhere, they started talking. And that's where it happened to Evan Harris.</p><p begin="00:01:07.20" data-timestamp="67.2">You know how sometimes the smallest thing can end up changing your entire life? A casual comment by somebody that you barely know, an ad you happen to spot in the newspaper, a chance encounter, one little moment. And later when you look back, you realize, oh, that was it. That was the moment. That was the pivot point around which my entire life turned and spun and went into a totally different direction. And you know you don't get to choose. You do not get to choose this pivotal moment. And sometimes, as anybody over a certain age can tell you, the pivotal moment can be kind of stupid.</p><p begin="00:01:57.81" data-timestamp="117.81">In Evan Harris's case, it was about as unlikely as they come.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:02:02.55" data-timestamp="122.55">We were doing some sort of menial labor. She was doing a mailing, and I was putting some stuff in alphabetical order. And I said to her, &quot;Shouldn't the letter Q be further toward the back of the alphabet?&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:17.44" data-timestamp="137.44">That's it. With the utterance of those words-- let me just play this back for you. Because these are words which changed an adult human life. Here we go.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:02:29.50" data-timestamp="149.5">&quot;Shouldn't the letter Q be further toward the back of the alphabet?&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:33.85" data-timestamp="153.85">With those words, Evan Harris's life took a 180 degree turn.</p><p begin="00:02:49.16" data-timestamp="169.16">Well, from WBEZ Chicago. This is <i>Your</i> <i>Radio</i> <i>Playhouse.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Today on our program, Quitting, stories of people who quit everything in their lives that they hated and what happened to them afterwards.</p><p begin="00:03:05.65" data-timestamp="185.65">Coming up, writer Sandra Tsing Loh, Lisa Buscani, and others. Stay with us.</p><p begin="00:03:22.12" data-timestamp="202.12">So Evan Harris and her friend and coworker, Shelley Ross, were at this job and they started talking about the letter Q. And it was the kind of conversation you can only really have at a job that does not require total concentration. So you're sorting mail, you're frying burgers, you're doing whatever. And so they're talking about the letter Q.</p><p begin="00:03:44.64" data-timestamp="224.64">And again, remember, this is the pivotal conversation in a person's life. They're talking about the letter Q and they get to talking about where it should be in the alphabet. And in their minds, Q does not belong in the middle of the alphabet where it is, with the hoi polloi of the alphabet. Your Ms, your Ns, your Ps. Letters that will just join any word for the asking. N is in letters-- you don't even hear it. Damn. What's it doing in there?</p><p begin="00:04:16.02" data-timestamp="256.02">Q, in their mind, has more character. Q, in their mind, doesn't take anything off of anybody. It makes demands and it sees that they are met. What other letter has an adjunct, a bodyguard with it, carried into every word? In their mind, Q belongs at the end of the alphabet, with your Xs, your Ys, your Zs. Letters that do not care what anybody thinks of them and that don't just join up to any word.</p><p begin="00:04:44.94" data-timestamp="284.94">And Q, of course, is the first letter of the word &quot;quit.&quot; You can't spell &quot;quit&quot; without Q, at least on public radio you can't. And Evan Harris and her friend Shelley Ross started talking about what it means to be able to quit something. What are the qualities that you need? And they decided the qualities were that kind of fierce independence that, in fact, they saw in the letter Q. And they talked about quitting for a long time that day.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:05:13.68" data-timestamp="313.68">And <i>Quitter</i> <i>Quarterly</i> just came out of my mouth, really. I mean it just came out of my mouth. It kind of came from heaven. It kind of came from heaven. Really. It just kind of lit down on me. And there it was, and that's how I saw it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:33.87" data-timestamp="333.87"> <i>Quitter Quarterly.</i> At that moment, Evan Harris and her friend Shelley Ross decided to publish a little zine, a little Xerox thing, just a couple pages long, called <i>Quitter</i> <i>Quarterly.</i> And that changed Evan's life.</p><p begin="00:05:48.96" data-timestamp="348.96">Recently I heard about the butter sculptures at the Minnesota State Fair. These are these big sculptures made entirely out of butter. The way it was explained to me was that they are often of the queen for the day. That some teenage girl will be selected as the fair queen that day, and then her bust will be done in butter.</p><p begin="00:06:17.09" data-timestamp="377.09">And it got me to thinking, what if my muse had called me to work in butter? What if my muse said, you will now represent the human form in butter? What if your muse calls you to do something hokey and out-of-date, like singing in coffeehouses or I don't even know. The point is we cannot choose who we fall in love with. We cannot choose what we are interested to write about. We cannot choose our muse.</p><p begin="00:06:50.20" data-timestamp="410.2">And quitting was the muse that called Evan Harris. She had written other things before <i>Quitter</i> <i>Quarterly,</i> but back then the ideas came out in a trickle. When she wrote about quitting, it was like this inexhaustible fountain. It was standing in front of a fire hydrant, immense force, full blast coming at her.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:07:10.25" data-timestamp="430.25">It really came all at once. I mean, I really sat down to start writing about it, and it really came out. And it seemed to have already been formed.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:20.85" data-timestamp="440.85">And the way that Evan Harris and Shelley Ross write about quitting is so evocative and funny that after just two issues of this tiny little zine, <i>Quitter</i> <i>Quarterly</i> was excerpted in <i>Harper's</i> <i>Magazine</i> and it was named Zine of the Month by <i>Sassy</i> <i>Magazine</i>, which covers your sort of highbrow to lowbrow scale. Soon a publisher had Evan writing a book which is going to be out this coming May called <i>The</i> <i>Quit</i>.</p><p begin="00:07:49.94" data-timestamp="469.94">And all of this was happening because Evan Harris and Shelley Ross were not just writing about quitting. They were inventing an entire philosophy of quitting, a theory of quitting. <i>Quitter Quarterly</i> is like a scientific inquiry into the properties of quitting. For example, the euphoria of the quit.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:08:09.67" data-timestamp="489.67">The euphoria of the quit. Quitting euphoria is an incredible thing. I don't know how much you have quit, but there is an incredible charge to quitting that is like a drug. It's like being in love kind of, except it's being in love with your decision.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:08:30.96" data-timestamp="510.96">Evan Harris was writing about quitting, and thinking about quitting, and gathering the strength to quit her job and her boyfriend and her city, which, by the way, she did. And at some point in this entire process, her view of the world began to change. Quits and potential quits seemed to be everywhere. She would go out with a friend for coffee and, of course, talk about all this quitting business that she was thinking about all the time. And then this friend, no matter who it was, would come out with some story about something that he or she wanted to quit. Quitting started to seem like the engine that made everything in the world go round.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:09:11.18" data-timestamp="551.18">The way I see it in thinking about my own life, I see it as being framed and the narrative of it being pushed along by quitting, by quits. So that my first big quit was to leave the college that I was going to, and I moved to New York City. And then my next big quit was to quit the graduate program that I was in, and then I fell into this job. And so now I have nothing left to quit. I mean I've quit my boyfriend. I've quit my city. I've quit my job. And I have nothing left to quit. And so really now there's nothing to do but build things up again. Do you see what I mean?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:09:51.64" data-timestamp="591.64">Yeah, but you say that as if you're just building them up for what will be the central fact of your life, which is then you're going to quit them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Harris</h4><p begin="00:09:58.70" data-timestamp="598.7">That's right. The way I see it is that there's sort of like two basic, big, important concepts. OK? And the first one is that nothing lasts forever. There is nothing that you can do that you can keep on doing forever. Nothing. That's it. Period. End of story. And then the other big one is that you have to be doing something. So my feeling is it's better to willfully quit whatever it is and then go on to the next thing that you have to be doing, because you have to be doing something, than to just kind of let things fall apart.</p><p begin="00:10:41.70" data-timestamp="641.7">The quitting cycle. In everybody's life, there is something of a quitting cycle. So you can talk about adolescence being a time where people quit listening to their parents. <div class="act" id="act3">
  <h3>Act Three: Act Three</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Lisa Buscani</h4><p begin="00:41:29.24" data-timestamp="2489.24">Part one, Work.</p><p begin="00:41:32.41" data-timestamp="2492.41">The morning began with weight. My head sown down to an unbearably heavy burden, pinned to the narcotic pillow. My eyes like their lids shut. Blindness would hinder my motion. My arms and legs remembered the heavy metal burned into their sense memory and refused me the grace I needed to get through the day. My body's refusal to function was in the name of survival. If I moved, that meant I would get up. If I got up, I would probably take a shower and get dressed. If I did that, I would go to work, to unspeakable, unthinkable horror.</p><p begin="00:42:13.18" data-timestamp="2533.18">Now, don't get me wrong. I have nothing against work per se. The concept of joining shoulder-to-shoulder with efficient, enthusiastic coworkers, discovering problems, crafting solutions, pushing onward, always onward via honest sweat of brow is attractive to me. I think of well-constructed women in business suits, the ones who really know how to tie a scarf, dashing through airports to catch that last flight to Rome, manhandling a potential empire crashing crisis, and spending an exhausted yet satisfied overnight in the company pensione.</p><p begin="00:42:50.42" data-timestamp="2570.42">I think of post office murals and 1950's communist propaganda. Strong, textured, geometric men in the stoic blue of the overall and the work shirt, reverently folding open the land, courageously braving the hell of the forge, lifting their ruddy cheeks and angular chins to God or government, simply, deeply, thankful for their job. That was work to me, not the salaried conflagration I went to every day.</p><p begin="00:43:21.37" data-timestamp="2601.37">I worked at a small trade journal in the suburbs where I handled various layout and writing tasks. It was a tiny firm, a mom and pop organization, if you will, starring Leona Helmsley as mom. The publisher was a shrunken, shrewish woman in her 50's, the kind of woman who thought the word &quot;broad&quot; was a badge of honor. She fancied herself the last of the old Chicago newsies, barking her wisdom from her stuffy, windowless office, her cover sticks sweating off the gin blossoms in her nose, one of an endless supply of beige stained Virginia Slims dangling from her lipless mouth.</p><p begin="00:43:58.44" data-timestamp="2638.44">Her motto was this-- yell, yell, and yell some more. If they're there the next morning, you've lost your touch. It was as if Satan had made a foray into human resources. She stood over my shoulder constantly, criticizing my work with raptorial fervor, waiting like a blood-mouthed hyena for the moment when I tripped up and made an error. And when I did screw up, as employees inevitably do when told they can do nothing else, she was all over me like drone bees to the queen. I was not capable of being right. The presumption that I could be right, that I could even have an opinion, infuriated her. Any argument I sputtered in my defense was dissected, mocked, and negated. After all, how can one question the wisdom of a woman who cornered the floral industry tabloid market? It was better to tip my neck open and let her drink.</p><p begin="00:44:58.86" data-timestamp="2698.86">As horrible as it was, I couldn't just leave. She paid me $12,000 a year. Even with a second job on weekends, I barely got by. I rarely went out anywhere. I felt like restaurants were meant for other people. The world seemed to narrow to the places where I could and could not go. My friends nicknamed me Florence Nightingale. No one could nurse a drink like I could. I bought the cheapest food possible, the cheapest clothes available, and forgot about cutting my hair. My thought process was consistently reduced to the bottom line. Basically, the job taught me one thing-- how to live a life with little or no option. And after a while, I was incapable of imagining myself in any other circumstance. Forget about my college degree and all the experience I obtained. This was how it was and always would be. If you thought about it too much, it became hard to breathe.</p><p begin="00:46:03.19" data-timestamp="2763.19">The breaking came when review time rolled around, two months later than she said it would when she hired me. She told me she wasn't going to give me a raise. Raises were given on merit in her office, she said. My work did not merit a raise. The room was suddenly very literally light. It was almost too bright in the room to look at her without wincing. I could hear the hum of the office lamps. I was amazed at my calm. I stood, walked to my desk, took my office keys off the chain, and walked out the door.</p><p begin="00:46:45.64" data-timestamp="2805.64">I did not know what I was going to do and, blessedly, I did not care. I sat quietly on the train back to the city, feeling my back muscles slowly unravel and my stomach lift six inches into my heart. The warmth started in my cheeks and spread down to my fingers, down to my feet. My lungs expanded completely, which hurt a bit. It felt like I had been swimming all day and had forgotten to breathe. I didn't mind the pain. Peace is a fine travelling companion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:47:30.39" data-timestamp="2850.39">Lisa Buscani. More from her in a minute. But first this song from Robert Metrick, first performed in Chicago's Club Lower Lengths. You're listening to <i>Your</i> <i>Radio</i> <i>Playhouse.</i></p><p begin="00:47:41.89" data-timestamp="2861.89">[MUSIC - &quot;THE CALENDAR SONG&quot; BY ROBERT METRICK]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lisa Buscani</h4><p begin="00:50:39.10" data-timestamp="3039.1">Part two, Love.</p><p begin="00:50:43.96" data-timestamp="3043.96">When we started, he and I, I was thin. My face was a diamond, all clean walls. Nothing held me back. Not my clothes, not my lack of money. I was weightless but I had control, like astronauts after they learn to swim properly in space.</p><p begin="00:51:03.87" data-timestamp="3063.87">Our conversations used to overlap and dovetail and interweave, and occasionally we'd stop to breathe. Great pictures of limitless futures, expensive and unfettered. That's how we talked it. I tried to break my visions down to see how to make them happen and he just made his bigger, which is one way of doing it, I guess.</p><p begin="00:51:26.58" data-timestamp="3086.58">When we made love, we knew how to forget ourselves. That, I'm convinced, is passion. To kiss him so hard and hold him so hard that the act itself is forgotten. And all that is remembered is skin and hair and warmth. That's a gift. That's something we kept for a long, long time.</p><p begin="00:51:52.63" data-timestamp="3112.63">The decline was relatively slow. Every time we fought, I took the opportunity to shift the relationship from one shoulder to another just to give myself a break and better balance. And we'd start again. I had carried us a long time. He could say that, too, I suppose.</p><p begin="00:52:16.84" data-timestamp="3136.84">I started gaining weight, and that seemed to be the theme of it. It became harder and harder to get down to the bone. The way we were put together began to escape me. When we made love, we suddenly knew exactly where we were and what was going on. It was one of those great sadnesses that you carry in your throat.</p><p begin="00:52:42.35" data-timestamp="3162.35">I couldn't quit it because this was one so easy. On the morning of the first night he stayed with me, I woke up and looked at him and found home. I could fold myself into him, into the warmth found in those deco corners.</p><p begin="00:52:58.96" data-timestamp="3178.96">I couldn't quit it because I had been alone much of my life. To be alone again meant an energy my aging had confiscated. And even though I was still alone with him, a future was the crumb of consolation.</p><p begin="00:53:15.18" data-timestamp="3195.18">I couldn't quit it because it didn't fit the only picture I had. My parents knew the necessary work of love. They were the model here. If they could work dogged for their heart, so could I, I thought.</p><p begin="00:53:29.36" data-timestamp="3209.36">I couldn't quit it because of winter. I can't stand to sleep alone in the cold.</p><p begin="00:53:37.39" data-timestamp="3217.39">The end happened several times, actually. We would inevitably hook up again and mess around. We just didn't look in each other's eyes is all. One night we were eating dinner and his smile was less than honest. And I thought, well, then this is it. I said, I've been working far too hard for far too long. And he agreed, and I wanted him to fight it, and was disappointed by how light he was. Smiling, so ready to fly. I felt light, too, but it was the way you are before you catch a cold, all unfocused and buzzing. And the chill comes from inside.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:29.17" data-timestamp="3269.17">Lisa Buscani.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act4">
  <h3>Act Four: Act Four</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:31.55" data-timestamp="3271.55">Dwight Okita has this poem about quitting from his book <i>Crossing</i> <i>with</i> <i>the</i> <i>Light</i>. He wrote it back when he worked as a dance instructor. It's about a student he became friends with.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dwight Okita</h4><p begin="00:54:43.67" data-timestamp="3283.67">Somewhere in Chicago, a woman unplugs a toaster from a wall, and suddenly her apartment is empty. She wraps the cord jump rope style into a bow, lowers the appliance into a box marked &quot;Kitchen Things,&quot; and tapes it shut. All day, boxes move past her, a brown blur against the white walls. How many men does it take to lift a woman's spirits?</p><p begin="00:55:12.19" data-timestamp="3312.19">&quot;Make your arms like a barrel,&quot; I scolded, your dance instructor, fox-trotting you around the room. &quot;Women are always walking backwards, aren't they?&quot; you said, looking at your feet. And I spun you. &quot;No, just going in circles.&quot; And here we laughed. In the mirrors of the dance studio, we laughed. And I saw us, lost in the fun house again. I want it always to be fun.</p><p begin="00:55:38.13" data-timestamp="3338.13">Now everything is loaded on the truck. She sits behind the wheel of something larger than her. &quot;I'm going to Timbuktu and I'm taking my time,&quot; she says her hands on the wheel. &quot;Peaches, pears, apples, plums. Tell me when your birthday comes.&quot; And I wave to her from the curb.</p><p begin="00:55:58.12" data-timestamp="3358.12">It paints a sad picture, this woman in a van pulling out of a driveway no longer hers. And for her, I do a farewell samba on the lawn, alone, taking the darkness in the crescent of my arms, leading it in a dance I'm just beginning to learn.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:56:18.68" data-timestamp="3378.68">&quot;The Farewell Samba&quot; by Dwight Okita.</p><p begin="00:56:21.64" data-timestamp="3381.64">And we're going to close our broadcast today with a piece by the late Philip Larkin, which seems perfect as you contemplate upcoming quits. Over the course of the next week, we're not quitting anything. I think that's what you've gotten out of this particular program. Anyway, this is called &quot;Poetry of Departures.&quot;</p><p begin="00:56:41.70" data-timestamp="3401.7">Sometimes you hear, fifth-hand/ As epitaph/ <i>He</i> <i>chucked</i> <i>up</i> <i>everything</i> <i>and</i> <i>just</i> <i>cleared</i> <i>off,/</i> And always the voice will sound/ Certain you approve/ This audacious, purifying,/ Elemental move.</p><p begin="00:56:53.48" data-timestamp="3413.48">And they are right, I think./ We all hate home/ And having to be there/ I detect my room,/ It's specially-chosen junk,/ The good books, the good bed,/ And my life, in perfect order/ So to hear it said/</p><p begin="00:57:05.33" data-timestamp="3425.33"><i>He</i> <i>walked</i> <i>out</i> <i>on</i> <i>the</i> <i>whole</i> <i>crowd/</i> Leaves me flushed and stirred,/ Like <i>Then</i> <i>she</i> <i>undid her</i> <i>dress/</i> Or <i>Take</i> <i>that,</i> <i>you</i> <i>bastard;/</i> Surely I can, if he did?/ And that helps me stay/ Sober and industrious./ But I'd go today,/</p><p begin="00:57:21.72" data-timestamp="3441.72">Yes, swagger the nut-strewn roads,/ Crouch in the fo'c'sle/ Stubbly with goodness, if/ It weren't so artificial,/ Such a deliberate step backwards/ To create an object/ Books; china; a life/ Reprehensibly perfect.</p></div>  </div>

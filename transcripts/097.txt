  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.74" data-timestamp="1.74">Not long ago I went to see this movie called <i>Fast,</i> <i>Cheap</i> <i>&amp;</i> <i>Out</i> <i>of</i> <i>Control</i>. Hadn't read any reviews, hadn't seen any ads for it. Didn't know anything about it, really. Except that it was by Errol Morris, a documentary filmmaker whose work I like. And that friends of mine enjoyed it. And it was great. It had funny moments and reflective moments and interesting characters. Totally satisfying, soulful film. And after the movie I'm walking through the theater lobby and I see the poster for the movie, which I somehow missed on my way in.</p><p begin="00:00:30.55" data-timestamp="30.55">It was a bright, unnatural yellow. The main characters from the film are photographed with this fish eye lens so their heads are huge and their bodies are tiny. And there, in big print, the poster says these words, &quot;The bizarre at its best.&quot; And I realized what was going on. Someone, somewhere thought that this film was wacky.</p><p begin="00:00:52.50" data-timestamp="52.5">Or they thought they could sell it as wacky. Which, if you see the film, is astounding. It's a great film but it's not wacky. There's a whole section of the film about death, a meditation on death. It's not wacky.</p><p begin="00:01:09.12" data-timestamp="69.12">Tom Bernard is one of the co-presidents of Sony Picture Classics. He saw what a great film <i>Fast, Cheap &amp; Out of Control</i> was and bought the distribution rights from Errol Morris in a bathroom, at the Sundance film festival. It was, he says, the only quiet place to get anything done without competitors overhearing.</p><p begin="00:01:27.31" data-timestamp="87.31">And he says they market the film as wacky for a simple reason. Wacky sells. Market a film as wacky and people come to see it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Bernard</h4><p begin="00:01:35.00" data-timestamp="95">Well, I mean, to me one of the ultimate wacky sells on a movie was <i>Brazil</i>.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:41.66" data-timestamp="101.66">And what was the sell? I don't even remember that. What was the ad campaign?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Bernard</h4><p begin="00:01:44.87" data-timestamp="104.87">It was just a bizarre campaign with sort of South American lettering style, you know?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:52.38" data-timestamp="112.38">Right.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Bernard</h4><p begin="00:01:53.20" data-timestamp="113.2">It had Brazil and a lot of wacky lines and colors. We had a movie, <i>Crumb</i>, which was a very, very moving film but to talk about R. Crumb, the guy's a wacky guy.</p><p begin="00:02:09.54" data-timestamp="129.54">Even <i>In the</i> <i>Company</i> <i>of</i> <i>Men</i> was a very dark comedy and wacky to some extent. That's a movie we released and I think the campaign is a bit strange, a bit wacky.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:24.38" data-timestamp="144.38">I don't know if you've seen any of these films, but they are-- every single one of them-- they are very dark films. These are bleak films, really. I suggested to Mr. Bernard that wacky seems to miss a lot of what these films are about. He said, no no no no. Look at the four characters in <i>Fast,</i> <i>Cheap</i> <i>&amp;</i> <i>Out</i> <i>of</i> <i>Control</i>. Two of them are scientists. One's an expert on robots. One, the world's leading authority on naked mole rats. There's a lion tamer. There's a guy who carves trees and bushes into the shapes of animals.</p><p begin="00:02:53.49" data-timestamp="173.49">And, said Mr. Bernard, that is, by most people's objective standards, actually kind of wacky.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Bernard</h4><p begin="00:02:59.00" data-timestamp="179">The film is a very strange film. The people are very strange. I think, in word of mouth, people would talk about it and they'd say, look at these crazy guys, let's go see what they're up to.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:07.05" data-timestamp="187.05">You know what I realized, as we talk, is that I guess I just don't think of people as wacky. I guess some people just think of people as wacky and some don't. I mean, when I think of all the people in this Errol Morris film, or I think of R. Crumb, I think of them as being people who are fundamentally very, very serious.</p><p begin="00:03:22.27" data-timestamp="202.27">Do you see them as crazy guys? Or eccentric guys? Or wacky guys?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Bernard</h4><p begin="00:03:26.62" data-timestamp="206.62">I think they're very eccentric and very wacky. And I think that if you know people in your world that you consider genius, and I think these guys are all geniuses at what they do, they sort of have a view that is so focused and so-- would people call Albert Einstein eccentric?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:51.44" data-timestamp="231.44">With all respect to Mr. Bernard, who, as far as I'm concerned is doing God's work, distributing these unusual, interesting, amazing films, with all respect I have a different point of view about this. Wacky to me seems to miss the point of everything interesting. Wacky eradicates empathy and thoughtfulness and feeling. Wacky is what people say when they're too polite to say freak. Wacky is what people say when they don't want to feel anything or think anything. Wacky is what people say when they don't know what else to say.</p><p begin="00:04:19.09" data-timestamp="259.09">When you call somebody wacky you're ignoring who they are and painting a big smiley face on top of their real face. I didn't used to have an opinion about wackiness, one way or another. But after I saw <i>Fast, Cheap &amp; Out of Control</i> I did. And that opinion is death to wacky. Death to wacky.</p><p begin="00:04:42.45" data-timestamp="282.45">And then we started putting this week's program together, this week's radio program. And everything I thought about wacky got thrown up in the air again.</p><p begin="00:04:58.22" data-timestamp="298.22">Well, from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, I'm Ira Glass. Today on our program, 13 ways of looking at wacky. Act One, Negative. Writer David Sedaris faces a sheet of glass, a glass of milk, a floor of carpet tacks, and a photographer with big, big ideas.</p><p begin="00:05:17.03" data-timestamp="317.03">Act Two, The Good, the Bad, and the Wacky. We squint our eyes and try to distinguish good kitsch from bad kitsch, good wacky from bad wacky.</p><p begin="00:05:24.60" data-timestamp="324.6">Act Three, The Politics of Wacky. A conversation with Michael Lewis about republicans, the press, and, of course, wackiness.</p><p begin="00:05:33.45" data-timestamp="333.45">Acts Four through Ten, well, you'll see about those.</p><p begin="00:05:37.50" data-timestamp="337.5">Act Eleven, Self-Proclaimed Wacky. We find someone who is not called wacky by marketers or promoters, she calls herself wacky.</p><p begin="00:05:47.45" data-timestamp="347.45">Act Twelve, Sidestepping Wackiness and Seriousness and Embracing Both.</p><p begin="00:05:54.08" data-timestamp="354.08">Act Thirteen, Dr. Jeckyl and Mr. Wacky. That story from our own Sarah Vowel. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Negative</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:06.91" data-timestamp="366.91">Act one, &quot;Negative.&quot; David Sedaris is a regular contributor here on <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. A <i>Morning</i> <i>Edition</i> commentator. Author of the books <i>Naked</i> and <i>Barrel</i> <i>Fever</i>. His stories are the sort of complicated mix of seriousness and funny that is easy to love but comes nowhere near, I have to say, nowhere near to wacky.</p><p begin="00:06:25.98" data-timestamp="385.98">What follows is a set of true stories plus a fictional story inspired by the true ones.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Sedaris</h4><p begin="00:06:32.66" data-timestamp="392.66">I wore my new tie to this afternoon's photo shoot. It was an extravagant purchase but I justified it thinking of all the money I would have spent on ties if I'd had a real job. I thought the magazine would want a picture of me standing with my book, so I packed a copy along with an extra shirt in case they had something against white. I arrived in the studio where the photographer said, &quot;I'm not loving that tie much at all.&quot; He didn't care for the shirt, either and called out to his assistant, &quot;Brian, hey Brian, do we still have that t-shirt from last week?&quot;</p><p begin="00:07:08.69" data-timestamp="428.69">The t-shirt was produced and I explained that I'm incapable of wearing anything with words on it, especially the words &quot;Deliveries in Rear.&quot; &quot;You are gay, aren't you?&quot; the photographer asked. &quot;It says right here in your press kit. Have I got the wrong person?&quot; When it came to wearing that t-shirt he most definitely had the wrong person.</p><p begin="00:07:29.56" data-timestamp="449.56">&quot;Well, maybe you can turn it inside out,&quot; he said. &quot;We won't be seeing much of it anyway since I'd like to get you crouched down on your hands and knees. Seeing as you're an author and all, I thought it might be fun if we shot you cleaning the bathroom floor, the black and white tiles are going to look great. So what do you say you change out of that shirt and we get started?&quot;</p><p begin="00:07:51.50" data-timestamp="471.5">Then he called for his assistant to fill up a bucket of sudsy water, adding that the bubbles on top should be as fat as possible.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Photographer</h4><p begin="00:08:12.42" data-timestamp="492.42"> As a professional photographer, I have to say that my two greatest assets are my tenacity and my sense of humor.

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:04:14.28" data-timestamp="254.28"> The thing that got me interested in all this was something called a NINA loan. Back when the housing crisis was still a housing bubble, a guy on the phone told me that a NINA loan stands for no income no asset. As in, someone will lend you a bunch of money without first checking to see if you have any income or any assets.</p><p begin="00:04:30.57" data-timestamp="270.57">And it was an official loan product, like you could walk into a mortgage broker's office and they would say, well, we can give you a 30 year fixed rate, or we could put you in a NINA. He said there were lots of loans like this, where the bank didn't actually check your income, which I found confusing.</p><p begin="00:04:44.68" data-timestamp="284.68">And it turns out even the people who got them found them confusing. For example, a guy I met named Clarence Nathan. He worked three part-time, not very steady jobs, and made a total of $45,000 a year, roughly. He got himself into trouble and needed money. So he took out a loan against his house, a big one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:05:00.90" data-timestamp="300.9"> Call it $540,000 for round figures.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:05:03.74" data-timestamp="303.74">You basically borrowed $540,000 from the bank, and they didn't check your income?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:05:08.75" data-timestamp="308.75"> It's a no income verification loan. They don't call me up and say, how much money? They don't do that. I mean it's almost like you pass a guy in the street, and you say, will you lend me $540,000? He says, well what do you do? Hey, I got a job. OK.</p><p begin="00:05:28.57" data-timestamp="328.57">It seems as if it's that casual, even though there are a lot of papers that get filled out and stuff flies all over with the faxes and the emails, and all like that. Essentially, that's the process.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:05:45.88" data-timestamp="345.88">Would you have loaned you the money?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:05:49.56" data-timestamp="349.56"> I wouldn't have loaned me the money. And nobody that I know would have loaned me the money. I mean, I know guys who are criminals that wouldn't lend me that money, and they'd break your kneecaps. So [LAUGHING] yeah, I don't know why the bank did it. I'm serious. $540,000, a person with bad credit.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:06:10.25" data-timestamp="370.25"> As it turns out, Clarence's friends, acquaintances and shadowy criminal contacts would have been right not to lend him the money. By the time I talked to him, Clarence hadn't made a payment in almost a year, and his house was in the process of foreclosure.</p><p begin="00:06:20.63" data-timestamp="380.63">And stories like this have been in the news for months. And they often feature an innocent homeowner who was duped by a lying, greedy mortgage banker. Or if you're more of a <i>Wall</i> <i>Street</i> <i>Journal</i> editorial page type, an innocent mortgage banker who was duped by lying, greedy homeowner. And no doubt, both categories exist. But Clarence's case is more nuanced, and much more common.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:06:39.65" data-timestamp="399.65"> Nobody came and told me a lie, and told me a story, and said, oh, just close your eyes, and all your problems will go away. That wasn't the situation. The situation was that I needed the money. And I'm not trying to absolve myself of anything.</p><p begin="00:06:54.76" data-timestamp="414.76">I had a situation. And I thought that I could do this, and then get out of it within six to nine months. The six to nine month plan didn't work, so I'm stuck. But if somebody had told me, you couldn't borrow the money, I probably would have had to do something else more drastic and dramatic, and not be in this situation now. The bank made an imprudent loan. I made an imprudent loan. So the bank and I are partners in this deal.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:07:25.30" data-timestamp="445.3"> This imprudent partnership is new. And it's at the heart of the current housing crisis. For most of the history of banking, bankers wouldn't have loaned Clarence their money either. They didn't let people like Clarence near their money, in fact, people with part time employment and unpaid debts in their past.</p><p begin="00:07:41.26" data-timestamp="461.26">And then suddenly in the early 2000s, everything changed. There is this question people would ask, will things get like the 1930s or the 1970s?</p><p begin="00:56:34.56" data-timestamp="3394.56">There's a real fear that just like in the '30s, hundreds of banks would collapse. It's barely growing. There aren't a lot of new businesses, new jobs, unemployment keeps creeping up. We're just sort of stuck in neutral for a while. Anyone under, say, 45 probably doesn't remember that 1970s malaise too well. Anyone under 30 has barely known a US economy that wasn't growing.

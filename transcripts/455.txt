And then, last fall, Andreas Georgiou got a phone call from a Greek government official.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andreas Georgiou</h4><p begin="00:45:07.58" data-timestamp="2707.58">The Prosecutor for Economic Crimes.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:45:10.27" data-timestamp="2710.27">The prosecutor, Georgiou says, explained that the office planned to clarify the contested 2009 deficit number and he wanted Georgiou's help. And to my surprise they asked me where my lawyer was. And I asked why I needed a lawyer, and they explained to me that I was being invited as a suspect not as a witness.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:45:51.01" data-timestamp="2751.01">In other words, Andreas Georgiou is being accused of a crime.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andreas Georgiou</h4><p begin="00:45:55.24" data-timestamp="2755.24">Something called breach of faith against the state.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jacob Kirkegaard</h4><p begin="00:46:02.55" data-timestamp="2762.55">To me, well there's no other word for it than insane.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:46:06.55" data-timestamp="2766.55">This again, is Jacob Kirkegaard with the Peterson Institute for International Economics. <div class="act" id="act5">
  <h3>Act Five: What's a Greek Accountant Got to Do With Me?</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:47:25.57" data-timestamp="2845.57">This brings us to Act Five, What's a Greek Accountant Got To Do With Me? The answer would normally be nothing. Greece is a pretty tiny country and every other country in the Eurozone is in much better shape than Greece. But that doesn't matter right now. You're going to be hearing a lot about Greece in the next couple of weeks and months. People will be talking about whether Greece can get some of its debt forgiven, whether Greece will get its next bailout. And the thing you need to keep in mind as you hear all this, what those people are really talking about when they're talking about Greece is Italy.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:47:55.45" data-timestamp="2875.45">It feels like a big leap to go from Greece to Italy.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:47:58.80" data-timestamp="2878.8">Yeah, Chana, it really does. And so let's just talk through this. I mean Greece in in much worse shape than Italy. But Italy is next in line. You remember worries about Greece cast the shadow on Portugal which then cast a shadow on Ireland and then Spain? At the end of last year it finally reached Italy. Suddenly Italy, like Greece and Portugal and Ireland and Spain before it, was having a harder and harder time finding anyone to lend it money at reasonable interest rates.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Female Reporter</h4><p begin="00:48:23.45" data-timestamp="2903.45">Italian bond yields hitting record levels today as investors expressed their concern about how and when the country will address its fiscal constraints.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:48:30.34" data-timestamp="2910.34">And this is the moment where the debt crisis took a really scary turn. Because Italy, Italy is enormous. Italy's the seventh-largest economy in the world.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Male Reporter</h4><p begin="00:48:40.34" data-timestamp="2920.34">Everybody knows Italy is a huge economy. If Italy starts teetering more than it already is, it's going to be an issue.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Male Reporter</h4><p begin="00:48:47.30" data-timestamp="2927.3">The situation in Italy is really now getting serious, and I think we are approaching a kind of systemic crisis right now that reminds very much to the situation in 2008.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:48:58.78" data-timestamp="2938.78">2008, you may remember, that's when the US economy fell apart. And this is why when people say they're worried about Greece, they're actually worried about Italy. Because Italy's destiny is now tied to the destiny of Greece.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:49:12.32" data-timestamp="2952.32">If our last financial crisis taught us anything, it's that relatively small defaults can have very large consequences. The last crisis began, you may remember, with the collapse of a pretty small investment bank, Lehman Brothers. A default in the tiny country of Greece could have similar catastrophic consequences, cause a chain reaction of defaults ending with Italy. Greece, Greece is the cigarette. Italy is the gas station.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:49:35.86" data-timestamp="2975.86">And this is where, if you're in the United States and you didn't already care, you might start to care.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:49:41.61" data-timestamp="2981.61">The United States seems finally to be on a slow, fragile path to recovery. Europe could kill that. It could slow it down. It could stop it. It could make things worse than they were before. Most days the crisis in Europe seems like this slow-moving mess that will eventually work itself out. But every once in a while the question arises, what if it doesn't? Satyajit Das is a risk consultant and former banker. He spent his life in finance, and he's been following the crisis closely. How scared are you?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Satyajit Das</h4><p begin="00:50:13.07" data-timestamp="3013.07">I've been doing this for 34 years in financial markets. This is the first time in my life I actually am really worried. Because when you're talking about countries the size of Italy, this is really truly one of the scariest moments in my professional life.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:50:33.48" data-timestamp="3033.48">Das is scared because in his view, the new plan to save the euro is exactly the opposite of what should be happening. European leaders, he says, largely Germans, are still trying to get countries to obey their rules, those rules they set up back at the very beginning of the euro, to get countries like Greece to cut spending, bring in more in tax revenue, get their budget in line.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:50:55.24" data-timestamp="3055.24">But here is what happens when you cut spending and raise taxes-- the economy generally shrinks, especially in the middle of an economic crisis. In Spain, the unemployment rate has continued to rise. It's now over 20%. That is Great Depression territory. In Ireland and Portugal it's not much better. Most people think that all of Europe is in a recession right now. And the question is only, how bad will it get?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:51:17.57" data-timestamp="3077.57">I have to say, being in Greece, it really does feel like a country on the verge of falling apart. Do you remember Katarina Margaritu, the Greek chemist?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:51:25.68" data-timestamp="3085.68">The one who who went back and remembered, oh that's all those things that we spent money on that we couldn't afford?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:51:29.43" data-timestamp="3089.43">Right, my friend got a job, we got the subway system. So she actually works as a contractor for the Greek government. About a third of the Greek population relies on the government for a paycheck. And right now?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Katarina Margaritu</h4><p begin="00:51:40.62" data-timestamp="3100.62">They don't pay us because they have no money. Truth is that the last paycheck I took was from July. So my company now owes me the salary from August, September, October, November and December. So I haven't been paid from July.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:52:04.01" data-timestamp="3124.01">So what keeps you going to work?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Katarina Margaritu</h4><p begin="00:52:08.78" data-timestamp="3128.78">Well, I like my work. You see, every day we work we bring money to the company. So every time we bring money we are increasing the possibility to get paid.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:52:29.45" data-timestamp="3149.45">Do you think that you'll get paid the back pay?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Katarina Margaritu</h4><p begin="00:52:32.80" data-timestamp="3152.8">Yes. I'm sure.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:52:35.85" data-timestamp="3155.85">Katerina's boyfriend, you can hear him in the background there, he is not so sure. And I have to say I'm with the boyfriend on this.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:52.52" data-timestamp="3172.52">Europe today feels like a far cry from the dream of Victor Hugo, from that exciting scene at the launch of the euro with the fireworks and the people talking about magic. Germans worry now that they are on the hook for bailing out a huge section of the continent. Half of Germans now think that the euro was bad for Germany.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:53:09.91" data-timestamp="3189.91">And that question, whether or not the euro was a good idea, is on a lot of people's minds. It came up with Katarina in Athens.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Katarina Margaritu</h4><p begin="00:53:16.64" data-timestamp="3196.64">Maybe it's better to go back to drachma.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:53:19.32" data-timestamp="3199.32">The drachma is Greece's old currency. But this idea, at least to her, is a painful one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Katarina Margaritu</h4><p begin="00:53:25.57" data-timestamp="3205.57">I like this whole European family. I like to be a member of this family. We have exchanged ideas and culture all these years being together. I don't want to leave them. It's my family now. But I don't like their currency.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:44.24" data-timestamp="3224.24">In the effort to keep the family together there is one other thing that gets talked about all the time as the only real true solution. Create money. Get the European Central Bank to make a bunch of new euros, which the troubled countries of Europe could use to pay off their debts.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:53:58.89" data-timestamp="3238.89">But the richest country in the club, the one that is calling all the shots right now, firmly does not want that. That country, of course, is Germany. Instead, Germany seems much more focused right now on fixing that old mistake, getting the rest of Europe to act more German, the way they wanted when the euro started.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:54:17.11" data-timestamp="3257.11">The European Central Bank has been making tentative steps in the money-creating direction. But a lot of people say small steps are not enough. They say, in other words, the only way out of this crisis may be for Germany to become even more like the rest of Europe, not the other way around.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:39.47" data-timestamp="3279.47">That was Alex Blumberg. He and Chana Joffe-Walt and Adam Davidson and Zoe Chace are all part of <i>Planet Money</i>, which is an economics reporting project of <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> and <i>NPR News</i>. Their podcast, where you can get reporting like this twice a week, and their blog, are at NPR.org/money.</p><p begin="00:54:55.11" data-timestamp="3295.11">[MUSIC  - &quot;LEARN FROM YOUR MISTAKES&quot; BY JULIAN GASKELL]</p></div>  </div>

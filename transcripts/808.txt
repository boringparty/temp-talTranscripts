  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.07" data-timestamp="1.07"> For over 30 years, five days a week, there was this thing on the radio called The Rest of the Story, just 3 and 1/2, 4 minutes long.</p><p begin="00:00:11.84" data-timestamp="11.84"><i></i> <i></i> <i></i> <i></i> <i></i></p><p begin="00:00:19.18" data-timestamp="19.18">It ran all over the country. Paul Harvey was the announcer. He did two other daily broadcasts that, if you ever heard them, you will never forget them-- the way he wrote, the way he sounded.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:00:30.35" data-timestamp="30.35"> Hello, Americans. This is Paul Harvey. Stand by for news.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:35.09" data-timestamp="35.09"> My first job in Chicago, making stories for NPR, my windows at the NPR Chicago bureau looked out over a section of East Wacker Drive that was called Paul Harvey Drive, which I loved, thinking that somewhere not far from where I was sitting Paul Harvey had been up since 3:30 in the morning, pounding out his scripts on an electric typewriter. At his height, he was on over 1,200 radio stations, which, by the way, is more stations than NPR has-- just a massive audience, 24 million.</p><p begin="00:01:03.44" data-timestamp="63.44">His daily show, <i>Paul</i> <i>Harvey</i> <i>News</i> <i>and</i> <i>Comment,</i> was the day's news delivered like brisk radio poetry with some folksy humor or some conservative opinion. But his show <i>The</i> <i>Rest</i> <i>of</i> <i>the</i> <i>Story</i> is what I want to talk about today. Typical episode, just jumps into the action.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:01:19.37" data-timestamp="79.37"> Now is the winter of Ted's discontent. You can see the frustration, even the anger in his face and in his gait as he stalked down Madison Avenue that blustery morning. He had thought himself, hoped himself a book author, a poet. But the great publishers of New York, New York had repeatedly demonstrated the error of his ways.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:37.97" data-timestamp="97.97"> I have to say, listening to Paul Harvey makes me feel like I need to go back to radio school. He just has this incredibly musical sense of timing, of when to pause, when to take the pitch of his voice up and down, which he would do like a singer climbing up and down the scales. Listen to the way he performs the next few sentences as just one long sentence, including dialogue.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:01:57.05" data-timestamp="117.05"> A volume of verse? Oh, no, no, no, no, no. Verse was not selling these days. Anyhow, Ted's particular volume was simply too different from others in its category. And worse still, it was too fantastic.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:08.48" data-timestamp="128.48"> Who sounds like that?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:02:10.13" data-timestamp="130.13"> Sorry, they had said. 27 times. 27 respected publishing houses had perused Ted's imagined masterpiece and rejected it, and not all of them politely.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:23.03" data-timestamp="143.03"> Ted learned his lesson. He decided he's going to go home and had a plan for his manuscript.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:02:29.15" data-timestamp="149.15"> And he was going to vent his aggravation by burning that document, a bonfire of the inanities.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:36.59" data-timestamp="156.59"> Nice tip of the hat there to the title of a Tom Wolfe novel, or possibly to 15th-century church history. Then Ted runs into a guy he knew from school, Mike McClintock, there in Madison Avenue. And Mike invites him up to his office.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:02:51.02" data-timestamp="171.02"> Well, Mike's new office, the establishment by which he had been hired only three hours earlier, was Vanguard Press, a publisher, and more significant, a publisher Ted had somehow overlooked. And you have guessed the rest. Ted did get published. And he got famous. And later, he would marvel, "If I'd been going down the other side of Madison Avenue that morning, I'd be in the dry cleaning business instead."</p><p begin="00:03:13.58" data-timestamp="193.58">For once upon a grotesque disappointment in the winter of 1937, following an intolerable 27 turndowns by the high priests of the publishing industry of New York, there was a little volume on the verge of incineration entitled <i>And</i> <i>To</i> <i>Think</i> <i>That</i> <i>I</i> <i>Saw</i> <i>It</i> <i>on</i> <i>Mulberry</i> <i>Street.</i> And that was followed by <i>Horton</i> <i>Hatches</i> <i>the</i> <i>Egg.</i> And that was followed by <i>How</i> <i>the</i> <i>Grinch</i> <i>Stole</i> <i>Christmas.</i> Then came <i>The</i> Cat <i>in</i> <i>the</i> <i>Hat,</i> and <i>Green</i> <i>Eggs</i> <i>and</i> <i>Ham,</i> and <i>The</i> <i>Lorax.</i> Of course, you know their creator as Ted Geisel. You know him by his pen name, Dr. Seuss. Only now you know the rest of the story.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:56.93" data-timestamp="236.93"> Lots of episodes of <i>The</i> <i>Rest</i> <i>of</i> <i>the</i> <i>Story</i> are like this one, about somebody who fails at something for a long time, and then there's a lucky break, and everything changes. So for example, there's this skinny, shy, lonely kid who gets beat up till a fireman teaches him to stand and fight. And they became--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:04:17.09" data-timestamp="257.09"> The two-fisted, ultimately tough screen legend you knew as John Wayne.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:26.48" data-timestamp="266.48"> There's the 14-year-old boy who writes to Franklin Delano Roosevelt asking for $10, who turns out to be--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:04:32.21" data-timestamp="272.21"> Fidel Castro. Fidel Castro!</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:37.46" data-timestamp="277.46"> Fidel Castro is actually unusual. Most of the rest of the story episodes are about white guys. We sent away for this MP3 archive of 663 episodes. 34 were women.</p><p begin="00:04:49.10" data-timestamp="289.1">Sometimes Paul Harvey would go historical, like when he tells the story of a practice that was banned in England and colonial America because it led to disorder in the streets, unruly mobs spilling into churches, profanity. But people would not stop doing this thing. It went underground and continues to this day.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Harvey</h4><p begin="00:05:07.43" data-timestamp="307.43"> Americans simply could not, still cannot kick the habit of it, of observing Christmas.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:21.29" data-timestamp="321.29"> I went back and listened to a bunch of these in preparing this week's show. And I've wondered if you could tell the story of <i>The</i> <i>Rest</i> <i>of</i> <i>the</i> <i>Story</i> in the style of <i>The</i> <i>Rest</i> <i>of</i> <i>the</i> <i>Story.</i> OK, here's my best shot at that.</p><p begin="00:05:34.48" data-timestamp="334.48">Mr. Aurandt was 28 years old-- a concert pianist, doing great, performed with orchestras all over the world. But then he decided to make a change, something that would be less nerve-racking than waking up to perform. His new gig? Writing radio scripts, five a week, for somebody else to read. They made thousands this way together.</p><p begin="00:05:59.10" data-timestamp="359.1">The guy on the radio got all the credit. People thought of it as his show. The person who got all the credit was Paul Harvey. Mr. Aurandt was in the background. And who was Mr. Aurandt? Well, I should tell you his full name-- Paul Aurandt, Jr.</p><p begin="00:06:18.57" data-timestamp="378.57">His father? Well, some people once knew him as Paul Aurandt, but you know him by a different name-- Paul Harvey. That's right. Every episode of the rest of the story was written for Paul Harvey by his own son. And now you know the rest of the story.</p><p begin="00:06:47.30" data-timestamp="407.3">What's so enjoyable about these stories is they just put you into this world where it seems like, OK, everything is going one way, and then there's a twist at the end that changes everything. It all looks different. And here at our show this week, we thought, we wanna do that. That seems fun.</p><p begin="00:07:04.05" data-timestamp="424.05">So today, we have three real-life stories where things are going along one path, and then things turn, and the meaning of everything changes. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Burning Down The Couch</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:29.87" data-timestamp="449.87"> Act one, "Burning Down the Couch." OK, so at any point in the last four decades if you've felt depressed or anxious, or for any other reason you decided to see a psychiatrist, there's a <i>Rest</i> <i>of</i> <i>the</i> <i>Story</i> story behind the treatment they offer. It's the story of a man named Ray Osheroff. He was a psychiatry patient in 1979 whose case was part of turning the entire field of psychiatry into what it is today.

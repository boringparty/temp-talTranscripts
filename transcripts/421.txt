  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.40" data-timestamp="0.4"> OK. Imagine for second what it would be like to have the entire country have one opinion about something and you were the only person who disagreed. Out of the entire country. This pretty much happened to the woman I'm about to introduce you to. She was a juror in the criminal trial of Rod Blagojevich.</p><p begin="00:00:17.52" data-timestamp="17.52">You may remember Blagojevich, Governor of Illinois. When Barack Obama became president, Blagojevich was caught on tape talking on the phone about how he might use Obama's senate seat for his own personal profit.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rod Blagojevich</h4><p begin="00:00:29.57" data-timestamp="29.57"> I've got thing, and it's [BLEEP] golden. And I'm just not giving it up for [BLEEP] nothing.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:38.28" data-timestamp="38.28"> When the tapes became public, Blagojevich did the kind of media blitz that did not seem to win many people over. He squirmed on talk shows, he appeared as a contestant on <i>The</i> <i>Apprentice.</i> Here he is about to get fired by Donald Trump.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Trump</h4><p begin="00:00:50.40" data-timestamp="50.4"> Your <i>Harry</i> <i>Potter</i> facts were not accurate. Who did the research?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rod Blagojevich</h4><p begin="00:00:55.56" data-timestamp="55.56"> There was not a specific direction to do the research on <i>Harry</i> <i>Potter,</i> but the inability to learn the product-- and it was an issue in the--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Trump</h4><p begin="00:01:04.88" data-timestamp="64.88"> Was what their inability or your inability?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rod Blagojevich</h4><p begin="00:01:09.10" data-timestamp="69.1"> I suppose I should have directed everybody else to learn the product.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:12.66" data-timestamp="72.66"> And so most of us who saw him on TV decided, &quot;that guy is guilty.&quot; And then the case went to trial. Seven weeks. Jury deliberated for 14 days after that. Six men, six women. 24 counts against the governor. The jury could only agree on one count-- lying to the FBI. They convicted him on that one. It was split, with a handful of not guilty votes in various configurations, on the 23 other counts.</p><p begin="00:01:37.75" data-timestamp="97.75">And on the charge that everybody was the most interested in, selling the senate seat, that was the one charge where the vote was 11 to 1. 11 to 1. The holdout? A 67 year old grandmother, JoAnn Chiakulas, who says that yes, the jury did know that the people of America wanted this guy convicted.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:01:55.09" data-timestamp="115.09"> Well, I think that that was a big part of it. I think that there was a tremendous amount of pressure on the jurors to convict, and I do think a lot of it had to do with people didn't like him. He was an annoyance, he was on TV. And so I think it was like people wanted to make him go away. I mean, I certainly had that feeling. And I think that those of us who had some doubts felt a great deal of pressure.</p><p begin="00:02:28.49" data-timestamp="148.49">It was probably one of the most difficult things I've experienced in life. I couldn't sleep at night. I had headaches, I had stomachaches.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:50.07" data-timestamp="170.07"> Today on our radio show, we have three stories of people who end up as the lone holdout, the last man standing, in various situations. And if these three stories are any indication, it is always pretty dreadful.</p><p begin="00:03:01.61" data-timestamp="181.61">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act Two: Last Man Stand-Up</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:06.87" data-timestamp="186.87"> In JoAnn's case, she says that she's been on a couple of juries before, and nothing like this happened. She went into the case with no particular fondness for Blagojevich and no desire to draw attention to herself. In fact, it was kind of the opposite on that score. In the aftermath of the trial, hounded by the press for weeks, she did one interview. I was her second.</p><p begin="00:03:24.87" data-timestamp="204.87">She says she simply felt that the prosecution failed to make its case. When she looked at the entirety of the secret tape recordings of Blagojevich, he said so many scattered different things about what he would do with that senate seat-- maybe he'd give it to Oprah, maybe he'd take it himself, maybe he'd leverage it into something else-- it just didn't seem to rise to the level of a criminal conspiracy, which was the charge against him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:03:45.39" data-timestamp="225.39"> There were enough other things in the transcripts that could make one think that this guy was, like, a little nuts and just talking. Just talking off the top of his head.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:06.65" data-timestamp="246.65"> Do you remember when you got your first hint that you were seeing the case differently than some of the other people on the jury?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:04:13.82" data-timestamp="253.82"> Probably almost as soon as we were beginning our deliberations. I think when we came back into the room, as people started to talk, there were some people who were very firm in their belief that he was guilty, and there were some other people who were not so certain of that. I think what happened as we went along is that the people who felt he was guilty did not look at anything to the contrary. I got a sense that there was no sense that there might be reasonable doubt or there could be reasonable doubt. So whenever anyone in the room raised an issue that might have questioned the guilty verdict, those that felt that he was guilty were very antagonistic, very hostile, very critical, very demeaning.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:14.25" data-timestamp="314.25"> Can I ask you, does some particular example come to mind of some time when you or somebody else raised one of these points and the reaction you got from the side that thought that he was guilty?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:05:24.06" data-timestamp="324.06"> Well, I think that some of us felt that some of the material was open to interpretation as to what the governor meant when he said certain things. And it was often the women who had more reasonable doubt then the men. Most of the men on the jury felt that the defendant was guilty. We were criticized.</p><p begin="00:05:55.98" data-timestamp="355.98">And it was so difficult in the room sometimes that one juror cried several times because she felt that her point of view was not being listened to. We were made to feel that we were not smart enough, we weren't looking at the evidence correctly, we were misinterpreting the facts.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:23.97" data-timestamp="383.97"> You're saying that the men were saying things that were demeaning. What kinds of things did they say?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:06:28.42" data-timestamp="388.42"> Well, I can't recall specifically, but it was a dismissive attitude. &quot;Oh, you're not looking at the facts.&quot; &quot;We have to convict him, because the prosecution will have to retry the case if we don't.&quot; Or, we'll be embarrassed if we don't find him guilty.&quot;</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:54.55" data-timestamp="414.55"> Hey JoAnn, are there any other moments for you that stand out when you were deliberating?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:07:01.03" data-timestamp="421.03"> One gentleman who sat somewhere else decided that he had to sit across from me so that he could look into my eyes. And I felt it was very much an act of intimidation. I think that one day in particular towards the end-- I think we had been there for 12, 13 days at that point-- and I think that was the day the gentleman sat across for me, and another man said he wanted to tell the judge that one of the jurors was not deliberating in good faith. I really felt that I was going to lose it that day because of the pressure.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:49.94" data-timestamp="469.94"> What happened after the verdicts were announced? What happened to you then?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joann Chiakulas</h4><p begin="00:07:53.80" data-timestamp="473.8"> Well, we were cautioned by the judge to not give any statement and to take a few days to think about how we wanted to approach it. And we all agreed that we were not going to talk about who voted how, and that we were all going to go home and not give a statement. So we all left thinking that that was going to be the case.</p><p begin="00:08:21.04" data-timestamp="501.04">But I could not go home, because I received a phone call after I got off the train that there were swarms of reporters in front of my house. And so I went straight to my daughter's. And at that point, the phone started ringing nonstop and went on for days, if not weeks. We turned on the TV, and we saw some of the jurors talking immediately. They were pointing to the fact that there was one juror who was the holdout, and the press figured out that it was me.</p><p begin="00:09:00.06" data-timestamp="540.06">There was a helicopter flying over my daughter's house, which is where I was saying. One gentleman on TV, who was a reporter, said that he had listened to the evidence and he definitely thought that Blagojevich was guilty. And the only way that he could explain the fact that I voted not guilty was because I was crazy. So it was absolutely overwhelming. I don't think it stopped for at least a month.</p><p begin="00:09:30.60" data-timestamp="570.6">And I think that it-- I don't think, I know that it really made me question jury service in general in terms of, if you go in and you do your duty as a citizen, you're vilified if you do not give the verdict that people want and expect. If a person who serves on a jury knows that they are going to be vilified, harassed after the fact, maybe that'll have an effect on how they act in the jury room.

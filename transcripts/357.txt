    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.31" data-timestamp="0.31"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, distributed by Public Radio International. Where I am. Who I'm with.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:18.50" data-timestamp="18.5"> This girl is a high school senior. And let's just identify her as living somewhere in North America. And, probably, the biggest lie she perpetrated against her mom was an ongoing deception that lasted two years, starting when she was 14, a freshman.</p><p begin="00:00:32.40" data-timestamp="32.4">She would stay out till midnight in the park with her friends, drinking, most Friday and Saturday nights, which her mom definitely would not allow. So she told her mom that they were at a Chinese restaurant every weekend for two years. She'd come home drunk and convince her mom she was sober.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Girl</h4><p begin="00:00:48.70" data-timestamp="48.7"> What I'm doing, she never normally, really finds out. Like, I think she has no idea that I hung out in a park for two years of my life.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:57.44" data-timestamp="57.44"> And what would happen if she found out?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Girl</h4><p begin="00:01:00.95" data-timestamp="60.95"> I think she'd just be really upset, because she really thinks she knows exactly what's going on in my life, and she really has no idea. I think that would just hurt her.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:09.75" data-timestamp="69.75"> Oh, she thinks you guys are really close.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Girl</h4><p begin="00:01:11.79" data-timestamp="71.79"> Yeah, she thinks we are really close. And, yeah, she thinks she knows everything I do, and she doesn't.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:18.99" data-timestamp="78.99"> But now, they're both at a turning point. Graduation is next week, which makes this girl just old enough that she's starting to wonder if, someday, she should let her mom know the truth about all of it. In the past when she thought about telling her mom the truth, it was actually more  of a revenge kind of thing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Girl</h4><p begin="00:01:33.75" data-timestamp="93.75"> There have been points where, I really just want my mom to feel kind of stupid and let down. Has been points when, I've been really mad at her, we haven't been getting along, that's been like, I want to make you feel like you really don't know what you're talking about. Because every time she's always like, you know I'll always catch you. You know I always know what you're doing.</p><p begin="00:01:50.58" data-timestamp="110.58">And there's been points where I just want to be like, listen mom, you really don't know what's going on. This is what's happened for the past four years of my life. You really haven't known much. It would just be like a really big power trip for me.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:00.52" data-timestamp="120.52"> And now with school ending and college so soon, already her mom is loosening up the reins and not checking up on her so much. And if it did come out, she'd want it to be for a good reason.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Girl</h4><p begin="00:02:15.53" data-timestamp="135.53"> I mean, I might not never tell her, might not ever tell her about the park. I really do want to become close to my mom again.</p><p begin="00:02:29.54" data-timestamp="149.54">We had a really big falling out, especially, when she started going out with her boyfriend. In a way it's remarkably rare for the truth to come out at all. It takes ingenuity, and effort, and cunning, and a decision.</p><p begin="00:03:35.25" data-timestamp="215.25">Today we have three stories of lies, finally exposed, each under very dramatic circumstances. Act one, "Lieland" in which we bring you a cheerfully and surprisingly unrepentant story about lying.</p><p begin="00:03:47.89" data-timestamp="227.89">Act two, "The Spy Who Bugged Me," journalist Lawrence Wright tries to uncover some basic truths about whether his phone is being tapped and by whom. And when he finds the whom behind it, they are also surprisingly unrepentant.</p><p begin="00:04:00.75" data-timestamp="240.75">Act three, "Rosa In The Study With the ATM Card," A woman tries to get to the bottom of a domestic mystery in her father's house. And the suspect, one of the butlers, kind of. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Lieland</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:19.84" data-timestamp="259.84"> Act one, "Lieland," we start with this story about lying from Etgar Keret, who has been on our show before. He is an Israeli writer. This story was read for us by actor Dermot Mulroney.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dermot Mulroney</h4><p begin="00:04:29.50" data-timestamp="269.5"> Robbie was seven when he told his first lie. His mother had given him a wrinkled, old, 10 lira bill and asked him to go buy a pack of king-sized Kents at the grocery store. Robbie bought an ice cream cone instead. He took the change and hid the coins under a white rock in the backyard of their apartment building.</p><p begin="00:04:49.08" data-timestamp="289.08">And when mother asked him what had happened, he told her that a giant red-headed kid, with a front-tooth missing, tackled him in the street, slapped him, and took the money. Mother believed him. And Robbie hadn't stopped lying since.</p><p begin="00:05:07.83" data-timestamp="307.83">When he was in high school, he spent the better part of an entire week vegging out on the beach in Eilat, after selling the student counselor a story about his aunt from Beersheba, who discovered she had cancer. When he was in the army, this imaginary aunt went blind and saved his ass big-time for going AWOL-- no detention, not even confined to barracks, nothing.</p><p begin="00:05:29.35" data-timestamp="329.35">Once, he justified being two hours late to work with a lie about a German shepherd he'd found lying beside the road. The dog had been run over, and Robbie had taken it to the vet. In this lie, the dog remained paralyzed in two of its legs. Those legs did the trick.</p><p begin="00:05:47.03" data-timestamp="347.03">There were a lot of lies along the way in Robbie's life: lies without arms, and lies that were ill, lies that do harm, and lies that could kill, lies with legs, lies on wheels, lies in tuxedos, lies that steal, lies he made up in a flash and just as soon forgot about.</p><p begin="00:06:11.71" data-timestamp="371.71">It all started with a dream, a short, fuzzy dream about his dead mother. In this dream, the two of them are sitting on a straw mat in the middle of a clear, white surface, devoid of all detail, that seemed to have no beginning and no end. Next to them on this infinite, white surface was a gumball machine with a bubble top, the old fashioned kind where you put a coin in the slot, turn the handle and out comes a gumball.</p><p begin="00:06:39.33" data-timestamp="399.33">And in his dream, Robbie's mother told him that the afterworld was driving her up the wall. Because the people there were OK, but there were no cigarettes-- not just no cigarettes, no coffee or talk shows, nothing.</p><p begin="00:06:55.66" data-timestamp="415.66">"You've got to help me Robbie," she said. "You've got to buy me a gumball. I raised you. All these years, I gave you everything and asked for nothing, but now it's time to give something back to your mom. Get me a gum ball, a red one if you can, but blue is OK, too."</p><p begin="00:07:14.32" data-timestamp="434.32">And in his dream, Robbie rummaged through his pockets, over and over, trying to find some change. Nothing.</p><p begin="00:07:22.00" data-timestamp="442">"I haven't got any, Mom," he said, the tears welling in his eyes. "I haven't got any change. I've gone through all my pockets."</p><p begin="00:07:35.70" data-timestamp="455.7">Considering that he never cried when he was awake, it was strange to be crying in his dream.</p><p begin="00:07:41.74" data-timestamp="461.74">"Did you look under the rock?" his mother asked, and clasped his hands in her own. "Maybe the coins are still there."</p><p begin="00:07:51.01" data-timestamp="471.01">Then he woke up. It was 5:00 AM on a Saturday, still dark out. Robbie found himself getting into the car and driving to the place where he had grown up. On a Saturday morning with no traffic on the road, it took less than 20 minutes to get there. On the ground floor of the building, where Plisken's grocery store had been, there was a dollar store. You know, there's a dilemma. I was talking to some <i>Times</i> reporters-- they've been advised by one of their intelligence sources that they should get new cell phones. ABC had a similar kind of discussion about whether we should all change our phones, or whether we should behave like terrorists by getting disposable cell phones, have a conversation and throw it away. That's the way terrorists evade this kind of monitoring.</p><p begin="00:38:30.85" data-timestamp="2310.85">I, so far, haven't done that. And maybe I'm being naive about it, but there are times when, if I come up on a question that might require a really confidential answer, that I've been more interested in doing it in person than on the phone.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:38:54.82" data-timestamp="2334.82"> Have you had a situation where you realize that you know more about Al Qaeda than the people who are supposed to be the experts?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lawrence Wright</h4><p begin="00:39:04.12" data-timestamp="2344.12"> All the time. I mean, it's pretty demoralizing when you are talking to people that are on the Al Qaeda Squad, and they can't pronounce the names of these guys. When the head of counterterrorism for the FBI testifies under oath that he doesn't know the difference between a Sunni and a Shiite. and he thinks that's an irrelevant question. I mean, if you don't know the first thing about the enemy you're fighting, you're always going to be handicapped, blindfolded, deaf-and-dumb. And really, that's the situation that you find.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:39:43.61" data-timestamp="2383.61"> It seems like, in a weird way, that is the most frustrating thing of this whole thing. If it seemed like they had more information than you do, with their heightened ability, than it would maybe make more sense. But it seems like they're doing all this, and it's still not putting them ahead of a single reporter with no special access to anybody and just his own reporting skills. I mean, is that too strong?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lawrence Wright</h4><p begin="00:40:12.20" data-timestamp="2412.2"> No, it's not at all. There are some good people involved in this effort. I'm not denigrating them. But when I wrote my book, one of the heroes in my book was a native Arabic speaking FBI agent who came closer than anybody to stopping 9/11. He was one of eight Arabic speaking agents in the FBI at that time. Now, nearly seven years later, there are nine.</p><p begin="00:40:44.20" data-timestamp="2444.2">And I'm picking on the FBI because they are open about their figures. It's true all across the intelligence community. They don't have the kind of people that really understand who these people are, where they come from, and what they're fighting for. Who's in the FBI, especially in headquarters seventh floor in Washington? Irish and Italian guys.</p><p begin="00:41:06.89" data-timestamp="2466.89">And if you look at the history of that organization, which made its reputation fighting the mafia and, to some extent, the IRA, it's no wonder that they were successful in fighting against those communities. They came from the same neighborhoods. They spoke the same languages. They knew the culture. They don't know these people.</p><p begin="00:41:29.20" data-timestamp="2489.2">And if you have family in the Middle East, essentially, you are not going to be a candidate to be in America's intelligence community. And that rules out almost everybody who fluently, natively speaks not just Arabic, but Farsi, Dari, Pashto, all these languages of these communities that we need to understand so vitally. We've done such a poor job of understanding, much less penetrating and disrupting that organization simply because we don't have the right people.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:41:59.29" data-timestamp="2519.29"> Lawrence Wright, talking to <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> producer Alex Blumberg. Wright is the author of the book <i>The</i> <i>Looming</i> <i>Tower</i>. Just a few weeks ago, he did another story about Al Qaeda for <i>The</i> <i>New</i> <i>Yorker.</i> You can read that story for free on the magazine's website, newyorker.com where you can find lots of great stories.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act3">
  <h3>Act Three: Rosa In The Study With The ATM Card</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:42:33.25" data-timestamp="2553.25"> Act three-- "Rosa in the Study with the ATM Card." We close today's show about uncovering the truth with a story about how difficult it can be to get to what really happened, even when you're looking at what seems like the simplest kind of crime possible. To preserve everybody's privacy in this story, we have changed all their names. And the woman in the story, who is going to remain anonymous, has an elderly dad. He's over 80, and he's has gone through a variety of medical emergencies, kidney stones, growth on scalp, heart attack, blood in his urine, hernia surgery. And he has several caretakers who come to his house. His daughter steps when there's a crisis.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:43:09.69" data-timestamp="2589.69"> During a recent visit, my father tells me that Lourdes, who cooks his meals, is on the verge of quitting. "It is something about Rosa," my father says.</p><p begin="00:43:19.94" data-timestamp="2599.94">I look at him blankly. "Rosa?"</p><p begin="00:43:23.16" data-timestamp="2603.16">"You found her for me," he says.</p><p begin="00:43:25.53" data-timestamp="2605.53">"You mean they sent her from one of the agencies," I remind him.</p><p begin="00:43:29.81" data-timestamp="2609.81">"I guess so. Anyway, can you deal with it?"</p><p begin="00:43:34.33" data-timestamp="2614.33">As my father has aged, he's grown proficient at delegating. Some of this stems from his Parkinson's, but some of it is just my dad. Though he grew up working-class in the depression, his passions run more in the line of those of a rich, country squire: opera, fine wine, domestic servants. Gradually, he's enlisted a growing army of caretakers to minister to his needs.</p><p begin="00:44:01.12" data-timestamp="2641.12">Lourdes arrives at his house later that afternoon. I ask her if there is anything she wants to say.</p><p begin="00:44:08.03" data-timestamp="2648.03">"Si," she says pivoting towards my father. "I'm leaving you, Mr. Ken. There are too many people in your business. Yesterday, you couldn't find your cash. And I don't know who this Rosa is. Why does she have your ATM? I ask you two times Mr. Ken, who is this Rosa?"</p><p begin="00:44:26.95" data-timestamp="2666.95">"It sounds like you don't trust her," I say to Lourdes.</p><p begin="00:44:30.28" data-timestamp="2670.28">"No, she is always whispering, giggling, an ass kisser."</p><p begin="00:44:35.68" data-timestamp="2675.68">A couple weeks later, I get a message from my dad's bookkeeper, Amy, the general of my father's army of caretakers. "I'm looking at your dad's books and there's something going on here," she says ominously. "Please, give me a call as soon as it's convenient."</p><p begin="00:44:52.12" data-timestamp="2692.12">Amy tells me she's discovered strange charges on my dad's Visa card, huge ATM withdrawals, and fat checks signed in an unfamiliar handwriting and made out to a woman named Rosa. "I'm looking at upwards of $6,000 missing from your dad's checking accounts. And then there's $20,000 that seems to be missing from his investments," she says. "I never heard of this Rosa before. Your dad says you found her."</p><p begin="00:45:23.12" data-timestamp="2723.12">For the rest of the day, my brother and I are on the phone with Amy getting updates on her accounting discoveries. For a long time, we kids have felt my father is pressing his luck living alone. Amy calls to say she has gone over to my father's and confronted him.</p><p begin="00:45:40.44" data-timestamp="2740.44">Apparently, he told her it was all easily explained. He had lent Rosa money for her mortgage. Amy says, she asked to see the paperwork for the loan, and he refused. Then he said it was a gift. Then he said he didn't remember.</p><p begin="00:46:01.85" data-timestamp="2761.85">Amy goes on, "I found these weird charges, like one at Macy's and another Victoria's Secret," she says. "Your father says, he was out with Rosa, and she saw some jewelry, and so he bought it for her. He said it was only $40. And I said, but Ken, the charge was for almost $100. And he said, oh well, maybe that was it." Then she adds, "Lourdes told me she thinks Rosa is a prostitute, because of how she acts and dresses. She says she thinks your father has fallen in love with her."</p><p begin="00:46:36.88" data-timestamp="2796.88">Apparently, Lourdes has told Amy that she's been cleaning up a lot of dinners for two with wine glasses, which she took to be signs that Rosa was visiting him at night. I get this awful feeling. Amy tells me she has a plan.</p><p begin="00:46:53.04" data-timestamp="2813.04">She wants to go to my father's house tomorrow morning when Rosa is scheduled to pick up my father for a dentist appointment. The plan is to confront Rosa and get to the bottom of this. Amy's husband, who happens to be a high-ranking military officer just home from Iraq, will also help. She wants me to be there too.</p><p begin="00:47:15.90" data-timestamp="2835.9">The next morning, I drive south through the oaky hills of central California to my father's home. As I approach, the weedy medians give way to manicured landscaping, roses, carved bushes, and lawns clipped like French velvet. My father had once cultivated a similarly refined community of friends, people with multiple homes, colleagues with charitable trusts, but now that's changed.</p><p begin="00:47:43.34" data-timestamp="2863.34">Though he still lives in the same place, his friends mostly include his caretakers and their families. A lot of them live paycheck to paycheck. He hear stories about broken hearts and broken down cars, bouts of depression and unemployment, and tells them to me.</p><p begin="00:48:07.68" data-timestamp="2887.68">When I arrive, my father sits in the monstrous Naugahyde recliner we bought for him, a veritable throne of old age. C-SPAN is on, as it often is.</p><p begin="00:48:19.14" data-timestamp="2899.14">"I'm glad you came," my father says.</p><p begin="00:48:22.90" data-timestamp="2902.9">When Amy and her husband, Mike, pull up, I meet them outside. Mike is wearing crisply ironed fatigues and army boots. We enter the house, and Amy bends down to my father and hugs him. She broaches the subject of Rosa's use of his Visa over the weekend, which my father gave her to buy herself some hospital scrubs for a new job.</p><p begin="00:48:47.49" data-timestamp="2927.49">"We're looking at nearly $700 spent at Target and Walmart," Amy says. "If it turns out she's stolen this money, do you agree to press charges?"</p><p begin="00:48:57.81" data-timestamp="2937.81">My father looks at her, "You haven't met her. You can't just accuse someone. You don't even know her."</p><p begin="00:49:05.31" data-timestamp="2945.31">"Ken, I don't need to know her. I have the evidence right here. What kind of services is she providing to you, Ken?" she asks.</p><p begin="00:49:14.80" data-timestamp="2954.8">"Nothing."</p><p begin="00:49:16.27" data-timestamp="2956.27">Her face is inches from his. "I know this is hard for you  to accept that someone you care for is taking advantage of you, but what's going on is just not right."</p><p begin="00:49:27.67" data-timestamp="2967.67">"You shouldn't accuse her. You need to talk to her first." My father bangs his hand on the arm of the chair, "God damn it. I've told you everything is all right, and it is. If you won't listen to me, you can get out of my house." His hands are shaking, but not the loose trembling that comes from neurological misfiring. It's concentrated rage.</p><p begin="00:49:49.53" data-timestamp="2989.53">Amy stands, "You want me to leave? I'm leaving." Mike stands, holding Amy's coat for her. Amy turns to dad, "It won't break my business, losing you as a client, but it will break my heart."</p><p begin="00:50:04.08" data-timestamp="3004.08">As she walks towards the door, I look at my father, "Dad, say something."</p><p begin="00:50:09.96" data-timestamp="3009.96">My dad relents, "I don't really want you to go," he says. "I just don't want you yelling at this woman."</p><p begin="00:50:18.73" data-timestamp="3018.73">We sit and wait. Finally, an old-model American car pulls up. We peer through the windows, careful not to tip her off. Then Rosa walks in. She's wearing baggy layers over a heavyset frame. Her hair is cut short. She wears no makeup. She bears no resemblance to the Victoria Secret-wearing wine-swilling seductress I had imagined.</p><p begin="00:50:45.63" data-timestamp="3045.63">She walks into the room and kisses my father on the cheek. "How are you, Ken?" she asks.</p><p begin="00:50:52.63" data-timestamp="3052.63">"I've been better," says my dad.</p><p begin="00:50:55.73" data-timestamp="3055.73">Then she looks around, noticing the strange faces in the room. Amy, Mike and I introduce ourselves and shake hands with her. Mike stand sentry at the door. Amy asks her to sit down and tells her that there's money missing from Ken's accounts. We ask her about getting a loan or a gift from Ken.</p><p begin="00:51:18.09" data-timestamp="3078.09">She looks baffled and turns to my father, "No, Ken, I earned that money. Remember, I spent 10 days here, 12 hour shifts." My father nods.</p><p begin="00:51:29.81" data-timestamp="3089.81">I explain, "It's that he can't exactly recall how this money has been spent, but he's spending it a lot faster than he has in the past."</p><p begin="00:51:40.42" data-timestamp="3100.42">She looks at me pleadingly, "I'm sorry," she says quietly. "I thought he was alert."</p><p begin="00:51:54.68" data-timestamp="3114.68">Point by point, Amy goes over his suspicious expenditures. At every point, Rosa defends herself. All the hours she's been working, neither she nor Ken kept track, but she says they were extensive. The Visa use over the weekend, she bought a lot of scrubs and planned to pay my father back.</p><p begin="00:52:15.05" data-timestamp="3135.05">Since my father gives out his ATM card to all his caretakers, she can't really be expected to explain all the withdrawals. She says the checks made out to her name were payment for more work. I don't know what to think. The relationship between my father and his caretakers is so intimate. They do stuff for him I won't do, all for a few bucks an hour.</p><p begin="00:52:39.70" data-timestamp="3159.7">It hardly seems crazy that my father and Rosa might have had long talks over dinner, that my father would have wanted to repay her kindness, and that Rosa would have wanted to accept. Still, I can tell Amy remains unmoved. For her, the numbers tell the whole story. If I defend Rosa, dad will lose both Lourdes and Amy, and I still won't know what happened.</p><p begin="00:53:06.46" data-timestamp="3186.46">With halfhearted logic, I decide she's crossed some professional line Even if she isn't swindling my dad, caretakers shouldn't go shopping for jewelry with their clients or use the client's ATM card to buy hundreds of dollars' worth of clothing. When I tell Rosa she can't work for Ken anymore, she looks like she doesn't know what hit her.</p><p begin="00:53:30.06" data-timestamp="3210.06">"Can I say goodbye to him?" she asks.</p><p begin="00:53:34.07" data-timestamp="3214.07">I watched her lean over him and whisper in his ear. I realize, if this were a movie, and I were watching this scene, I know one thing for sure. I'd hate the daughter. After it happened, we really didn't talk about it. I wondered if he was angry at us, or if he just wanted to forget the whole incident. He's 81 and tires easily. But I thought that if I could just get him to tell me what he thought happened, I could stop guessing.</p><p begin="00:54:12.01" data-timestamp="3252.01">Well, what do you remember? I mean, do you remember giving her a loan, or do you remember giving her gifts?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ken</h4><p begin="00:54:20.26" data-timestamp="3260.26"> Yes, I gave her $1,000 because she had a $3,000 mortgage. She was divorced. She didn't have a lot of money. She was in transition from one job to another. And I helped her out.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:54:38.55" data-timestamp="3278.55"> And did you feel like, did you think at some point that it was a loan or a gift?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ken</h4><p begin="00:54:47.74" data-timestamp="3287.74"> I guess originally, I thought of it as a loan, but I think as time went on, I assumed it would be a gift because she just couldn't pay it back in any reasonable time. And that was fine with me. It still was helpful to her, and I felt good about it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:55:07.25" data-timestamp="3307.25"> So, how did you feel when people started to question your judgment?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ken</h4><p begin="00:55:13.03" data-timestamp="3313.03"> Well, I didn't like it of course, because I felt like it was my own business, what I did with my money. You know, you might be heir to my money, but you don't control it now, right?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:55:26.65" data-timestamp="3326.65"> Right.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ken</h4><p begin="00:55:31.99" data-timestamp="3331.99"> I feel like I've been misrepresented on the situation, that I can't take care of myself. And that I somewhat resented it, because I felt like I was in control. You know, I know these things happen, where these older people get taken. I just didn't feel like I was one of them. Even if she had taken me for every dime, it wouldn't be a big deal. It's not a lot of money.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:56:02.42" data-timestamp="3362.42"> It also made me feel like, you're were kind of feeling a certain kind of sympathy. Where you were like, even if she stole several thousand bucks from me, that's not a big deal. She's struggling, she's got little kids.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ken</h4><p begin="00:56:15.60" data-timestamp="3375.6"> Right, absolutely. Absolutely, that's part of it. From what I understand, she's a good mother. She just struggles to get along. Anyway, I'm tired of talking about it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:56:38.24" data-timestamp="3398.24"> OK.</p><p begin="00:56:44.94" data-timestamp="3404.94">My father never used to seem like someone who lost sleep over other people's troubles, especially people radically different from him. But now I think he might. Amy the bookkeeper tells me, she's always canceling generous contributions he makes to far flung charities he's seen on late night TV. She thinks these bouts of insomniac philanthropy are a sign he's not looking out for his own best interests.</p><p begin="00:57:12.53" data-timestamp="3432.53">But I'm not so sure. What if instead, my father is becoming the person we all want to be, someone who doesn't fixate on the bottom line, but empathizes with people in need. Then again, as I'm getting ready to go home after our interview, I hear my dad talking on the phone, making an appointment, giving out his address. When I inquire who it was, he says it was a very nice guy selling reverse mortgages. "A telemarketer," I ask, "coming to your home?"</p><p begin="00:57:45.13" data-timestamp="3465.13">"Don't worry," he says, playing with the remote control. "I know what I'm doing."</p></div>  </div>

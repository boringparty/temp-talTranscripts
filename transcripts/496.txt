  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.15" data-timestamp="1.15"> OK. I listen to a lot of podcasts. I host a radio show that is distributed as a podcast. So I had a special interest in some interviews that one of my colleagues, Zoe Chace, did recently. She's one of the reporters for <i>Planet</i> <i>Money,</i> which itself is a podcast.</p><p begin="00:00:14.27" data-timestamp="14.27">Zoe was interviewing these two guys, Jim Logan and Richard Baker, who say that their company, Personal Audio, holds the patent on podcasts. And basically, anybody who makes a podcast owes them money. Zoe pointed out to them that their interview was going to be used on a podcast that has not paid them.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Zoe Chace</h4><p begin="00:00:31.61" data-timestamp="31.61"> So are we doing something illegal right now, though?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Baker</h4><p begin="00:00:37.25" data-timestamp="37.25">I wouldn't comment on that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:00:38.30" data-timestamp="38.3">I wouldn't comment on it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jim Logan</h4><p begin="00:00:39.32" data-timestamp="39.32">We don't-- I don't comment on the legalities.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:41.31" data-timestamp="41.31"> Jim Logan says that he is one of the inventors of podcasting, even though he has never made a podcast himself. Here's how he figures. His company Personal Audio, back at the dawn of the internet age in 1996-- this is before the iPod existed-- it's before most homes were on the internet-- they decided to manufacture an MP3 player that would do all kinds of stuff that nobody seemed to be doing just yet, like downloading sound from the internet. And Jim says they filed for a patent.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jim Logan</h4><p begin="00:01:07.08" data-timestamp="67.08"> We didn't use these words back then. But buried within that patent description were ideas such as playlist and podcasting.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:14.66" data-timestamp="74.66"> They tried to design and manufacture their newfangled MP3 player for about a year, he says. And they failed. They gave up.</p><p begin="00:01:20.71" data-timestamp="80.71">So they never actually succeeded in making a device that could download audio off the internet and play it. They never made a podcast. Instead, they switched to a different technology-- an earlier technology. They decided to distribute magazine articles on cassette tapes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jim Logan</h4><p begin="00:01:33.93" data-timestamp="93.93"> --which was kind of a crude form of podcasting, you might say.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:41.77" data-timestamp="101.77"> In this version of the history of podcasting, this right here would be one of the very first podcasts. And there is nothing digital about it at all. This is one of those old cassette tapes. These guys brought a stack of them to the interview.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:01:53.34" data-timestamp="113.34"> Welcome to "Amazing-- the Best of Popular Science," brought to you by Magazines on Tape. Coming up, answers to some questions you have probably wondered about. What happens in your head when you have a headache? How does quicksand work? Why does your car--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:07.82" data-timestamp="127.82"> This business also failed. But the way our patent system works, even though these guys never successfully created a system that took podcasts from the internet and put them into a portable audio player, they patented the idea that such a thing could happen. And now that other people have figured out how to do it, they want to get paid. It's only fair, Jim Logan told Zoe.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jim Logan</h4><p begin="00:02:29.59" data-timestamp="149.59"> I put my dollars and my time and my energy on the line. I took the market risk. And I went to market 10 years before people say that podcasting was invented in 2005. We were out there in-- well, not 10 years. We were out there in 1998, probably, with an offering.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Zoe Chace</h4><p begin="00:02:48.32" data-timestamp="168.32"> But I mean, these are cassette tapes. Like, this is not the iPhone.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:02:54.92" data-timestamp="174.92">Well, remember, the patent talks to having it very similar to an iPhone-- a computerized device that would do this. What was happening there is they couldn't get the product done.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:06.41" data-timestamp="186.41"> These guys say that for now, they're not asking nonprofit podcasters, like our program, to pay them licensing fees-- just for-profit podcast businesses, like Adam Carolla and <i>The</i> <i>Stuff</i> <i>You</i> <i>Should</i> <i>Know</i> podcast, which is owned by Discovery Communications, and Marc Maron's comedy podcast, <i>WTF,</i> which Maron produces literally in his garage.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marc Maron</h4><p begin="00:03:23.97" data-timestamp="203.97"> There's no way I can afford to fight a court battle. And they know that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:28.15" data-timestamp="208.15"> --which means he probably has to pay, though the letter he got doesn't specify a price.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marc Maron</h4><p begin="00:03:32.73" data-timestamp="212.73"> Why wouldn't they have a number? Why don't they have a percentage in mind or a number that they think is justified? Why don't they have that? Why aren't they willing to say that?</p><p begin="00:03:42.49" data-timestamp="222.49">Like, yeah, you all owe us $10. Why are they being cagey about that? Because it's a shakedown.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Baker</h4><p begin="00:03:48.78" data-timestamp="228.78"> We have a price. We just don't want to make it public.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:53.28" data-timestamp="233.28"> I found Richard Baker of Personal Audio to ask the price myself. He wouldn't tell me, either.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:57.93" data-timestamp="237.93"> The reason why I'm asking this is because this podcaster said, if this is a fair price-- if you have a fair claim to make-- why wouldn't you name the price publicly if it's a fair price?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Baker</h4><p begin="00:04:10.51" data-timestamp="250.51"> For one, most of our licensees don't want people to know how much they've paid or how many times they're downloading. If we were to give a public price of what it is-- say we settled with someone, and say they paid us $200,000-- someone could easily do the math and figure out exactly what their downloads are.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:37.72" data-timestamp="277.72"> Can you see why I'm asking this? One of the podcasters said, you are not naming a price because it's a shakedown. It's the secrecy that this podcaster found alarming.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Baker</h4><p begin="00:04:52.02" data-timestamp="292.02"> Honestly, we believe this is a very, very fair price. Our goal is to make sure these podcasters succeed so that everybody makes money.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:03.18" data-timestamp="303.18"> Settlements that Personal Audio has made in the past with Apple Computer and with some cellphone companies are also not public information. And the fact is most patent settlements are not public. And you might think, OK, well, so what? But these patent lawsuits are huge. And they're on the rise.</p><p begin="00:05:19.52" data-timestamp="319.52">The number of patent lawsuits climbed 50% in one year from 2011 to 2012. Entrepreneurs and engineers say these suits hurt and sometimes destroy young companies. They have an effect on the economy, on what we can and cannot do. But it is very hard to find out exactly what is going on.</p><p begin="00:05:35.80" data-timestamp="335.8">A good example-- two years ago, we devoted an episode of our program to the patent system. And we tried to explain what was going on by focusing on the company that some people say is the biggest patent troll out there. A patent troll is a company that uses patents mostly to sue other companies and ask for licensing fees.</p><p begin="00:05:52.23" data-timestamp="352.23">And in that show, we investigated this one patent that the company was involved with. The inventor whose name was on that patent was Chris Crawford. And we learned all kinds of things in that investigation.</p><p begin="00:06:02.48" data-timestamp="362.48">But the world of these lawsuits is so secretive there were basic questions we could not answer, like how much money was involved. What did Chris Crawford think of what was being done with his patent? There were 18 companies being sued for infringement on the patent. What was their opinion? How were they handling it?</p><p begin="00:06:18.53" data-timestamp="378.53">Well, two years have passed since then. And I am pleased to say that today-- today-- we can bring you a show where we actually answer the questions that usually in these kinds of cases go unanswered. It is a rare look inside this world.</p><p begin="00:06:30.60" data-timestamp="390.6">That litigation from two years ago has finished. The silence has been broken. Many, many facts have emerged that, frankly, kind of stunned us.</p><p begin="00:06:37.16" data-timestamp="397.16">And so today on our show, what we're going to do-- in the first half of the program, we're going to return to that old episode from 2011 explaining the patent and the lawsuit. And then in the second half, we're going to have the answers to many of the mysteries that were still unsolved two years ago.</p><p begin="00:06:52.02" data-timestamp="412.02">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Stick around.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">

And of course, one of the excellent attorneys that worked on this for EMC and Carbonite located that document and questioned Chris about it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Attorney</h4><p begin="00:43:30.70" data-timestamp="2610.7">The May 23, 1992, proposal, which is marked as Exhibit 10.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chris Crawford</h4><p begin="00:43:38.19" data-timestamp="2618.19"> Yes. I'm looking at it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:43:40.30" data-timestamp="2620.3"> And here he is, the man we've been looking for, Chris Crawford, the man who was granted patent number 5,771,354, one of the patents used to sue 18 companies. This is sound from a videotaped deposition he gave as part of the Oasis trial. He's polished-- crisp white shirt, paisley tie, tortoise-shell reading glasses. He's got sandy brown hair, a tan. The lawyer continues to question him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Attorney</h4><p begin="00:44:08.76" data-timestamp="2648.76">So the first paragraph of this document reads, quote, "This proposal, it is in response to Jack Byrd's idea to provide automated offsite backup services for PC users." And aren't you in the second sentence saying that Jack Byrd had an idea to provide automated offsite backup services for PC users?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chris Crawford</h4><p begin="00:44:39.98" data-timestamp="2679.98"> No. What I'm saying is that Jack Byrd had an idea of me pursuing the automated offsite backup services that I had--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:44:49.44" data-timestamp="2689.44"> Chris Crawford then launches into a rather surprising explanation for how the sentencing that this business was, quote, "Jack Byrd's idea," doesn't actually mean that the business was Jack Byrd's idea. His explanation? He was using the apostrophe S incorrectly.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chris Crawford</h4><p begin="00:45:04.95" data-timestamp="2704.95"> As far as the apostrophe S-- and I'm still not clear if you're trying to derive a specific meaning with respect to the apostrophe S--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:45:15.79" data-timestamp="2715.79"> Of course, what would a sentence reading, "in response to Jack Byrds idea" even mean if the S was plural?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Attorney</h4><p begin="00:45:22.09" data-timestamp="2722.09">I'm asking you, though-- you certainly know what the use of an apostrophe S means, do you not?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chris Crawford</h4><p begin="00:45:32.81" data-timestamp="2732.81"> [SNIFFING] As I've written documents over the years, there are times when I use an apostrophe S, and it seems like I'm supposed to use an apostrophe S. But I have to say that my grammar is not strong enough to tell you right now with clarity when an apostrophe S is used.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:46:08.76" data-timestamp="2768.76"> This explanation, apparently, did not convince the jury, and Oasis lost the case. The jury concluded that the patents were indeed invalid for failing to name the correct co-inventors. Yet this victory just shows how difficult it is to defend yourself against a patent infringement claim. The lawyers for Carbonite believe that without that one document-- that one apostrophe S-- they might have lost.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:46:29.52" data-timestamp="2789.52"> Because here's how tough the jury was. The jury verdict said that the patents were improperly filed because Jack Byrd's name should have been listed as a co-inventor. Just Jack Byrd-- not Chuck Campos and Don Atwood, the other men who testified under oath that they too had been involved in developing the ideas. Only Jack Byrd's name had been on that document. And only Jack Byrd got credit from the jury as a co-inventor.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:46:56.73" data-timestamp="2816.73"> And for the 16 companies that did settle, the verdict may not change anything. In most cases, these licensing agreements have language that makes them nearly impossible to get out of, no matter what happens with the patent later on. This week, we heard back from a spokesman from one of the companies that chose to settle.</p><p begin="00:47:11.91" data-timestamp="2831.91">The spokesman wrote in an email, quote, "We were hit hard by this lawsuit. Infringement on our part seemed completely bogus, but we could not afford to fight it. Even with the settlement, we were forced to lay off employees. We are still--" and "still" is in all caps-- "still paying out on the settlement agreement. We were unaware that the patent had been invalidated. We will be contacting our attorney to see what recourse we may have."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:47:34.62" data-timestamp="2854.62"> Like we've been saying, because so many of these cases settle with nondisclosure agreements, we almost never find out how much money changed hands.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:47:43.03" data-timestamp="2863.03"> But because of the Oasis case, we now have a sense of how much money that can be. Because Carbonite won their case and didn't settle, they weren't bound by a nondisclosure agreement. And they were happy to reveal how much money Oasis wanted from them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Danielle Sheer</h4><p begin="00:47:55.84" data-timestamp="2875.84"> Well, the original number that was thrown out-- I don't think it was official-- was they wanted $20 million.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Friend</h4><p begin="00:48:01.73" data-timestamp="2881.73">Plus a share of our revenues--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Danielle Sheer</h4><p begin="00:48:02.84" data-timestamp="2882.84"> --going forward.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Friend</h4><p begin="00:48:03.42" data-timestamp="2883.42">--ongoing.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:48:03.85" data-timestamp="2883.85"> What?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:48:04.75" data-timestamp="2884.75"> [CHUCKLING]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Danielle Sheer</h4><p begin="00:48:05.66" data-timestamp="2885.66"> Which what David was at was at a little under half of the cash we had in the bank at that time.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Friend</h4><p begin="00:48:10.67" data-timestamp="2890.67">Plus a percentage of our ongoing business forever.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:48:15.37" data-timestamp="2895.37"> And remember, Carbonite was one of the smaller companies being sued. We don't know what they were asking from the other guys. But a lawyer named Tom Ewing says you can make an educated guess.</p><p begin="00:48:27.00" data-timestamp="2907">Ewing runs a small side business tracking and identifying Intellectual Ventures' shell companies. And he says, assuming Oasis was asking for settlements in rough proportion to the size of the company being targeted, you can put together a pretty good estimate of their total take from this lawsuit.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Ewing</h4><p begin="00:48:43.00" data-timestamp="2923">I'm guessing it would be in excess of $100 million.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:48:46.05" data-timestamp="2926.05"> $100 million. And who would have gotten that $100 million? Some of the money went to the lawyers who prepped the case and negotiated the settlements for Oasis. Some of it went to the owner of Oasis Research, a mysterious figure named Gordon Jumonville, who showed up in court every day but never testified and never returned our phone calls. But most of the profit went to Intellectual Ventures.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:49:08.45" data-timestamp="2948.45"> Remember Peter Detkin? When we spoke with him, he explained the financial arrangement with Oasis this way.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Peter Detkin</h4><p begin="00:49:14.96" data-timestamp="2954.96"> We get some percentage of the royalty stream down the road that is generated from the monetization of these assets.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:49:22.78" data-timestamp="2962.78"> And because of documents filed with the court, we now know what the percentage was-- 90. Intellectual Ventures sold Chris Crawford's patents to Oasis Research on the condition that it get 90% of the net profit. That's 90% of tens of millions of dollars.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:49:45.35" data-timestamp="2985.35"> Chris Crawford didn't make out too badly, either. Documents also say that Chris Crawford gets 17 and 1/2% of the money that Intellectual Ventures makes. Now, let's just pause to consider that $100 million for a second. That's $100 million off of one guy's patents. That is a lot of money.</p><p begin="00:50:02.04" data-timestamp="3002.04">And other people see that money. And they want in on that action. They think that's a good business-- getting a hold of patents-- using them to sue people.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:50:10.23" data-timestamp="3010.23"> Tom Ewing, the lawyer who tracks Intellectual Ventures, says that's getting easier because every year more patents are issued than the year before. According to a Yale study, it's 100 software patents a day.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tom Ewing</h4><p begin="00:50:23.22" data-timestamp="3023.22">It took 121 years for us to get the first 1 million patents. Now it takes more or less six years to get another million patents.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:50:33.30" data-timestamp="3033.3"> And the more patents are issued, the less careful the patent office seems to be. According to a study by the Organization of Economic Cooperation and Development, quote, "The rush to protect even minor improvements in products or services is overburdening patent offices. This slows the time to market for true innovations and reduces the potential for breakthrough inventions."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:50:55.43" data-timestamp="3055.43"> Case in point--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nick Desaulniers</h4><p begin="00:50:56.75" data-timestamp="3056.75"> About three years ago this time of the year, my father passed away from a heart attack.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:51:03.02" data-timestamp="3063.02"> Not long ago, I was at a meeting in San Francisco put on by the Electronic Frontier Foundation, an advocacy group that's been critical of the patent system. It was an open meeting. And this guy you hear talking, Nick Desaulniers, had decided on the spur of the moment to drop in. And after hearing a couple of other people tell their stories, he got up to tell his.</p><p begin="00:51:22.68" data-timestamp="3082.68">He's an engineer. And his father's death had spurred him to invent a low-cost heart monitor, which he believed might have saved his dad's life. He developed a prototype in a graduate school class. And he'd started dreaming a bit.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nick Desaulniers</h4><p begin="00:51:36.11" data-timestamp="3096.11"> You know, I got really excited about it. And I was thinking, maybe I can make a business out of this. My grandfather started his own business.</p><p begin="00:51:44.28" data-timestamp="3104.28">My father made his own business. I need to make my own business someday. So this was something I was really thinking, like, hey, I can make a business out of this.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:51:52.30" data-timestamp="3112.3"> But then he started a patent search to see if anyone else had come up with something resembling his idea already.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Nick Desaulniers</h4><p begin="00:51:59.18" data-timestamp="3119.18"> I was horrified at how generic some of the patents were-- "a system for remotely monitoring physiological signals." What the hell does that mean? There are so many ways, so many variables.</p><p begin="00:52:13.01" data-timestamp="3133.01">How are you getting the data from point A to point B? There are so many ways to do that. And this patent, it covers-- I guess it covers them all.</p><p begin="00:52:21.58" data-timestamp="3141.58">And seeing some of the numbers that these companies are going back and forth suing each other for, I'm terrified to create a business because of the patent system. I'm horrified. And I'm scared.</p><p begin="00:52:34.07" data-timestamp="3154.07">And I'm not going to create a business because of it. So however many jobs I could have created or however many lives I could have saved, that's it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:52:47.56" data-timestamp="3167.56"> Thanks.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:49.49" data-timestamp="3169.49"> And that is the current state of things. Something as dry as the US patent system can move somebody to tears.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:52:58.44" data-timestamp="3178.44"> Since our last broadcast, we've gotten used to front-page stories about huge tech companies in multimillion dollar battles over patents. But what hasn't been making the front page as often-- patent cases spreading to the rest of us. Over the last couple of years, there have been numerous stories about small businesses-- individuals even-- getting hit with patent infringement lawsuits.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:21.03" data-timestamp="3201.03"> A group called Innovatio has been going around suing coffee shops and grocery stores for violating its patent on providing Wi-Fi in a public space. They say they own that idea. And if your coffee shop is providing Wi-Fi, you owe them money. They say they've made a strategic and business judgment not go after people using Wi-Fi in their own homes, quote, "at this stage." Another company called Boadin Technologies, LLC, has sued a bunch of media organizations, saying that one of its patents is infringed every time a word or phrase on your computer autocompletes.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:53:51.69" data-timestamp="3231.69"> And a company in Vermont called MPHJ Technology has sent licensing letters to hundreds of businesses in Vermont, saying, any time an employee scans something to be emailed, that employee is infringing on MPHJ's patent. They say they own the idea of scanning a document and then emailing it. They're asking for around $1,000 per employee in damages. One of the companies they've approached is a nonprofit serving disabled people.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:54:23.87" data-timestamp="3263.87"> There are five bills in Congress co-sponsored by Republicans and Democrats aimed at overhauling the patent system. This is still in the very early stages. And of course, it's hard to get any bill passed ever. But this issue has attracted one thing you need to get action in Washington, DC-- powerful people in business interests who want change-- the tech industry, the supermarket industry, the Consumer Electronics Association, Google, and of course, the mighty podcast lobby.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Laura Sydell</h4><p begin="00:54:49.60" data-timestamp="3289.6"> That's one difference between now and when we first reported on this story two years ago.

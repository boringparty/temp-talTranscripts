  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.53" data-timestamp="0.53">I was in the kitchen cooking some food for my dog, which I don't say with pride. And I was listening to <i>Marketplace,</i> which is actually the point of this story I'm telling you. I was listening to the radio on <i>Marketplace</i> and I heard this commentary by one of the regular commentators, this conservative commentator, David Frum, who said that new numbers had just been released by the government. Showing that when you look at family incomes-- and what happened to family incomes during the Bush presidency.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Frum</h4><p begin="00:00:29.97" data-timestamp="29.97">George W. Bush has the worst economic performance of any two-term president since the numbers were collected. Jimmy Carter did worse, but he only had one term.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:38.91" data-timestamp="38.91">I asked Frum to come onto our show to talk about this finding.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Frum</h4><p begin="00:00:41.25" data-timestamp="41.25">This is a little painful. I was part of the Bush administration. In fact, I was part of the Bush administration's economic team. So I take this a little personally.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:53.72" data-timestamp="53.72">But Frum says, and this was his point on <i>Marketplace</i>, that it's important to understand why Bush's performance was so bad. Frum says that over those eight years what employers actually paid for each worker rose. And it rose a lot: 25%. But--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Frum</h4><p begin="00:01:09.33" data-timestamp="69.33">Employees received none of that money. The median American worker was earning less in the year 2007 than that worker was earning adjusting for inflation in the 2000. And yet, his employer was paying 25% more. So where did that money go? And the answer is, it all went to pay the rising cost of health care.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:28.00" data-timestamp="88">The average cost of a health insurance policy for a family of four between 2000 and 2007 more than doubled, from $6,000 to $13,000. A $7,000 climb. Coincidentally, during the boom years of President Clinton, when Americans did so well, family incomes rose about that same amount, $6,500. This is just how quickly health care costs are rising in this country.</p><p begin="00:01:50.99" data-timestamp="110.99">If you take into account health care, George W. Bush stops being the worst two-term president in the modern America era for family incomes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Frum</h4><p begin="00:01:58.27" data-timestamp="118.27">If you were to compare, say, President Bush's performance to Harry Truman's or the Ford-Carter years, it looks good. If you compare it to the Clinton years, the Reagan years, or the Johnson years, it doesn't look so good.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:09.39" data-timestamp="129.39">So basically he's somewhere in the middle? He's kind of like--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Frum</h4><p begin="00:02:11.87" data-timestamp="131.87">No, I would say the lower part of the middle. I don't want to oversell the record.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:20.79" data-timestamp="140.79">Rising health care costs are affecting everything in the economy. Rising health care costs are the thing that's driving our health care system off the rails. It's what's led so many people to being uninsured. It's making it hard for US businesses to compete. It's gobbling up more and more of all spending in the United States.</p><p begin="00:02:36.16" data-timestamp="156.16">I saw this number recently that said that at the current rate of growth, in just 9 years, the amount that the average American family is going to spend on health care each year is going to be $38,000. $38,000. That's more than half of what family income will be that year. What was striking about that was that this isn't one of those projections that the government does looking at health care costs 75 years from now, showing how grim it's going to be far in the future. This is nine years from now at the current rate of growth. Basically, this is going to happen. Half of our incomes are going to be going to health care very, very soon, unless somebody does something.</p><p begin="00:03:12.29" data-timestamp="192.29">At this point, we, Americans, spend 50% more on health care than any country in the world, and the money isn't buying us better health. If you look at the numbers, when it comes to infant mortality compared to other countries, we are 45th. Worse than Cuba. When it comes to life expectancy, we're 50th. Behind, by the way, Bosnia and South Korea. And so today we ask the question, why is this happening? What's going on in health care that makes it so impossible to hold costs down?</p><p begin="00:03:40.75" data-timestamp="220.75">We're tackling these questions now, this week, as health care is being debated in Washington. Because fixing our health care system means fixing two different things: getting more people covered by insurance and slowing the increases in health care costs. And the bills on Capitol Hill right now seem much more about solving the insurance problem than about controlling costs.</p><p begin="00:04:02.20" data-timestamp="242.2">So we're going to do something we've never done. We're going to two shows about this, this week and next week. This is a co-production we're doing with <i>NPR</i> <i>News</i>. Next week, the most entertaining hour, I guarantee, that you're ever going to hear on the insurance industry. This week, we ask why costs keep rising.</p><p begin="00:04:19.06" data-timestamp="259.06">From WBEZ Chicago it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> distributed by Public Radio International, I'm Ira Glass. Our show today in four acts. Act one, Dartmouth Atlas Shrugged, in which we examine whether doctors are the ones to blame for rising costs. Act two, Every Cat Scan has Nine Lives, in which we wonder if patients are the problem. Act three, Who Would Win in a Fight Between a Polar Bear and an Insurance Company? Is the problem the insurance companies? And act four, Now What? In which we learn what the new health care bills do to fix all this, if they do anything. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Dartmouth Atlas Shrugged</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:08.68" data-timestamp="308.68">Act one: Dartmouth Atlas Shrugged.</p><p begin="00:05:12.30" data-timestamp="312.3">There are all kinds of reasons that health care costs have risen so quickly over the last few decades. We have higher administrative costs than other countries because our health care system with its insurers and providers is so complicated. We use more expensive high tech gear, our drugs cost more. But there's a whole school of thought that blames a lot of the rising costs on doctors. Prescribing drugs that people don't really need, doing too many procedures. And the reason people think this all goes back to the work of one person, a health researcher named Jack Wennberg. NPR science correspondent, Alix Spiegel, explains what that's all about.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:05:45.94" data-timestamp="345.94">To understand the work of Jack Wennberg, let's start with one patient and one medical problem, a young woman with a sharp pain in her lower uterus.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne Tremblay</h4><p begin="00:05:55.34" data-timestamp="355.34">I was having a lot of problem, just a constant ache right here that never went away. And my boss kept saying, you should go get that checked. You should go get that checked. Because I was like doubling right over. It was like a really, really deep ache all the time.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:06:13.45" data-timestamp="373.45">In 1974, Roxanne Tremblay was 29 years old. A single mom living in a small apartment in Lewiston, Maine. Because Roxanne was the only one supporting her young daughter, she had to be able to work, and the pain was getting in the way. The pain was a problem. So Roxanne went to see her gynecologist, a nice man who did a short exam, and then kindly explained that Roxanne would need an operation. Three weeks later, Tremblay had her uterus and ovaries removed, a total hysterectomy.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne Tremblay</h4><p begin="00:06:41.03" data-timestamp="401.03">It was what he called the seed of cancer. It wasn't cancer, but it had the potential of developing into it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:06:52.74" data-timestamp="412.74">Now Roxanne didn't mind losing her uterus. She never planned to get married or have kids again. But she was slightly surprised that her doctor had been so quick, and before surgery hadn't taken any x-rays or anything.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne Tremblay</h4><p begin="00:07:04.43" data-timestamp="424.43">No pictures. No.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:07:06.93" data-timestamp="426.93">He just felt you were--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne Tremblay</h4><p begin="00:07:09.60" data-timestamp="429.6">And based on what I told him that it should come out.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:07:15.43" data-timestamp="435.43">29 years old is pretty young for a hysterectomy. But in the mid '70s in Lewiston, Maine, lots of women were getting them. Roxanne, herself, knew a bunch.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne Tremblay</h4><p begin="00:07:24.43" data-timestamp="444.43">My boss that I had when I worked at Kmart, she had one shortly after I did. One of my friends that I've had since I was six years old, she lives right five minutes from here, she had one. Just about anybody you talked to would say, oh, I had a hysterectomy. Oh, yeah. So I did. So and so did mine. Our I just remember there was a lot of them. I do remember that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:07:52.82" data-timestamp="472.82">Now most Lewiston women, including Roxanne, weren't suspicious about the hysterectomies. And this is where Jack Wennberg's research comes in. Until Jack Wennberg made Lewiston and its hysterectomies famous, no one had even noticed.</p><p begin="00:08:06.61" data-timestamp="486.61">But in the late '70s, Wennberg published a paper, &quot;A Study of Health Care in Maine.&quot; And in it he showed that an unusually large number of women in Lewiston were having their uterus removed. Democrats and republicans are arguing over who's going to get insurance, and public option, and how it's paid for. Not to mention death panels and abortion. There's really not much discussion about how to fix the health care system as a whole to slow the runaway costs that threaten our entire economy. The stuff we've been talking about all this hour.</p><p begin="00:53:18.24" data-timestamp="3198.24">And so to end our hour, we invited somebody who's been following this very closely in Washington, DC, Susan Dentzer. She's been reporting on the politics and economics of health care since the 1980s for <i>Newsweek</i>, and then later for <i>US News</i> <i>&amp;</i> <i>World</i> <i>Report.</i> She's seen HMOs come and go, she's seen Hillary Clinton's health care plan come and go, and now she's monitoring the latest attempt to remake the health care system as the editor of a policy journal called <i>Health</i> <i>Affairs</i>.</p><p begin="00:53:42.67" data-timestamp="3222.67">And I'll be frank, when I sat down with her to talk about what is actually in the bills, she totally blew my mind. We'll get that part in a minute, but first, the actual detail of the bills when it comes to cutting costs. There is some stuff in these bills that addresses rising costs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:53:58.45" data-timestamp="3238.45">If you are a glass half full kind of person, you look at it this way. There are some measures in the bills that would give the secretary of health and human services, among others, enormous authority to experiment with new ways to deliver health care.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:15.96" data-timestamp="3255.96">This doesn't mean that little, experimental projects Dentzer says. The head of HHS will have czar like powers to change very basic things in how the system works by changing how Medicare pays doctors and hospitals.</p><p begin="00:54:28.58" data-timestamp="3268.58">And so for example, Medicare could stop paying fee for service. That system where doctors get money for every procedure that they do. They could bundle doctors and patients into groups to cut inefficiency and waste.</p><p begin="00:54:41.28" data-timestamp="3281.28">The bills also set up a Medicare commission, which could do the kind of restructuring and cutting that would be too hot politically for Congress to do itself.</p><p begin="00:54:49.08" data-timestamp="3289.08">Now the weaknesses of these bills, Dentzer says, is that they don't force the head of HHS or anybody in the government to make any changes at all. There are no deadlines. And the amount that's going to get saved if everything goes perfectly, according to the Congressional Budget Office, is much smaller than health care costs are going to rise. But the idea is that Medicare is such a huge part of the health care system, it pays for a fourth of all the health care in the country. It's the biggest insurer in America. Changes made in Medicare would be adopted by others.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:55:19.04" data-timestamp="3319.04">So the notion is to really create a platform for gradual change.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:55:23.75" data-timestamp="3323.75">And is part of the impulse behind this basically to say, we are not going to agree in the Senate and the House on the exact ways to cut costs and exactly which things to do? And so let's just agree, somebody's going to do that. It's going to be the head of the Department of Health and Human Services and some of these other things that you're saying. But let's not discuss that here.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:55:43.82" data-timestamp="3343.82">Yes, that's part of it. And the other realistic piece of this is if you tried to build all of this into the bill right now. If you said, we're going to make sweeping changes in the system. We're going to completely pay you in a totally different way. We're going to take you doctors who've been out in the hinterlands collecting your fee for service monies and basically restructure the system in such a way that you might have to live your life really differently. Just try putting that into a bill that gets everybody to accept it. You can't.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:56:17.31" data-timestamp="3377.31">Susan Dentzer told me that there are people in the administration who are deeply involved in the details of how to cut health care costs, and she talks to them. But she says they don't talk about this stuff much publicly for similar reasons. It's a political loser. It's a tough sell to say to people, not only will you get less health care, you'll be better off.</p><p begin="00:56:35.78" data-timestamp="3395.78">OK, now I'm going to tell you the thing that Susan Dentzer says that totally shocked me. She told me that for the first time since she's been covering health care decades ago, all the major players in health care-- the hospitals, the insurance companies, the doctors-- she says they are all for the first time agreeing that something has to change. And she says this is the biggest political achievement made in health reform so far.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:56:58.16" data-timestamp="3418.16">Almost all the stakeholders in health care have been at the table and are talking about all of this. They know that the system cannot persist the way it is. That we've got to do things differently. We have got to get more value out of the dollars spent on health care.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:16.04" data-timestamp="3436.04">How recent is that agreement? Is that because of Obama and his people like saying, OK, here's what we're going to do?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:57:22.87" data-timestamp="3442.87">Not really. We would probably be at this juncture no matter who had been elected president. Because of what was happening to the cost over the course of this decade.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:33.76" data-timestamp="3453.76">Right, there's this growing consensus, like it's going up so fast.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:57:36.27" data-timestamp="3456.27">Well, health insurance premiums went up 130% from the year 2000 to now. Two years ago I was talking to an insurance executive from one of the leading insurance companies and I said, OK, so what's the situation like, and draw me the analogy with the threat alert system. You know, red alert, orange, green, whatever. He said, we're on red alert. We're on red alert. The system is falling apart.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:58:02.69" data-timestamp="3482.69">The insurance companies?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:58:03.21" data-timestamp="3483.21">Yes.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:58:03.93" data-timestamp="3483.93">I'm just surprised to hear the insurance companies are on red alert because they are still making nice profits.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Dentzer</h4><p begin="00:58:09.25" data-timestamp="3489.25">But they can see the handwriting on the wall. They see the system crumbling. They understand the pressures that will ensue if things get really out of hand. Just imagine if we had had 100 million people uninsured, what kind of pressures there would have been for, say, a single payer system. They know that. They think the only solution now for preserving any part of the system as a private system is to stop the bleeding now.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:58:41.28" data-timestamp="3521.28">And so in addition to the health care reform happening in Washington, Susan Dentzer says there's a second reform that's happening right now all over the country. Hospitals and health care providers and state legislatures coming up with their own ways to restructure and contain costs. That's what gives her hope. That things are so hopeless for every player across the board that a consensus is actually taking hold that things can't continue as they have. And she sees people and organizations taking action.</p></div>  </div>

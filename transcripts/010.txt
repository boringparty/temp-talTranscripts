  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.50" data-timestamp="1.5">There's what we wish for and there's what we get. For Susan Bergman, the story went like this. Her father led a double life. On the one hand, he proudly described himself as a family man, a church organist, in a denomination that was so strict the women covered their heads, wore no make-up, no dancing, no smoking, no drinking, no going to theaters, no swimming with members of the opposite sex, even. Her father stage-managed things so they appeared to be the perfect blond-haired, blue-eyed, American family. But secretly, he was having sex with men. By all accounts, it was lots of men. Sometimes he would even fly off to New York, go to gay clubs there.</p><p begin="00:01:02.36" data-timestamp="62.36">In 1983, he was one of the first victims of AIDS. They had barely named the disease. The symptoms weren't familiar. And he died before his children got the chance to ask him about who he really was and talk to him about how they should reconcile who he had pretended to be all those years, with who he was. What was real of their childhood?</p><p begin="00:01:41.08" data-timestamp="101.08">Susan Bergman wrote a book about her family's experience. And on her book tour, a very particular thing started to happen. Gay men, who were still married or who had been married, started to contact her. They wanted to explain her father's double life to her. They wanted to explain their own double lives to her. And they wanted to offer her the conversation that she never got to have with her father.</p><p begin="00:02:18.81" data-timestamp="138.81">Well, from WBEZ in Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Back once again for another hour documenting life in these United States. Today's program in four acts. Act One, gay men. I talk to Susan Bergman about her father. Act Two, a gay man talks to me in a parked car in an undisclosed location about why he thinks that his children don't need to know that he's gay and why he stays married. Act Three, we take all the heavy, very heavy themes about lying and families from the first two acts and rework them as comedy, if you can believe that. And finally, Act Four, the sins of the fathers pass on to the sons. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: The Book Tour</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:30.65" data-timestamp="210.65">Act One, The Book Tour. Susan Bergman came to our studios, loaded down with evidence.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:38.96" data-timestamp="218.96">OK, now you've brought in some tapes of yourself being interviewed on talk radio shows that I've got here. So this first one, do we need to explain it at all before we play it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:03:52.64" data-timestamp="232.64">I think this was a caller that was, in some ways, typical of the response that I got on talk radio.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:01.33" data-timestamp="241.33">Here we go.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:04:02.75" data-timestamp="242.75">I have a particularly close connection to this sort of thing. I'm a gay father, divorced now. So I'm very close to this. In particular, I can understand your father's secretiveness and his pain. There's always the resentment that you had to go through the pain, that you had to hide, that you had to lie. And you live with that resentment. It really never quite goes away, even in my case, after you've come completely out and divorced and the whole bit. And the resentment lives on, that I had to do live that lie, that I had to lie to my wife, that I had to lie to my children, that I had to life to my family. And never be happy. Never, never, never know happiness.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:53.85" data-timestamp="293.85">Hey, Susan?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:04:54.62" data-timestamp="294.62">Uh-huh.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:55.47" data-timestamp="295.47">Can I just ask you, what was your reaction to-- when he was saying this, had it occurred to you before that your father would actually be resentful of the family for the secret that he was keeping from you?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:05:10.56" data-timestamp="310.56">He was angry at us a lot. But it seemed like it was more-- I mean I interpreted that as we were bothering Dad, or Mom was asking too many questions. Yeah. It's not one that had occurred to me. And I would have to contest that assumption that one has to lie or one is put in a situation where lying is the only appropriate gesture or response.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:42.71" data-timestamp="342.71">Let's go back to this tape.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:05:44.23" data-timestamp="344.23">And I hope that-- I don't know. Is there anything that I can help you. Any questions that I could answer. Because I'm still alive and I've been through it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:05:54.50" data-timestamp="354.5">That's great. Boy, I hope I can do this without just kind of gushing all over you, because it's so great of you to call in and offer that. It's beautiful. I didn't get that chance with my father. I guess I would ask you--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:09.55" data-timestamp="369.55">I'm just stopping the tape here. I'm just going to stop the tape here, because as you're listening, you're making the gagging signal at your own reaction.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:06:18.61" data-timestamp="378.61">Oh, I think that's so great. Oh, I think that's so wonderful. I was not in touch with the full range of the reaction until after I hung up the telephone and stopped the radio interview. And clearly, that man was hurting in a lot of ways. And the last thing I needed to do was have a fight with him on the air about being a liar, or whatever. Whatever anger I would have had towards my father didn't seem to me to apply to him. He was just some innocent bystander. So I just tried to be nice. And it was kind-- oh yeah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:04.99" data-timestamp="424.99">There's the thing that you wish for and there's the thing that you get. And if what you really need is a long talk with your father, the kind of talk where you get mad and argue, and maybe he gets mad, and maybe people admit mistakes, and maybe things get resolved or maybe they don't get resolved, and you learn that they won't get resolved. But if that conversation is what you need, then no stranger on a radio call-in show, however well meaning, is going to give that to you. It's a mockery of what you need.</p><p begin="00:07:57.03" data-timestamp="477.03">But there you are. There you are. You're talking to this man, this stranger. And you find yourself asking a version of the question that you would most like to ask your own father. And you get an answer that is totally useless, because really it has nothing to do with you.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:08:16.40" data-timestamp="496.4">You said, you didn't want to share your quote, &quot;real life,&quot; maybe. But why do you feel that you were forced to lie about that? How could your family have shared your life?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:08:30.44" data-timestamp="510.44">You see the problem is that once you get trapped into doing the thing you think you're supposed to do at a young age-- I married at 23 because I thought it was-- I knew I was gay then already.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:08:39.76" data-timestamp="519.76">Hm.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:08:40.01" data-timestamp="520.01">So did my wife, for that matter. She put it aside.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:08:51.68" data-timestamp="531.68">Gay men sent Susan Bergman photos of themselves. One happy couple stands there in one of the photos in matching sweaters, raising glasses, smiling. The letter said that if her father had lived and divorced her mother, this is the kind of happiness he could have had. The letters that Susan Bergman got from the children and spouses of gay fathers, she said, seemed to be written in a completely different language than the letters she got from the gay fathers. It was like two different cultures, two different perspectives.</p><p begin="00:09:34.94" data-timestamp="574.94">There are lots of books about men coming out, gay men coming out, gay women coming out. But nobody seemed to be able to remember a book that had been told from the point of view of the children and the wives that these gay men had left behind. And families who are in that situation felt this shock of we are not alone. And they contacted her.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:10:02.21" data-timestamp="602.21">You brought examples of letters?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Bergman</h4><p begin="00:10:05.12" data-timestamp="605.12">Yeah. The most poignant letter that I got, this woman says she read the book. &quot;Our lives are so similar, Susan, that it was eerie for me to read.&quot; She talks about their background. They were all so fundamentalist. Our family wasn't quite fundamentalist. But this woman says, &quot;Bible believing churches that started in their living room. We had six well-behaved, talented, athletic children in our prosperous and highly visible family.&quot;</p><p begin="00:10:37.75" data-timestamp="637.75">&quot;When my dad was diagnosed in 1988,&quot; she writes, &quot;my mom kicked him out of the house and would have nothing else to do with him. Three of us moved him out of town, encouraged him to change his name, and lied about his mysterious disease and our parent's sudden separation. Now, they are both gone. And we are left to deal with the fraud that was our life.&quot;</p><p begin="00:10:59.77" data-timestamp="659.77">They had just buried their mother-- when she wrote me this letter-- who had died of AIDS because the father didn't protect the mother at all. She says, &quot;I'm writing to you now, not to pour out my heart, but to ask if my sisters and I can come take you out for lunch soon there in Chicago? But I had yet to go to the man I had wronged, and whom I desperately wanted to love me, to cancel my ongoing lie, my maintaining the family pattern of dishonesty.</p><p begin="00:53:28.70" data-timestamp="3208.7">My husband couldn't bear ever to look at me again. Hadn't my family put him through enough already? Wasn't I a hard pill he had to swallow and swallow? He would remarry within the year, a woman of fidelity and beauty and a way with children, my children. What kinds of stories would they be told about me? All that we had made together-- I took inventory-- did not add up to the ultimate banishment of the untruthful from the presence of God.</p><p begin="00:54:03.53" data-timestamp="3243.53">If you give me the opportunity Lord, one more time, whatever the consequence, I prayed, I will tell the truth. Judson put the question simply, the same one as before. That morning, I had sat in the sun, and read, and drunk my coffee, uninterrupted. Maybe it was a morning unlike any my father had ever been given. When Judson came home later that morning, is when he asked, &quot;Is there something you'd like to tell me?&quot; There was a reason my husband was unable to retire his doubts. I had made an offer of honesty, yes, but hadn't thought the test would come so soon.</p><p begin="00:54:48.40" data-timestamp="3288.4">Walking out on the plank of my own promise, I peered down at the water. First, you leave your father's house, and then your own. There was a deep gulf below me I could not see into. This was the last of my life, as I knew it. &quot;Whatever the consequence,&quot; I said inside my head to remind myself, breathing once. He could tell in the stillness of the pause between his question and my looking back up at him, that his life was changing too.</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.64" data-timestamp="0.64">The argument is simple. Why have an eagle as your national bird? The eagle is a bird of bad moral character. He does not get his living honestly. He sits on the tree branch, watches other birds swoop down for fish. Once they grab the fish, the eagle chases them, steals the fish. Also, he's a coward. At least that is the argument that Benjamin Franklin lays out in a letter dated January 26, 1784.</p><p begin="00:00:26.27" data-timestamp="26.27">"It would be far better if the symbol of the new United States of America were a turkey," Franklin writes. Quote, "The turkey is, in comparison, a much more respectable, and withal a true, original native of America. Eagles have been found in all countries, but turkey was peculiar to ours. He is besides, though a little vain and silly, a bird of courage and would not hesitate to attack a grenadier of British guards who should presume to invade his farm yard with a red coat on."</p><p begin="00:00:56.19" data-timestamp="56.19">A modern American reads this and thinks, bird of courage?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:01:00.21" data-timestamp="60.21">Well the wild turkey of the 18th century was very different from the butterball turkey of Thanksgiving day dinner.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:07.24" data-timestamp="67.24">That's Jack Hitt. He is one of contributing editors here at <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. He has written about the Benjamin Franklin papers in <i>The New York Times.</i> He writes about history, biology and pretty much anything else he wants to in various national magazines. And he offered to explain this turkey mystery.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:01:22.10" data-timestamp="82.1">If you look at the label of a Wild Turkey bottle, you're probably closer to Ben Franklin's turkey than the one in the grocery store. It's all leg. It's fast. It's speedy. It's an athletic bird. It has almost no breast whatsoever. And if you've hunted them, they're incredibly quick and smart, whereas over the centuries, the turkey was eventually bred into this enormous walking piece of breast meat.</p><p begin="00:01:59.35" data-timestamp="119.35">The turkey itself is so front-heavy-- I think in some cases, they even have trouble walking towards the end.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:06.51" data-timestamp="126.51">You're saying that we as a nation have altered the turkey from a smart, fast cunning animal into a stupid, heavy one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:02:15.00" data-timestamp="135">What we've done really is hybridize it to the point where it's just basically a living dinner waiting to get on the table. There's really nothing about the turkey from its birth until its death that is about anything except being a meal.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:35.41" data-timestamp="155.41">And is the thing about poultry, that probably more than any other animal, we've turned birds, chicken, turkey, into what we want them to be. Which means that chickens and turkeys, more than other animals, are a mirror of ourselves, of our desires, of our needs. When we tell stories about poultry, we're telling stories about ourselves. And given this, and given the history of the turkey in America, maybe Benjamin Franklin was right. Maybe it should be the symbol of our nation.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:06.13" data-timestamp="186.13">In a way it begs the metaphor. I mean he's saying that this should be the symbol of our country. And I wonder if maybe it should, depending on what you think of the United States of America.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:03:17.51" data-timestamp="197.51">You're saying that if we had chosen it 200 years ago, it would have neatly tracked our national growth. Are you saying that now, we're more like the contemporary turkey than we were like Ben Franklin's turkey?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:31.42" data-timestamp="211.42">I guess I am saying that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:03:33.89" data-timestamp="213.89">That we're fat and out of shape and lazy and somewhat retarded?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:38.94" data-timestamp="218.94">I mean I admit, it's a little post-Vietnam syndrome. I'm going to suggest this. A little unfashionably, post-Vietnam syndrome.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:03:52.74" data-timestamp="232.74">Well, from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International. It is <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. And we, all of us, we stand at this moment poised between Thanksgiving and Christmas. We are in the weeks during the year when Americans consume more poultry than at any other time, which means it's time for <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life's</i> annual Poultry Slam. Stories about chickens, turkeys, ducks, fowl all kinds, and their mysterious hold over us.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:23.79" data-timestamp="263.79">Now Jack, although you're happily, and at our bidding, discussing Ben Franklin and turkeys, that is not the reason why you're here with us today, is it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:04:32.08" data-timestamp="272.08">No, actually I'm here for another poultry-related subject.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:34.51" data-timestamp="274.51">Let me just ask you to just give us a little hint. Just drop a phrase or two.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:04:39.93" data-timestamp="279.93">Two words Ira, Chicken Little.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:42.90" data-timestamp="282.9">Chicken Little. Adults all over America are turning off their radios. You think chicken little is that much of a draw?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:04:53.64" data-timestamp="293.64">Let me try that again. Two words Ira. Avian supernumeraries.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:04.00" data-timestamp="304">Well, Act One of our program today, Chicken Diva, in which we hear the story of yes, avian supernumeraries.</p><p begin="00:05:12.43" data-timestamp="312.43">Act Two, Headless Chicken in Topless Bar. Actually, there is no topless bar in that act. We just like the sound of that. But there is a headless chicken and a question. What does the headless chicken say about us?</p><p begin="00:05:25.89" data-timestamp="325.89">Act Three, Duck Warrior. Michael Lewis explains the natural way to hunt duck and his family's way.</p><p begin="00:05:32.06" data-timestamp="332.06">Act Four, Trying to Respect the Chicken, the story of one woman's quest to give chicken the honor and dignity they are rarely accorded, even though the chickens resist her efforts. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Chicken Diva</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:51.12" data-timestamp="351.12">Act One, Chicken Diva. Chickens are what we make of them. For further evidence of this, we have this story from our contributing editor, Jack Hitt.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:06:02.02" data-timestamp="362.02">Oddly enough, it wasn't Susan who was obsessed with chickens. It was Kenny, a pal who worked backstage at the 92 Street Y in New York. His house was filled with chicken cups, chicken masks, porcelain chickens. He got the whole staff onto chickens, including Susan. For a time there in the '80's, poultry-related jokes and references became the fast way to get a laugh at the Y. I guess most of us are condemned to see nothing more than the easy comedy of chickens, but Susan Vitucci saw something else, their potential greatness, their hidden beauty, their grandeur.</p><p begin="00:06:37.19" data-timestamp="397.19">One day, she glued together some finger puppets for a 10-minute rendition of the Chicken Little story for her nephew. That was 14 years ago. Today, it is a full-length opera, enjoyed by a cult following whenever it goes up in a workshop or cafe or small theater. It's still performed with finger puppets, but now it has a complete score written by a noted composer, Henry Krieger, who did <i>Dreamgirls,</i> and this season has a hit on Broadway, a musical called <i>Side Show.</i> The Chicken Little opera he wrote with Susan Vitucci is called <i>Love's</i> <i>Fowl</i>. Needless to say, that's F-O-W-L.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Henry Krieger</h4><p begin="00:07:12.53" data-timestamp="432.53">Well, we were going to start with the opening, [SPEAKING ITALIAN] We are the Clothespin Repertory Theater. And we have a special singing guest for you, which I don't know--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:07:25.56" data-timestamp="445.56">Susan and I are sitting at Henry's baby grand piano. Henry's guest is his Maltese terrier named Toby.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Henry Krieger</h4><p begin="00:07:31.99" data-timestamp="451.99">Perhaps Toby would be kind enough to join me over here.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:07:34.60" data-timestamp="454.6">Yeah, would she sit on your lap for this?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Henry Krieger</h4><p begin="00:07:36.38" data-timestamp="456.38">Yeah, let's see what we can do.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jack Hitt</h4><p begin="00:07:39.69" data-timestamp="459.69">OK, listen carefully because once Toby gets going, he actually harmonizes with Henry and Susan.</p><p begin="00:07:45.78" data-timestamp="465.78">[MUSIC - LOVE'S FOWL] [DOG BARKING]</p><p begin="00:08:07.93" data-timestamp="487.93">You may have noticed that this libretto is an Italian, just like a real opera.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Vitucci</h4><p begin="00:08:12.08" data-timestamp="492.08">Before, it was just a bunch of puppets and a box, with a good idea.

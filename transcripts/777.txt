    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.05" data-timestamp="1.05"> Denise Clark is a retired schoolteacher who still works as a substitute. When COVID hit and her high school switched to remote learning, it was hard for her for all the normal reasons. But also, like lots of older people, she wasn't so great on computers. Ditto for her friend, Pearl, who was another substitute at that school, who Denise would do crossword puzzles with in the faculty lounge.</p><p begin="00:00:22.69" data-timestamp="22.69">Pearl, also not so great on the computers. And Pearl was a decade older than Denise, in her mid-70s.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Denise Clark</h4><p begin="00:00:28.87" data-timestamp="28.87"> She always told me, can you help me? It took a while for me to learn it, to tell you the truth.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:39.93" data-timestamp="39.93"> You know, you think, if you're 75, and your job completely changes, you might call it quits. But Pearl was adamant. She was going to learn to work on the computer. And when she got stuck, she wasn't shy to call in help from the IT guy, or the assistant principal, or really, whoever she could find.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Denise Clark</h4><p begin="00:00:54.75" data-timestamp="54.75"> I had to tip my hat to her if I had one. Because she would keep trying. That's the thing I like about her. She'd ask questions. She was doing better than some of us. [LAUGHS]</p><p begin="00:01:05.22" data-timestamp="65.22">And Pearl, she kept up with it. You know, she didn't know sometimes-- she had some difficulties.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:09.99" data-timestamp="69.99"> What kind of difficulties was she having?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Denise Clark</h4><p begin="00:01:12.21" data-timestamp="72.21"> Well, we all had difficulties. Pearl's daughter, Pam, in fact, told me that she watched, fascinated that her 70-something-year-old mom was on the computer all the time.</p><p begin="00:01:31.95" data-timestamp="91.95">Over a year before this, she'd bought Pearl a laptop that had mostly gone unused. When something like the Buffalo shooting happens, each of the people killed gets summarized afterwards in a sentence. Pearl was Pearl Young, the 77-year-old grandmother who taught Sunday school and ran a food pantry at the church for decades.</p><p begin="00:03:29.56" data-timestamp="209.56">That leaves so much out about how she lived-- big things, like special connections she had with teenagers, how they'd bond with her, and could talk to her about what was really going on with them; little things, like how her world changed when she learned the computer and started mildly trolling her grown children on Facebook. But the guy was wearing body armor, and killed Aaron Salter.</p><p begin="00:04:10.76" data-timestamp="250.76">He was called a hero. There were photos of him in his uniform. But there were other sides to him. In his spare time, Salter would invent things. He was a tinkerer-- rebuilt a '67 Cadillac.</p><p begin="00:04:23.12" data-timestamp="263.12">In 2011, when gas prices rose to over $4 a gallon, he went online and bought plans to refit a car engine so it would run on water by splitting the water into hydrogen and oxygen, and using that hydrogen as fuel. I didn't-- you know, I didn't believe that it would work. But I just wanted to see.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:48.98" data-timestamp="288.98"> He says the plans that he bought online, they weren't any good. He's calm, methodical.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Aaron Salter Jr.</h4><p begin="00:06:02.83" data-timestamp="362.83"> Got to get this water.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:04.14" data-timestamp="364.14"> His son, Aaron III, told a reporter after he died that the last time he saw his dad, they went riding motorcycles. And when his motorcycle broke down, and his dad got into the engine to try to fix it, it was the most Aaron Salter Junior-y thing his dad could have done. Just seeing him in action, he said, doing stuff like that, is what I'm going to miss about him.</p><p begin="00:06:21.96" data-timestamp="381.96">[MUSIC - CURTIS LOVELL]</p><p begin="00:06:25.20" data-timestamp="385.2"></p><p begin="00:06:29.28" data-timestamp="389.28">Well, today on our program, this has been a year of so many mass shootings, really horrific ones, that it feels hard to absorb the names and pictures of each one before the next one happens. Grady learned that that person was, of course, the same person who came back the next day and allegedly shot and killed 10 people in front of him, a young white man who traveled to one of the most segregated cities in America determined to kill Black people.</p><p begin="01:11:58.93" data-timestamp="4318.93">And Grady, the man in front of the store, is still watching. But he sees things different now.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Grady Lewis</h4><p begin="01:12:06.46" data-timestamp="4326.46"> I was at the memorial. And a lady and her husband and her young son-- 18, 19, 20-- I'm talking to them, no problem. I looked at him, and I was scared of him. And I'd seen another young white kid, boy. I was scared of him.</p><p begin="01:12:30.42" data-timestamp="4350.42">So I have this thing now to where I'm kind of scared of young white boys now. Probably-- well, probably-- definitely from the shooting, for me talking to this kid, and him seeming to be like a regular guy. And I don't even think-- I went to the Tops on Elmwood, this all-white Tops. And then-- it's just-- it's me. I felt like every white person was looking at me.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Michael Harriot</h4><p begin="01:12:58.59" data-timestamp="4378.59"> Before the shooting, Grady's can-collecting trek took him to neighborhoods all over Buffalo.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Grady Lewis</h4><p begin="01:13:03.81" data-timestamp="4383.81"> Even when-- before this, I would always would be cautious. But I kind of had a break because I was an old Black guy pushing a shopping cart. Maybe nobody would bother him. Now, I mean, they're shooting old people in supermarkets. So yeah, so I don't feel safe in going in no neighborhoods anymore.</p><p begin="01:13:25.70" data-timestamp="4405.7">So I haven't been able to collect my bottles and cans anymore. Because like I said, I would go on the West Side and North Buffalo. And those are predominantly white neighborhoods. And I would do it at 12:00 at night, 12:00, 1:00, 2:00, 3:00, 4:00, 5:00. I can't take no chance and do that at all.</p><p begin="01:13:44.42" data-timestamp="4424.42">And it's kind of a shame because-- well, it ain't kind of a shame, it is a shame because actually, I love collecting bottles and cans. It made me feel like I had a purpose, doing something good for the environment, and also making money, and getting exercise in. I walked 23 miles, made my own time.</p><p begin="01:14:05.54" data-timestamp="4445.54">You know, if I wanted to work, I can work. If I want to work later, I can work later. If I wanted, could go to work earlier. So I felt free in that sense.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Michael Harriot</h4><p begin="01:14:13.80" data-timestamp="4453.8"> If the world made sense, Grady would still feel free. If the world made sense, Deacon Patterson's life could serve as a parable about love, charity, or the redemptive quality of grace. But there is a danger in manufacturing meaning out of something so meaningless. There is no way to rationalize the hate that left a huge, gaping hole in an entire community.</p><p begin="01:14:36.45" data-timestamp="4476.45">Then again, if any of this made sense, there'd be no such thing as Black neighborhoods, or poverty, or food deserts, or racial disparities, or senseless violence. If the world made sense, Deacon Heyward Patterson would still be a man in front of a store.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="01:15:00.45" data-timestamp="4500.45"> Michael Harriot writes for <i>TheGrio.</i> And he's the host of <i>TheGrio</i> <i>Daily</i> podcast.</p></div>  </div>

  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.00" data-timestamp="0"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. OK, so for months around here at our radio show, we have all been preparing for this day today. The date of this day has been written on our white board in big letters for months, because this is the day that we launch our first real spin-off. As of today, we're not making just one weekly show here, but two shows.</p><p begin="00:00:23.17" data-timestamp="23.17">Our second show is called <i>Serial</i>. That's not like breakfast, but the kind of serial where one thing follows another. And I've mentioned this show on the air before. But just in case you missed that, here's the premise of the new show. Instead of each episode bringing you a different theme and different stories, every episode of <i>Serial</i> brings you back to the exact same story and tells you the next chapter in that story.</p><p begin="00:00:44.86" data-timestamp="44.86">This is a long story told over a dozen episodes, a true story. One of our producers and regular contributors, Sarah Koenig, is going to be hosting the new series. And the first story <i>Serial</i> is taking on is about a murder.</p><p begin="00:00:59.27" data-timestamp="59.27">It's a case where what really happened is actually much more complicated than the jury ever heard when this thing went to trial. And each week we will go with Sarah on her hunt to figure out what really happened. And we will learn the answers as she does.</p><p begin="00:01:13.21" data-timestamp="73.21">Our new show, by the way, is not a radio show. It's a podcast. I'll explain more about that later in the hour. And what we're going to do today here on the radio is play you the first episode. OK, and so with that, here's Sarah. And I really hope you like this.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="credits">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:01:28.01" data-timestamp="88.01">For the last year, I've spent every working day trying to figure out where a high school kid was for an hour after school one day in 1999-- or if you want to get technical about it, and apparently I do, where a high school kid was for 21 minutes after school one day in 1999. This search sometimes feels undignified on my part. I've had to ask about teenagers' sex lives, where, how often, with whom, about notes they passed in class, about their drug habits, their relationships with their parents.</p><p begin="00:01:55.72" data-timestamp="115.72">And I'm not a detective or a private investigator. I've not even a crime reporter. But, yes, every day this year, I've tried to figure out the alibi of a 17-year-old boy. Before I get into why I've been doing this, I just want to point out something I'd never really thought about before I started working on this story. And that is, it's really hard to account for your time, in a detailed way, I mean.</p><p begin="00:02:16.71" data-timestamp="136.71">How'd you get to work last Wednesday, for instance? Drive? Walk? Bike? Was it raining? Are you sure?</p><p begin="00:02:23.35" data-timestamp="143.35">Did you go to any stores that day? If so, what did you buy? Who did you talk to? The entire day, name every person you talked to. It's hard.</p><p begin="00:02:32.72" data-timestamp="152.72">Now imagine you have to account for a day that happened six weeks back. Because that's the situation in the story I'm working on in which a bunch of teenagers had to recall a day six weeks earlier. And it was 1999, so they had to do it without the benefit of texts or Facebook or Instagram. Just for a lark, I asked some teenagers to try it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:02:49.73" data-timestamp="169.73"> Do you remember what you did on that Friday?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tyler</h4><p begin="00:02:51.54" data-timestamp="171.54"> No. Not at all. I can't remember anything.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:02:57.13" data-timestamp="177.13">Wait, nothing?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Tyler</h4><p begin="00:02:58.01" data-timestamp="178.01"> No. I can't remember anything that far back. I'm pretty sure I was in school. I think-- no?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:06.86" data-timestamp="186.86"> That's Tyler. He's 18. I asked my nephew Sam. He's 18, too.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sam</h4><p begin="00:03:11.63" data-timestamp="191.63">Not a clue. In school, probably. I would be in school. Actually, I think I worked that day. Yeah, I worked that day. And I went to school. That was about it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:23.98" data-timestamp="203.98"> Actually, on second thought?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sam</h4><p begin="00:03:25.91" data-timestamp="205.91"> I don't think I went to school that day.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:27.31" data-timestamp="207.31"> You don't think you went.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sam</h4><p begin="00:03:28.78" data-timestamp="208.78"> Yeah, no, I didn't. I definitely didn't.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:31.24" data-timestamp="211.24"> Here's Sam's friend Elliot. He seemed to have better recall.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Elliot</h4><p begin="00:03:34.66" data-timestamp="214.66"> Actually, I may have gone to the movies that night later.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:37.99" data-timestamp="217.99"> Do you remember what you saw?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Elliot</h4><p begin="00:03:39.10" data-timestamp="219.1">Now that I'm thinking. I'm sorry? Yeah, I think I saw <i>22 Jump</i> <i>Street</i>.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:42.49" data-timestamp="222.49"> OK. And did you go with friends?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Elliot</h4><p begin="00:03:45.59" data-timestamp="225.59"> Yeah. I went with Sam and this kid Sean, Carter, a bunch of people.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:51.64" data-timestamp="231.64"> Wait, Sam, my nephew Sam?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Elliot</h4><p begin="00:03:54.12" data-timestamp="234.12"> Yeah, yeah.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:03:55.08" data-timestamp="235.08"> Oh, OK. So Sam says he was at work.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Elliot</h4><p begin="00:03:58.48" data-timestamp="238.48"> Oh, then it wasn't that night, then.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Koenig</h4><p begin="00:04:02.18" data-timestamp="242.18"> One kid did actually remember pretty well, because it was the last day of state testing at his school and he'd saved up to go to a nightclub. That's the main thing I learned from this exercise, which is no big shocker, I guess. If some significant event happened that day, you remember that, plus you remember the entire day much better.</p><p begin="00:04:20.98" data-timestamp="260.98">If nothing significant happened, then the answers get very general. I most likely did this, or I most likely did that. These are words I've heard a lot lately. Here's the case I've been working on.</p><p begin="00:04:32.71" data-timestamp="272.71">Almost 15 years ago, on January 13, 1999, a girl named Hae Min Lee disappeared. She was a senior at Woodlawn High School in Baltimore County in Maryland. She was Korean. She was smart, and beautiful, and cheerful, and a great athlete. She played field hockey and lacrosse. And she was responsible.</p><p begin="00:04:50.84" data-timestamp="290.84">Right after school she was supposed to pick up her little cousin from kindergarten and drop her home. But she didn't show. That's when Hae Lee's family knew something was up, when the cousin's school called.</p><p begin="00:05:01.68" data-timestamp="301.68">About a month later, on February 9, Hae's body was found in a big park in Baltimore, really a rambling forest. A maintenance guy who said he'd stopped to take a leak on his way to work discovered her there. He'd noticed a bit of her black hair poking out of a shallow grave.</p><p begin="00:05:17.34" data-timestamp="317.34">The cause of death was manual strangulation, meaning someone did it with their hands. A couple weeks after that, so six weeks after she first went missing, Hae's ex-boyfriend, a guy named Adnan Syed, was arrested for her murder. He's been in prison ever since.</p><p begin="00:05:39.23" data-timestamp="339.23">I first heard about this story more than a year ago when I got an email from a woman named Rabia Chaudry. Rabia knows Adnan pretty well. Her younger brother Saad is Adnan's best friend. And they believe he's innocent.</p><p begin="00:05:51.03" data-timestamp="351.03">Rabia was writing to me because, way back when, I used to be a reporter for the <i>Baltimore</i> <i>Sun</i>, and she'd come across some stories I'd written about a well-known defense attorney in Baltimore who'd been disbarred for mishandling client money. That attorney was the same person who defended Adnan, her last major trial, in fact.</p><p begin="00:06:09.11" data-timestamp="369.11">Rabia told me she thought the attorney botched the case-- not just botched it, actually, but threw the case on purpose so she could get more money for the appeal. The lawyer had died a few years later. She'd been sick.</p><p begin="00:06:20.22" data-timestamp="380.22">Rabia asked if I would please just take a look at Adnan's case. I don't get emails like this every day. So I thought, sure, why not?</p><p begin="00:06:27.51" data-timestamp="387.51">I read a few newspaper clips about the case, looked up a few trial records. And on paper, the case was like a Shakespearean mashup-- young lovers from different worlds thwarting their families, secret assignations, jealousy, suspicion, and honor besmirched, the villain not a Moor exactly, but a Muslim all the same, and a final act of murderous revenge.

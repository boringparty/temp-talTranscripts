  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.01" data-timestamp="0.01"> Doctor Sean Roden normally works for NASA caring for astronauts before and during their missions, which he does from Houston Mission Control. But back in 2012, he had another gig as the doctor at the Amundsen-Scott Station at the South Pole. When he got there, they told him this story.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sean Roden</h4><p begin="00:00:16.37" data-timestamp="16.37"> I think it was probably the station manager. It's the story that's told.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:21.39" data-timestamp="21.39"> Is this the first story they tell every doctor who gets this job?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sean Roden</h4><p begin="00:00:26.41" data-timestamp="26.41"> Pretty much.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:27.98" data-timestamp="27.98"> They tell doctors this story because it is the most spectacular thing anyone has ever done in this kind of job. The story goes like this. In 1961, a Russian doctor got appendicitis at a Soviet Antarctic station, and so he needed emergency surgery to remove his appendix or he would probably die.</p><p begin="00:00:46.18" data-timestamp="46.18">And he was the one doctor there, snowed in during a blizzard. And so he had to figure out what to do. And so this Russian, a 27-year-old surgeon named Leonid Rogozov removed his own appendix and lived.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:58.71" data-timestamp="58.71"> Could you perform this procedure on yourself?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sean Roden</h4><p begin="00:01:03.49" data-timestamp="63.49"> Oh, hell, no. [LAUGHS] I guess if my life depended on it, I'd rather die trying than give up.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira  Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:14.96" data-timestamp="74.96"> Dr. Roden isn't a surgeon, though. And so to try to understand just how difficult this would be, I turned to a surgeon and associate professor at the Harvard Medical School named Doug Smink, who hadn't heard this story. But he's written a lot about appendectomies for textbooks and medical journals, and he's performed the procedure a lot.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:01:32.60" data-timestamp="92.6"> Probably 200 to 300 times, at least, maybe more.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:36.24" data-timestamp="96.24"> We have two accounts of this. There's a published report that the doctor wrote up in the Soviet Antarctic Expedition Information Bulletin. And then we have his--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:01:45.34" data-timestamp="105.34"> I don't read that.</p><p begin="00:01:47.47" data-timestamp="107.47">[IRA LAUGHS]</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:48.08" data-timestamp="108.08"> And we have the patient's diary.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:01:50.81" data-timestamp="110.81"> Are you calling him the patient or the surgeon? You could call him either one, right?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:56.57" data-timestamp="116.57"> Well, it's interesting because in his account, he goes back and forth between those things over and over again.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:02:03.24" data-timestamp="123.24"> That's fascinating.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:04.76" data-timestamp="124.76"> So this is from his published account. He says, "the symptoms noted were weakness, general malaise, later nausea. Body temperature rose to 37 degrees centigrade," which is 99 Fahrenheit. And then he wrote in his diary, April 30, 1961, "I did not sleep at all last night. It hurts like the devil. A snow storm whipping through my soul, wailing like 100 jackals."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:02:29.25" data-timestamp="149.25"> Sounds like appendicitis.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:31.27" data-timestamp="151.27"> Dr. Roden, the doctor who performed medicine at the South Pole went a little further.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sean Roden</h4><p begin="00:02:37.32" data-timestamp="157.32"> It sounds like a Russian. My wife and I have lived in Russia for over four years, off and on, with the space program and both speak Russian. So it's just-- it's poetic. It's perfect Russian.</p></div><div class="host"><h4> Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:54.58" data-timestamp="174.58"> This is from the diary. "An oppressive feeling of foreboding hangs over me. This is it. I have to think through the only possible way out, to operate on myself. It's almost impossible, but I can't just fold my arms and give up."</p><p begin="00:03:08.22" data-timestamp="188.22">And then, later that same day at 6:30 at night, "The guys have found out. They keep coming by to calm me down. And I'm upset with myself. I've spoiled everyone's holiday. Tomorrow's May Day and everybody is running around preparing the autoclave. We have to sterilize the bedding because we're going to operate."</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:25.70" data-timestamp="205.7"> And then he explains which person is going to do what. A meteorologist is going to hold retractors, a driver is going to hold a mirror, and then, just in case one of those two guys who's never witnessed an operation passes out, another guy was on standby to jump in. "In the event that the patient lost consciousness"-- he switches to third person and refers to himself as the patient, "they were instructed to inject the drugs in the syringes that I had prepared and to administer artificial respiration."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:03:56.29" data-timestamp="236.29"> Oh, geez.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:57.63" data-timestamp="237.63"> He writes, "The position of the patient at operation was designed to make it possible for him to perform the operation with minimal use of the mirror." And to do that, basically, they'd prop him up on his back.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:04:08.89" data-timestamp="248.89"> Right.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:09.96" data-timestamp="249.96"> He's leaning on one hip.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:04:12.11" data-timestamp="252.11"> Well, I'm just looking down at my own abdomen and trying to figure out how-- I know where I would make an incision. But to then see deeper down in the deeper layers would be a challenge. I'm sure that's where the mirror came in. It probably was also just really uncomfortable to sit in that position for two hours and try to work. I can only imagine.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:34.02" data-timestamp="274.02"> I'm going to read from the diary. "My poor assistants. At the last minute, I looked over at them. They stood there in their surgical whites, whiter than white themselves. I was scared, too. When I picked up the needle with the Novocain and gave myself the first injection, somehow I automatically switched into operating mode and from that point on, I didn't notice anything else."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:04:58.81" data-timestamp="298.81"> Wow. That's amazing. Almost like an out-of-body experience, it sounds like he had.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:04.72" data-timestamp="304.72"> "I worked without gloves. It was hard to see. The mirror helps, but it also hinders. After all, it's showing things backwards. I work mainly by touch. The bleeding is quite heavy, but I take my time. I try to work surely."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:05:19.42" data-timestamp="319.42"> The other thing to remember in this is that not only is he just operating on himself, but he is quite ill. Patients who have appendicitis don't feel like doing anything. They just want to curl up in bed and hope somebody will make them better. So to actually have to function and think clearly, both function physically and mentally, is amazing. It's just amazing.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:44.35" data-timestamp="344.35"> In fact, Rogozov spends most of the two-hour operation fighting off unconsciousness. After just 30 or 40 minutes, he wrote, he started to experience vertigo. Every four or five minutes he had to stop and rest. Dr. Smink explained to me that what happens in an appendectomy is you cut through the skin, then through a layer of fat, then three layers of muscle, then a layer of something called fascia. And then, finally, there's the peritoneum.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:06:08.02" data-timestamp="368.02"> I describe it like a clear balloon. It's about that thin.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:12.13" data-timestamp="372.13"> Inside that balloon are the intestines. And you kind of poke around the intestines with your fingers to find the appendix.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:06:19.19" data-timestamp="379.19"> The appendix is about the size of your pinky.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:22.72" data-timestamp="382.72"> And so he's having to move his own intestines out of the way to find the appendix.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Doug Smink</h4><p begin="00:06:28.61" data-timestamp="388.61"> Exactly. By feel, which is actually how we do it, often, in an open appendectomy. So he would have had experience with that. You basically feel around and you can feel what feels like a firm, almost like a sausage or something like that. And that is the appendix.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:46.44" data-timestamp="406.44"> But before Rogozov locates the appendix, he realizes that he accidentally tore into a part of the large intestine called the cecum and he has to sew it up.

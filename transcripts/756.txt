  <h3>Act Three: Bought the Farm</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:51:20.05" data-timestamp="3080.05"> Act 3, "Bought the Farm." So we close out today's program about people trying to do the right thing with something that happened to one of our staffers here, David Kestenbaum, and his family. They got this kid's toy, an educational toy that's been popular for decades. And his family followed the instructions that came with it to the letter. And they ended up learning a lesson I feel pretty confident the manufacturers did not intend. Here's David.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:51:46.29" data-timestamp="3106.29"> The ant farm was a gift to our kids from their grandparents. We were all excited for it-- though if I'm honest, maybe mostly me. I'd suggested it. I never had one growing up, but they seemed so cool. It's basically two sheets of see-through plastic, a half inch apart, made it into this frame. You get a bunch of sand to put in it. And then when you mail in the coupon, two tubes of live ants come to your house in the mail.</p><p begin="00:52:11.11" data-timestamp="3131.11">The promotional material read, "See the amazing behavior of live ants," with a photo of the ants digging elaborate tunnels. "Clean tunneling sand," it said, and also, "2016 National Parenting Product Awards winner." So we set it up, put the ants in as directed. There were, like, 100 of them.</p><p begin="00:52:28.81" data-timestamp="3148.81">The instructions say to feed them a crumb every week and add a few drops of water now and then. And at first, it was amazing. The ants got to work running in all different directions, over top of each other, picking up little bits of sand in their jaws. It was this chaotic construction site. Here's a little daily diary based on photos I took at the time. "March 9, we put them in. 10:50 PM, digging first exploratory tunnels. Energetic, fast-moving. Digging, digging, digging. March 10, morning, 8:00 AM, they made a loop, connected two tunnels, reached the bottom."</p><p begin="00:53:04.69" data-timestamp="3184.69">I shot a time-lapse video of them working, like you'd see on a TV nature special. It really was like a secret cross-section of an anthill. But then came the thing they don't touch on it all in the brochure. And for us, it really became the main thing. Later that day, 1:25 PM, our second day with the ant farm. There's a photo on my phone-- close-up of a dead ant buried in the sand. And a few days later, this video I happened to take--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:53:33.72" data-timestamp="3213.72"> And that's where the bodies are, in there. And over here. [GROANS]</p><p begin="00:53:40.41" data-timestamp="3220.41">Not only were some of the ants dying, but the other ants were picking up dead ones and carrying them, like soldiers in battle. And they'd been taking the bodies and body pieces-- there's an ant carrying another's head-- over into the corner and burying them. It was this mass grave.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:53:57.17" data-timestamp="3237.17"> [GROANS]</p><p begin="00:53:58.74" data-timestamp="3238.74">It seemed kind of outrageous that this is a kid's toy. I mean, I suppose death is part of other kid's games, but it's imaginary. This was not imaginary, and it really was hard to watch. Every morning, I'd look at the ant farm sitting on the counter in our kitchen. Day after day, they'd carry the new body to the corner and add them to the pile.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:54:18.33" data-timestamp="3258.33"> That guy is still a little alive, yeah. [GROANS]</p><p begin="00:54:22.68" data-timestamp="3262.68">The ant farm was basically a six-week exercise in watching things die. Eventually, there were just two ants left, and they did not seem well. They were having trouble walking.</p><p begin="00:54:41.24" data-timestamp="3281.24">Our kids honestly were sort of fine with this. Our younger son, who is eight, said, this is horrible. But I think mostly because he had in his mind that they would be pets, like a dog or something. His brother, who is one year older, nine, said, we could just buy more. They stopped looking at it and went on with their lives. But my wife and I felt terrible. The ant farm made us anxious. We felt responsible, like maybe we'd failed the ants. But also just so much death. There on our kitchen counter every morning, the mass grave.</p><p begin="00:55:13.94" data-timestamp="3313.94">What exactly were we looking at? What was this kid's toy supposed to be teaching us? So I called up a scientist, Deborah Gordon at Stanford, who has studied these kinds of ants, to ask, what the hell? She calmly explained the mass graves. She says, they're actually middens, meaning garbage heaps. The ants aren't burying their dead as much as taking out the trash. She says this may have evolved because dead ants sometimes carry disease.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:55:40.46" data-timestamp="3340.46"> Well, they take the dead to the furthest possible place, which ends up being the corner. If you gave them a really large space to move around, they'd take them even further away. I've seen them go out on the foraging trails 10, 15 meters away and dump the ant.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:55:57.66" data-timestamp="3357.66"> Wow.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:55:57.73" data-timestamp="3357.73"> So those are ones that I think must be infected in some way. That's a lot of work.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:56:04.07" data-timestamp="3364.07"> What are they dying of in here? Do they die of old age? Is their heart-- do they have a heart? Like, the way a heart is only good for however many beats. What fails in them?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:56:15.27" data-timestamp="3375.27"> I don't think anybody knows how ants die of old age.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:56:19.56" data-timestamp="3379.56"> Ants do have something like a heart, and a tiny brain. In fact, she says, they're stuck in a little repeating loop, waiting for a thing that never happens.</p><p begin="00:58:06.29" data-timestamp="3486.29">She says basically, these ants are supposed to be going out, foraging for food, and bringing it back to the nest, and then waiting until they come into contact with enough other ants coming back before going out again. No one leaves the ant farm.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:58:27.41" data-timestamp="3507.41"> So they're probably in some kind of a feedback loop that isn't what they usually do.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:58:34.55" data-timestamp="3514.55"> Because when they go up to another ant, they're never getting a signal that, oh, I've been out and I found food.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:58:39.20" data-timestamp="3519.2"> And I found food, yeah.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:58:40.85" data-timestamp="3520.85"> I see. I wonder what the last ant is going to die of.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Deborah Gordon</h4><p begin="00:58:46.38" data-timestamp="3526.38"> The last ant is just going to stand around, because nothing will happen to it that has any meaning.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:59:01.38" data-timestamp="3541.38"> Knowing this somehow changed things for me.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.50" data-timestamp="0.5">Last resort letters aren't exactly a secret, but most of us haven't heard of them. Journalist Ron Rosenbaum came across them because he's got a Google alert that brings him any news story with the phrase &quot;World War III&quot; in it. He's written about nuclear war on and off for decades. And news came across the Google about a BBC documentary that details what these last resort letters are all about. Ron explained.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:00:23.69" data-timestamp="23.69">On every British nuclear submarine, there's a safe. And then within that safe is another safe, and within that safe, the inner safe, is a letter from the British prime minister. And every new prime minister that comes into office is required to handwrite this letter and seal it. And the letter instructs the submarine commander what to do when the homeland has been wiped out by a nuclear attack.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:58.01" data-timestamp="58.01">In other words, Britain's destroyed. And maybe it's better if you hear this in a British accent. This is from the BBC documentary. The letter tells the sub commander--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Documentary Narrator</h4><p begin="00:01:06.11" data-timestamp="66.11">--whether to retaliate or not if Britain is already a smoking, irradiated ruin, and they're dead. It's a system peculiar to the British, and emulated by no other nuclear nations.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:17.91" data-timestamp="77.91">You write, with all due respect for our British cousins, this seems, well, insane. The old-fashioned pen and ink on paper quality of it all somehow makes the system seem like it emanated from a 19th century madhouse.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:01:28.76" data-timestamp="88.76">It's so absurd. It's Kubrick territory. It's Dr. Strangelove territory. I mean, I can't imagine, you know, someone writing, &quot;Dear Mr. Submarine commander, I'm dead. Everyone you know is dead. We're all radioactive ashes. Here's what I'd like you to do. Number one,--&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:51.22" data-timestamp="111.22">Rosenbaum says that the letter poses some immediate practical questions. If the whole country was just destroyed, how would the sub commander know who killed them and where to target his bomb? Does the sub commander have to show his letter to anybody else? If he's the only one reading the letter, what's to stop him from substituting his own judgement for what's on the paper?</p><p begin="00:02:09.33" data-timestamp="129.33">And it raises a few broader questions. If the purpose of a nuclear deterrent is to convince your enemies that if they destroy you, you're going to destroy them, doesn't it undermine the whole point of having nuclear weapons in the first place to publicize the fact that there's a secret letter that very well might say no, don't retaliate?</p><p begin="00:02:28.72" data-timestamp="148.72">Which, Rosenbaum says, gets to the moral paradox that is at the heart of all nuclear doctrines.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:02:34.57" data-timestamp="154.57">If your nation has been wiped out by a nuclear strike, is there any point in retaliating and killing tens, hundreds of millions of innocent people when the threat to retaliate has already failed?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:50.96" data-timestamp="170.96">Right. You've been annihilated.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:02:52.42" data-timestamp="172.42">Yeah. Your nation's been annihilated. Is there any point in killing millions out of pique, vengeance, or is it justice? You know, I was at this conference of generals and admirals in July, and I actually made a point of asking them, what's the point of retaliation? And they all recognized that this was a difficult question, but they all said that well, to talk about it invites attack. Because you're undermining the threat of retaliation by even discussing your Hamlet-like doubts and dithering about whether you'd retaliate.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:35.84" data-timestamp="215.84">And so then why have a letter? Why let people know there's a handwritten letter that might say, oh, don't launch the missiles?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:03:43.71" data-timestamp="223.71">It doesn't make any sense. Why did they make the existence of this letter known?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:53.73" data-timestamp="233.73">Ron Rosenbaum contacted the British Defense Ministry and was told essentially that the letter was a fail-safe, almost a formality. If Britain were destroyed, somebody was going to need to issue an order to launch those nuclear missiles. The letter is that order.</p><p begin="00:04:07.30" data-timestamp="247.3">But of course, this is what they would have to say, even if it's not true, to make the threat seem credible. And you really need all the theatrics of the two safes and the personal handwritten note for all that, you know? And remember, no one knows what's in the letter. It's sealed. Or-- maybe this is something that's maybe better to hear in a British accent. As the BBC puts it--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Documentary Narrator</h4><p begin="00:04:28.76" data-timestamp="268.76">History will never find out, because nobody knows what's in the prime minister's letters of last resort, and they're destroyed unread by the cabinet office when the premiership changes.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:38.82" data-timestamp="278.82">Rosenbaum believes that most people, faced with the choice of ordering genocide after the threat of genocide has failed, would not launch the missiles. And one of the few people who've had their finger on Britain's nuclear button, have ever talked about this publicly, a former defense minister named Dennis Healy, says in the BBC documentary--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Dennis Healy</h4><p begin="00:04:55.88" data-timestamp="295.88">I realize I would find it very, very difficult indeed to agree to use a nuclear weapon, and I think most people would. Because most of the people you've killed will be innocent civilians.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:08.60" data-timestamp="308.6">Which brings us back to the question, why do it this way? What's the advantage of a secret that's inside a safe, inside another safe, deep below the surface of the ocean? A secret sealed away where nobody can get to it?</p><p begin="00:05:20.84" data-timestamp="320.84">Well, Rosenbaum found himself asking a very similar question while researching a very different subject years ago. He spent ten years writing a book about Adolf Hitler. And he said that when he was researching that, some of the evidence that he's heard about-- there was stories, for example, of a hypnotherapist report on Adolf Hitler from when Adolf Hitler was in a sanitarium after the first World War-- that supposedly explained why Hitler wanted to conquer the world. And this was, supposedly, again, sealed away in a safe deposit box in Switzerland.</p><p begin="00:05:48.79" data-timestamp="348.79">And Rosenbaum has a theory about the appeal of that kind of thing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:05:52.51" data-timestamp="352.51">It allows us to believe that certain truths do exist. There's an explanation for Hitler, but it's locked away in some safe deposit box beyond our grasp. So it gives us a feeling that this is not some numinous, irretrievable mystery. We could explain Hitler, if only we had found the right safe deposit box.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:13.97" data-timestamp="373.97">To follow your logic, then the advantage of having this letter with the order locked away in a box inside another box-- what it does is that you don't want your enemy to know that you're not going to launch those missiles, but you don't want to think for yourself that you are. So it lets you believe both things. It lets two truths exist at the same time.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ron Rosenbaum</h4><p begin="00:06:37.37" data-timestamp="397.37">Yes, I suppose that's true. It allows us to have it both ways.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:48.19" data-timestamp="408.19">From WEBZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:06:53.68" data-timestamp="413.68">Today on our show, Contents Unknown. Stories of truths, big and small, that are locked away. Sometimes the most exciting thing about these truths is knowing that they're out there, the answer exists, but you don't know it.</p><p begin="00:07:06.74" data-timestamp="426.74">Our show today in three acts. Act one is about the truths contained in America's mini storage lockers. No kidding. Act two is about the truth of an entire empire 1,400 years ago. Act three is about truth wiped away from inside someone's head. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Needle In A Crapstack</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:32.20" data-timestamp="452.2">Act one. Needle in a crap sack. Self storage units. There's a lot of them. 2.35 billion with a B square feet in the United States, according to the Self Storage Association. That, in case you're wondering, is 7.4 square feet of self storage for every man, woman, and child in this country, meaning all of us, all of us, could stand inside self storage at the same time.</p><p begin="00:07:57.79" data-timestamp="477.79">And when people do not pay their self storage bills after the facility tries to collect through all the normal means and fails, often the contents of the storage lockers get auctioned off.</p><p begin="00:08:09.98" data-timestamp="489.98">Jon Mooallem went to some of those auctions in Northern California. They're a big exercise in guessing at the unknown.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jon Mooallem</h4><p begin="00:08:15.73" data-timestamp="495.73">This morning, a little after nine, a crowd of a few dozen prospectors is already waiting in the fog outside Bridgehead Self Storage, an hour east of San Francisco. The auctioneer, a guy with the briskly mustache named John Cardoza, runs a company called Storage Auction Experts. Today he'll go to six different storage places in three different towns and auction off upwards of 50 units. The crowd will follow him for nine hours.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Cardoza</h4><p begin="00:08:41.86" data-timestamp="521.86">Hey Ricky? Go ahead and open up this one too, please. I assembled a working self out of the behavior of others.</p><p begin="00:49:28.30" data-timestamp="2968.3">The truth, or at least what my family, the State Department, the Fulbright Organization, and I have determined as the truth is that the psychotic break, the hallucinations, the amnesia-- it all was an extreme side effect of the Larium, the anti-malarial drug that was a prescription of choice in those days. It's got a reputation for doing things like what happened to me, as well as much worse.</p><p begin="00:49:52.05" data-timestamp="2992.05">I started taking the drug a month before leaving, as prescribed. My mother says that she could tell I wasn't acting normally before I left, but she chalked it up to the stress of preparing for a year in India</p><p begin="00:50:08.77" data-timestamp="3008.77">When my parents walked into my room in the asylum, a motor spun inside my brain. I recognized them, and through them, I recognized myself. I wasn't sure of the details of my life, but my parents defined the broad particulars. We hugged, the three of us making a tent over the bed with our bodies.</p><p begin="00:50:29.27" data-timestamp="3029.27">I told my mom that I was sorry. She told me that she was sorry. My dad pushed his glasses up and rubbed his eyes. My sister Betsy had put together a box for me. In it were two dozen cookies and an array of pictures of me from different times of my life. Me in a kilt, me at the beach, me with a dog, me standing on a station wagon smoking a cigar, me with my sisters, me with my arms around different women. Whoever I was, I couldn't keep a straight face for a photograph.</p><p begin="00:51:05.13" data-timestamp="3065.13">My parents told me where they were saying. They said it was too nice for them, but the restaurant was good and didn't serve them anything too spicy for breakfast. They said I needed to stay for another night at Greenlands, and then I'd go home with them to their hotel, and then we'd get on a plane for the U.S. They said that Sally was excited to see me and that Ann was planning on coming to Ohio for a visit.</p><p begin="00:51:28.39" data-timestamp="3088.39">These names mean as much to you as they did to me. My parents said them like they were people I knew and loved, so I nodded and said I was excited to see them.</p><p begin="00:51:37.96" data-timestamp="3097.96">They checked me out the next day. My dad hailed a rickshaw and the three of us piled into the backseat, me in between the two of them.</p><p begin="00:51:51.62" data-timestamp="3111.62">In Ohio I met Sally, who was my dog. I talked to Ann, my girlfriend, on the phone. I recognized the dog, but not Ann's voice. We hadn't been dating for very long, and my parents didn't have any pictures of her. I had no idea what she looked like. She seemed nice. She seemed like she cared about me.</p><p begin="00:52:13.35" data-timestamp="3133.35">Those days spent in my parents' house, I mostly stared at pictures, trying to recapture anything of who I was. I developed a system. I would look at the picture and imagine this character of me. Then I would place this character I had developed into these settings. I would imagine the jokes that were told. I would imagine how the character of me would feel. I was gathering information on this character of me all the time.</p><p begin="00:52:46.34" data-timestamp="3166.34">Ann came out on Halloween weekend, and even when I picked her up at the airport, even when she kissed me, I wasn't sure I had the right woman. We spent the weekend hiking with my parents. We had sex that first night of her visit, and she was still a stranger. I could not remember her or any details of our relationship. It was wiped clear.</p><p begin="00:53:07.95" data-timestamp="3187.95">She was in love with me. I tried the memory trick of placing the character of me in a relationship with her, and it didn't fit. I couldn't conceive of myself loving her. Sometimes I think that forgetting her with the cruelest thing I've ever done to a person.</p><p begin="00:53:24.87" data-timestamp="3204.87">I called her a week later and broke up with her. Ann isn't her real name, by the way. She asked me to change it for this story.</p><p begin="00:53:38.24" data-timestamp="3218.24">I flew back to India on November 20, a little more than a month after the incident. I wasn't entirely stable yet, but I was terrified that the Fulbright Organization would pull my funding if I gave them too much time to mull my psychological condition. I figured the best way back to health was to act like I was healthy. I put on a placid professional exterior while inside I was a full martini glass placed on a rubber ball.</p><p begin="00:54:03.61" data-timestamp="3243.61">It wasn't until I was on the connecting flight into Hyderabad that I realized I had no idea of where I lived. I panicked as we were touching down. I figured I'd get a cab. I just had no idea where to tell the driver to go.</p><p begin="00:54:17.56" data-timestamp="3257.56">Instead, Veda was waiting for me at the airport. I didn't recognize him, but by that time, I recognized the look of someone recognizing me. It was a skill I had developed in Ohio. I'd forgotten that I had emailed him my flight details.</p><p begin="00:54:32.70" data-timestamp="3272.7">He clapped me on my back and said, &quot;The hero returns!&quot; He gave me a bag full of toothpaste, ramen, and milk. &quot;Enough to last you for a day,&quot; he said.</p><p begin="00:54:43.28" data-timestamp="3283.28">We drove through the city. It was 4 AM and silent. We made turn after turn until we ended up in front of a tall building that looked like all of the other apartment complexes. Veda took me up the elevator and then up a short flight of stairs. He handed me a keyring. &quot;OK then, hero. I will be off. I can get some sleep before the kids start causing terror.&quot;</p><p begin="00:55:06.33" data-timestamp="3306.33">&quot;You have kids?&quot; &quot;I will call you on your mobile, and we can go for some dinner at your favorite place.&quot;</p><p begin="00:55:12.69" data-timestamp="3312.69">I grabbed him, and I learned that Veda didn't like to be hugged.</p><p begin="00:55:16.10" data-timestamp="3316.1">I opened the door. Nothing. The road, the building, and now my apartment. Nothing was jogged, jostled, or loosened in my memory. I was alone, and lonelier than I thought it could be in a room filled with things that I'd selected.</p><p begin="00:55:32.14" data-timestamp="3332.14">There were books. I opened them and found my handwriting in the margins. Still nothing. I had read these books. Now I had to read them again. But why bother? If I lost my memory again, all that work would be futile.</p><p begin="00:55:51.29" data-timestamp="3351.29">The city started to wake up, and the sounds of bicycles, car horns, and newspaper wallahs rose up to me on the roof. I lit up a cigarette to fight the tension building in my chest. If it was going to happen again, I would throw myself off the roof.</p><p begin="00:56:07.77" data-timestamp="3367.77">I stubbed out the cigarette and opened the door to an apartment furnished with the possessions of a stranger.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:56:19.09" data-timestamp="3379.09">David MacLean fully recovered all his memory, except for the year proceeding his illness. The story is an excerpt from a book of essays that he's writing about losing his memory and memory loss. He's looking for a publisher.</p><p begin="00:57:03.05" data-timestamp="3423.05">Our program was produced today by Sarah Koenig with Alex Blumberg, Jane Feltes, Lisa Pollak, Robyn Semian, Alissa Shipp, and Nancy Updike.

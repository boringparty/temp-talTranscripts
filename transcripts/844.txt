  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.88" data-timestamp="0.88"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. So this is going to be one of those shows where I'm just going to say a few things here and then get out of the way. It's based on a single, kind of a remarkable recording of a thing that happens all over the country, but you never really get to hear it. It's a parole hearing in Illinois-- 13 people in a small room having to decide, should a man be released from prison?</p><p begin="00:00:24.36" data-timestamp="24.36">This fact surprised me. Parole hearings, the system where somebody with a long sentence comes before somebody and gets a chance to get out, that's been abolished in about a third of all the states. Most other states have limited it in various ways. The reasons for that-- people in prison and their advocates said it was really subjective, racially biased, unfair. There was no way to appeal the decisions. They said too few people were being set free.</p><p begin="00:00:49.89" data-timestamp="49.89">Conservatives, meanwhile, found it too lenient. They said too many people were being set free. So very few people get out of prison thanks to a parole board hearing determining if it's time for their incarceration to end, though right now, there is a lot of talk about expanding the use of parole boards, making more people eligible. There's a bill in the New York State Assembly, two competing bills in Illinois, and they're in other states, too.</p><p begin="00:01:13.86" data-timestamp="73.86">Reporter Ben Austen got interested in the question at the heart of all this-- what is actually happening in those parole board hearings? How do they make these monumental decisions? What sways them? What doesn't?</p><p begin="00:01:26.32" data-timestamp="86.32">These boards are trying to adjudicate these very squishy, nearly impossible questions, like when is a person rehabilitated? How can you tell? When should a long prison sentence end? This next question is almost too grand to say out loud, but it is in there, too-- what is justice?</p><p begin="00:01:43.96" data-timestamp="103.96">All this plays out in this weird backwater of the judicial system that doesn't get a lot of scrutiny. Can you remember the last time you saw a news story-- any news story-- about a parole board hearing? And for all the TV dramas about-- I have to say-- almost every aspect of the criminal justice system in all of its parts, there is none set in a parole board.</p><p begin="00:02:04.82" data-timestamp="124.82">Ben lives in Illinois and spent more than a year going to every parole board hearing there. They happen once a month, each one looking at 5 or 10 cases. And he put together what you're about to hear.</p><p begin="00:02:15.61" data-timestamp="135.61">The man they're considering for release in this case is 72 years old, been locked up for almost 50 years-- most of his life. The parole board has some information about the case, but definitely not everything you would want. That's part of what makes it so interesting listening to this, hearing how they deal with that.</p><p begin="00:02:30.19" data-timestamp="150.19">This is actually one of the hearings that Ben sat through on his very first day going to these hearings. Basically, he and his producer, Bill Healy, showed up, threw two recorders on the table in the middle of the room, and captured this conversation that you're about to hear.</p><p begin="00:02:44.22" data-timestamp="164.22">And it just stuck with him, this case-- not just the difficulty of the decision that they had to make or all the stuff that they wished they knew but didn't know, but the ruling they came to stuck with him. OK, enough said. Here's Ben Austen.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:03:03.16" data-timestamp="183.16"> I'm surprised by how plain the hearing room is, how small. There's barely enough space to fit a wood conference table. And squeezed around it, 13 board members are sitting elbow to elbow. These are people who spend a lot of time together. They're from different parts of the state. And in between the cases, they debate things like who has worse traffic, worse snow.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Perkins</h4><p begin="00:03:27.90" data-timestamp="207.9"> You guys don't get it like we get it in Peoria.</p><p begin="00:03:31.21" data-timestamp="211.21">(SUBJECT) BOARD MEMBER 1: I don't know. We're still about 50 miles south.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Perkins</h4><p begin="00:03:33.85" data-timestamp="213.85"> Yeah, you don't get it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:03:35.46" data-timestamp="215.46"> They tease each other about being long-winded.</p><p begin="00:03:38.07" data-timestamp="218.07">(SUBJECT) BOARD MEMBER 2: Keep it short, Vonetta. Remember that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vonetta</h4><p begin="00:03:40.20" data-timestamp="220.2"> Oh, look at you tell me to keep it short. Oh, OK.</p><p begin="00:03:45.64" data-timestamp="225.64">(SUBJECT) BOARD MEMBER 2: Who said that?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:03:46.35" data-timestamp="226.35"> They hunch over laptops and coffee cups and fat accordion files filled with case documents that go back way into the previous century. There's a former public school principal and a high school guidance counselor, former prosecutors, and three retired cops.</p><p begin="00:04:03.15" data-timestamp="243.15">By law, the parole board includes both Democrats and Republicans. They're appointed by the governor, approved by the state senate. And it's a full-time job in Illinois. They're currently paid about $100,000 a year.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Craig Findley</h4><p begin="00:04:15.68" data-timestamp="255.68"> OK, the next case is Mr. Henry Dee. Ms. Martinez is recognized.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:04:20.29" data-timestamp="260.29"> The hearing starts. The chairman acknowledges Virginia Martinez, one of the board members. She's seated near the head of the table. And she begins to talk about the person they're considering for release.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Virginia Martinez</h4><p begin="00:04:31.93" data-timestamp="271.93"> This is the case of Henry Dee, number C01657. Mr. Dee is currently 72 years of age, having been born on August 24, 1946. I interviewed him at Stateville Correctional Center.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:04:46.07" data-timestamp="286.07"> If you look around the small conference room, one person you won't see is Henry Dee, the guy up for parole. He's still in a prison more than 100 miles away. The way these hearings work, one board member travels to the prison and interviews the parole applicant. The pairings are chosen at random. It was Martinez's turn.</p><p begin="00:05:08.68" data-timestamp="308.68">Now, she'll walk her colleagues through the details of the case and what she learned in the interview. And she'll give a recommendation for or against Dee's release. The other board members don't have to follow it. Often, they don't. They'll debate, and after that, they'll vote. The whole thing takes less than an hour, and for some cases, way less.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Virginia Martinez</h4><p begin="00:05:32.07" data-timestamp="332.07"> Inmate Dee is serving 100 to 200 years for two counts of murder to run consecutively and 20 to 40 years for two counts of robbery. His projected discharge date is June 27, 2162.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:05:46.17" data-timestamp="346.17"> Martinez says the release date is 2162. Dee was given up to 200 years. It's what people in prison call "Buck Rogers time," like out of science fiction. Henry Dee-- that's his last name, D-E-E-- first came before the parole board in 1981. People who are rejected get another hearing every one to five years. And Dee has had two dozen hearings since.</p><p begin="00:06:12.85" data-timestamp="372.85">To make parole, you need a majority of the board's votes. Dee has never even come close. In fact, in all his years of coming up for consideration, only one board member has ever voted for release. That's it-- just one. A big reason-- the severity of the long-ago crime.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Virginia Martinez</h4><p begin="00:06:34.33" data-timestamp="394.33"> The facts of the case-- in the early morning of August 17, 1971, cab driver Arthur Snyder stopped at his Chicago home after his evening shift. He was accosted by inmate Dee and co-defendant James Sayles, who were armed and forced their way into the Snyders' apartment.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:06:54.22" data-timestamp="414.22"> The details-- they're painful to listen to. The crime is brutal. If you're listening with kids, this is a heads-up.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Virginia Martinez</h4><p begin="00:07:01.84" data-timestamp="421.84"> Once inside, the offenders hogtied, gagged, and blindfolded Arthur Snyder, leaving him in the kitchen. They then took Edith Snyder into the bedroom, where they bound, gagged, and blindfolded her.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:07:12.50" data-timestamp="432.5"> This was a robbery. The men stole some valuables. And then--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Virginia Martinez</h4><p begin="00:07:17.38" data-timestamp="437.38"> The offenders then beat her to death with a claw hammer, brutally striking her about the face, skull, and body. They then returned to the kitchen, where they beat Arthur Snyder with the same hammer, striking him so hard that the hammer became embedded in his skull.</p><p begin="00:07:32.36" data-timestamp="452.36">Mr. Snyder was 52 and his wife was 46 at the time of their murders. Before leaving with a number of items taken from the apartment, the offenders turned on the gas jets in the oven and set the mattress on fire where Mrs. Snyder's body was laying.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ben Austen</h4><p begin="00:07:47.42" data-timestamp="467.42"> There was evidence of rape, but no charges were brought. They stole Arthur Snyder's car, his taxi, and drove off. All this information is from Dee's original trial. But listening to it in this room, it feels present tense, like these terrible events just happened.

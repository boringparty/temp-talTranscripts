And the reason he keeps coming back?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil</h4><p begin="00:44:20.18" data-timestamp="2660.18"> Oyo, who is now part owner-- I knew him from working at another bar. So I knew that he was working here, or had become an owner here. So I decided to come here to support him.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:44:29.81" data-timestamp="2669.81"> And like that, I had my answer. I heard this from people inside and out of Summerhill-- staff and bartenders at other bars, regulars and haters. If you want to know why so many people come to Summerhill, look for Oyo.</p><p begin="00:44:44.53" data-timestamp="2684.53">Wallahi Oyo-- he mostly goes by just Oyo-- is the executive chef at Summerhill. He became partial owner of the restaurant shortly after all the controversy began. He comes up with the menu. He picks the DJs. On a busy Saturday night, you might find him standing out front, scanning Nostrand Avenue from beneath a black baseball cap, presumably looking for more customers. The word was, the embattled Becca Brennan took a diminished role in her own business so that a 27-year-old black man could become the face of Summerhill.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Neil</h4><p begin="00:45:13.90" data-timestamp="2713.9"> It's not like she has no involvement. There's a lot of stuff that she still does. But I feel like, mostly, when people come here, they see Oyo.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:45:24.06" data-timestamp="2724.06"> Oyo's wasn't just a symbolic contribution. I spoke to him in the kitchen that he oversees. If you ask him what exactly he and Becca did to get black people to come Summerhill after the protests and the bad press, he'll tell you nothing. He and his partner just weathered the storm, and eventually, people came. But that's not exactly true. Faced with the storm, Oyo sent up flares.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wallahi Oyo</h4><p begin="00:45:45.87" data-timestamp="2745.87"> And I said-- I told my friends, yo, come on. I need help. My bar has been protested. I need help. I mean, I need-- you know what I'm saying? I need y'all to come support, because I'm drowning.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:45:56.17" data-timestamp="2756.17"> And from the look of things, many of them did come. Maybe you've heard, we're a forgiving people. But not all of us.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Justine Stephens</h4><p begin="00:46:03.39" data-timestamp="2763.39"> She's using his blackness, in a way, to build this business and make it cool again, because of her mistake.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:46:13.95" data-timestamp="2773.95"> This is Justine Stephens. She and two of her peers organized the first protests against Summerhill after reading about the place on Gothamist last year. She still refuses to step foot inside. But she has noticed Summerhill becoming increasingly popular with black folks in the last few months. She says that's all Oyo remaking the place's image.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Justine Stephens</h4><p begin="00:46:31.97" data-timestamp="2791.97"> A lot of their online press is kind of fashioned in a way where it's-- it looks hip. It looks like a club scene. Like, if I take out my phone now and take a look at it-- like Wednesday Night Jump Up, for example. I don't think Caucasian Becca Brennan from Toronto would say "jump up."</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:46:57.02" data-timestamp="2817.02"> I mean, wouldn't that be something that is more representative-- that kind of marketing, that kind of branding-- more representative of the community?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Justine Stephens</h4><p begin="00:47:06.55" data-timestamp="2826.55"> But is it pandering?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:47:09.64" data-timestamp="2829.64"> I don't know. I mean, if-- it's kind of like, if they advertise for something, they advertise something that people in the community want, or cleave to, or are excited by, and those people come, and they support it, is that pander--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Justine Stephens</h4><p begin="00:47:24.89" data-timestamp="2844.89"> No, I think that's totally fair. And this annoyed the hell out of Oyo.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wallahi Oyo</h4><p begin="00:47:54.79" data-timestamp="2874.79"> I'm doing this for my pockets, you know what I'm saying? This is my business. Don't think that I'm doing this for her.</p><p begin="00:48:01.58" data-timestamp="2881.58">I don't think she's using me. If anything, I'm using Becca, you know what I'm saying? Like, trust me. This is for my pockets.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil  Drumming</h4><p begin="00:48:07.78" data-timestamp="2887.78"> Oyo thinks about his pockets a lot. An unapologetic capitalist, Oyo learned from his dad, who owned a nail salon nearby. Later, Oyo himself ran a DVD shop on the block, and then he worked as a chef at a black-owned bar down the street. He said that he loves doing business in Crown Heights, and that people in the neighborhood have been waiting for him to open up his own spot.</p><p begin="00:48:28.66" data-timestamp="2908.66">Before the protests happened, Oyo convinced Becca Brennan to let him launch a pop-up sandwich shop inside her fledgling restaurant. They were both satisfied with how that went, so they started negotiating for Oyo to accept an ownership stake in the restaurant.</p><p begin="00:48:41.44" data-timestamp="2921.44">Then came Justine and the protesters. Oyo took the deal anyway. It was the open door that he'd been waiting for, and there was no way he wasn't going to walk through it, no matter how many people stood outside, jeering. He became partners with Becca.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:48:53.03" data-timestamp="2933.03"> Were you worried?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>  Wallahi   Oyo</h4><p begin="00:48:54.90" data-timestamp="2934.9"> No. My confidence in myself was, no, I'm not worried. Me, you know, I feel like I'm Oyo. I can make it happen.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>      Neil  Drumming</h4><p begin="00:49:07.88" data-timestamp="2947.88"> Even Oyo will admit that Becca Brennan made a mistake. And for most people, that was the story-- a white woman who offended some people in a historically black neighborhood. But it's this other, smaller interaction that gets to me more-- two black people, Justine and Oyo, pointing at each other, asking who really belongs here.</p><p begin="00:49:34.66" data-timestamp="2974.66">When Justine saw Oyo defending his mostly silent white partner, she saw a black man who was willing to sell out his community for bread crumbs. Justine was new to the area, but her father grew up in Brooklyn. She thought that the people in the neighborhood deserved better and that she needed to alert them. Oyo was, in her mind, a puppet.</p><p begin="00:49:55.08" data-timestamp="2995.08">But when Oyo looked out and saw Justine leading a crowd of mostly white protesters, he didn't see a representative of the community. He was from the community. He had a tattoo on his arm of the street sign at Bedford and St. Marks, right up the block. Justine, who was from Norwalk, Connecticut, had chemically straightened hair, and she was threatening his pockets.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Justine Stephens</h4><p begin="00:50:14.25" data-timestamp="3014.25"> I had braids. I'm not trying to look like no fucking white bitch, I'm telling you. I'm telling you--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:50:17.55" data-timestamp="3017.55"> Justine sent me this recording from that day. You're really--"</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:51:09.38" data-timestamp="3069.38"> The Facebook messages went on and on. But then, it was Oyo's mother who ultimately told them to apologize to Justine, because he shouldn't talk to black women like that. He did try. Justine showed me contrite messages he'd sent to her on Instagram. But she's no longer interested in opening a dialogue with Oyo or anyone involved with Summerhill.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:51:58.87" data-timestamp="3118.87"> So what would it take for you to visit that bar?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4> Justine  Stephens</h4><p begin="00:52:03.28" data-timestamp="3123.28"> I'm not going to. <div class="act" id="act3">
  <h3>Act Three: An Awful Place That You’re Lucky To Get To</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>     Ira  Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:19.41" data-timestamp="3199.41"> Act Three, An Awful Place That You're Lucky to Get To. OK, so this is something that never really happens. We have a couple minutes left in the show. And I'm glad, because it lets me read you this thing that I like.</p><p begin="00:53:43.04" data-timestamp="3223.04">This is kind of tangentially on theme with If You Build It, They Will Come. It's about old age, which, I guess, is a place that is built for us. And maybe we get there. I don't know. Don't think too hard about that.</p><p begin="00:53:55.00" data-timestamp="3235">Donald Hall died a few weeks ago. He was on the show years back, and I knew him a tiny bit. He was a poet and writer, the poet laureate of the United States for a while. He wrote about lots of things.</p><p begin="00:54:06.43" data-timestamp="3246.43">But in the last few years, he's been writing about old age in this utterly unsentimental, lucid way that I really, really love, like in this piece. This is from his book, Essays After 80.</p><p begin="00:54:19.10" data-timestamp="3259.1">"After a life of loving the old, by natural law, I turned old myself. Decade follows each other. 30 was terrifying. 40, I never noticed because I was drunk. 50 was best, with a total change of life. 60 extended the bliss of 50.</p><p begin="00:54:33.51" data-timestamp="3273.51">Then came my cancers, Jane's death." Jane was his wife. "And over the years, I traveled to another universe. However alert we are, however much we think we know what will happen, antiquity remains an unknown, unanticipated galaxy.</p><p begin="00:54:50.08" data-timestamp="3290.08">It's alien. And old people are a separate form of life. They have green skin, with two heads that sprout antennae. They can be pleasant. They can be annoying. In the supermarket, those old ladies won't get out of my way.</p><p begin="00:55:03.66" data-timestamp="3303.66">Most important, they are permanently other. When we turn 80, we understand that we are extraterrestrial. If we forget for a moment that we're old, we're reminded when we try to stand up and when we encounter someone young, who appears to observe green skin, extra heads, and protuberances.</p><p begin="00:55:20.93" data-timestamp="3320.93">People's response to our separateness can be callous, can be good-hearted, and is always condescending. When a woman writes to the newspaper approving of something that I've done, she calls me a nice old gentleman. She intends to praise me with 'nice' and 'gentleman.' 'Old' is true enough. And she lets us know that I am not a grumpy old fart.</p><p begin="00:55:40.07" data-timestamp="3340.07">But 'nice' and 'gentleman' put me in a box where she can rub my head and hear me purr. Or maybe she would prefer me to wag my tail, lick her hand, and make ingratiating dog noises."</p><p begin="00:55:51.01" data-timestamp="3351.01">Anyway, that's by Donald Hall, from his book, Essays After 80. He just published another book on the subject with the vivid name, A Carnival of Losses. I recommend both books, or especially his book, Without, which is poetry that he wrote when his wife, Jane Kenyon, died. Anyway, thanks, Donald.</p></div>  </div>

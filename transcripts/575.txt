  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.75" data-timestamp="0.75">OK, this just in. Sometimes things are not what they seem. Big news, I know. Here's an interesting example of it.</p><p begin="00:00:07.51" data-timestamp="7.51">When Damien Cave moved to Mexico and started reporting there for <i>The New</i> <i>York</i> <i>Times,</i> a few months in, he noticed that when he would get onto an airplane and flip through the in-flight magazine, there would be these ads for tourists-- you know, those glossy, beautiful pictures of mountains, and river rafting, and nature, and "come visit" kinds of ads-- for places that he knew were centers of drug violence and gang violence.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:00:29.99" data-timestamp="29.99">I mean, it basically became the standard operating procedure for state after state. As the violence moved, the ads popped up in new places.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:38.60" data-timestamp="38.6">And at some point it occurred to him, maybe these ads could be a useful reporting tool.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:00:44.14" data-timestamp="44.14">You know, Mexico is a place that there's just no transparency. And the government never gave us reliable statistics on deaths and murders. And over time, I started to just realize, OK, if there's a state or a location that's spending a lot of money in a magazine to tell me how beautiful it is, it must be pretty messed up.</p><p begin="00:01:00.62" data-timestamp="60.62">And so I remember getting on a flight and there was a spread for Vera Cruz, which was a state that was a beach state. It seemed fine, at least as far as we knew at that time.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:10.69" data-timestamp="70.69">Mm hm.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:01:11.71" data-timestamp="71.71">Then I started to sort of dig in a little deeper. And it turns out it was about to explode with all these killings of local journalists, with this whole sort of drug war episode that turned it into this complete just hotbed of violence.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:22.84" data-timestamp="82.84">And the ad actually sent you reporting on Vera Cruz?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:01:26.16" data-timestamp="86.16">It did, and Michoacan, too. Like, Michoacan is this state that produces most of the limes we get in the United States, all kinds of avocados. It's this agricultural, beautiful place.</p><p begin="00:01:37.25" data-timestamp="97.25">And the ad that I saw was emphasizing that. It was green. It was lush. It was this place that, you know, it looked gorgeous. It looked like the best parts of California that you would want to go visit. And I knew that it was a state that was a little bit troubled.</p><p begin="00:01:49.78" data-timestamp="109.78">But that ad sort of said to me, OK, it must be even more troubled than you think. And sure enough, when I got there, this was a state that became this meth capital and this hotbed of vigilantism. But when I saw the ads, that's when I knew, OK, now it's time to go.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:10.18" data-timestamp="130.18">That is, like, the least effective propaganda imaginable. You are getting exactly the opposite message from the ads than the thing they're trying to get across.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:02:19.82" data-timestamp="139.82">True. I mean, it became a joke among some of us-- other reporters in the office too-- of, well, this is clearly a place where we should start looking.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:29.15" data-timestamp="149.15">Damien never investigated why state after state would take out these ads. But he suspects they weren't really even targeted at tourists, these expensive photo spreads.</p><p begin="00:02:37.13" data-timestamp="157.13">He assumes the ads were made by government officials who were trying to reassure business people and local people -- "OK, everything is fine. We got it under control. Nothing to see here." The ads were for them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:02:47.93" data-timestamp="167.93">And a little bit for their own government. I think it's a way for the politicians to signal to their bosses, "hey, look, I'm working on this. I'm making sure that people are saying what they should be saying about my state.</p><p begin="00:02:59.67" data-timestamp="179.67">And it won't hurt you, Mr. President, or it won't hurt you, head of the party, because we're doing this for all of us together."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:06.41" data-timestamp="186.41">Damien has spent a lot of time also in Cuba. And he says in Cuba, like in Mexico, there are subtle meanings contained inside government messages, but only if you understand the nuances.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Damien Cave</h4><p begin="00:03:17.37" data-timestamp="197.37">In some ways, propaganda is like poetry. You need to know how to read it. And some people read it and see it only on one level. And then lots of other people see it and read it at a secondary level that's deeper and that may speak to the exact opposite of what it says on that first reading.</p><p begin="00:03:31.04" data-timestamp="211.04">The Cubans will read <i>Granma</i>, the main state organ, for all kinds of little things that they'll see in there that I won't see. And so the slightest mention of President Obama, and the degree to which the negativity is calibrated, they can see, oh, this is a sign that the government is warming up to the United States.</p><p begin="00:03:51.23" data-timestamp="231.23">Even if it's negative, by the degree of negativity, they know how to interpret that. It's complicated. It may seem really simple. But I think that people inside these countries are the best interpreters of their own propaganda.</p><p begin="00:04:03.84" data-timestamp="243.84">And the great irony, it seems to me, is the government, in an effort to tell its people what they think they need to hear, they often underestimate the people's ability to deconstruct it and understand it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:19.51" data-timestamp="259.51">Propaganda is like poetry. It is complexity in the form of simplicity, if you know how to read it. And today on our program, we have three stories that illustrate that principle-- one from China, one from South America, and one from right here in our own United States of America.</p><p begin="00:04:34.80" data-timestamp="274.8">Two of these stories are about propaganda that was so ingenious and worked so well, it's kind of incredible, actually. It's <i>This American Life</i> from WBEZ Chicago. I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us, comrades.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Guerrilla Marketing</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:55.10" data-timestamp="295.1">Act One, "Guerrilla Marketing." Well, the nation of Colombia has been at war for more than 50 years. And if you've followed this even a little bit, you know that the main rebel group fighting the government is called the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia. The Spanish acronym for that is FARC, spelled F-A-R-C.</p><p begin="00:05:12.11" data-timestamp="312.11">The FARC are a leftist guerrilla contingent, sometimes funding their operations through ransoms from kidnappings, or through drug running. Though it should be said that there have been rightist paramilitary forces aligned with the government who have been involved in similar activities.</p><p begin="00:05:26.23" data-timestamp="326.23">One byproduct of the war-- and I say this. I don't want to sound glib or insensitive. But really, the war has yielded a brand of propaganda that is so ambitious and ornate-- well, you'll hear. Producer Sean Cole explains.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:05:39.69" data-timestamp="339.69">And this particular propaganda campaign began about 10 years ago, when an advertising executive in Colombia named Jose Miguel Sokoloff was offered what had to be the most demanding account of his career.</p><p begin="00:05:51.65" data-timestamp="351.65">The client was the government of Colombia. The assignment, convince as many FARC guerrilla fighters as possible to demobilize, to quit the FARC. In short, please help us end this war.</p><p begin="00:06:05.48" data-timestamp="365.48">A word about Jose-- he's deeply principled and earnest. And if you Google image him, you'll see a lot of v-neck sweaters. His ad agency-- Lowe SSP3 it's called-- has been around since the mid '90s. His clients are not usually the defense ministries of South American countries.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jose Miguel Sokoloff</h4><p begin="00:06:21.81" data-timestamp="381.81">We just finished a beautiful project for Lifebuoy, which is an antibacterial soap in India.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:06:27.86" data-timestamp="387.86">They've done ads for dog food, bug repellent. But at the same time, he says, the fundamental mission, whether he's selling that stuff or trying to demobilize guerrillas, is still the same.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jose Miguel Sokoloff</h4><p begin="00:06:37.37" data-timestamp="397.37">I think about change, about how to change a behavior, how to change a perception. That's the business we're in. We're in the business of changing minds.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:06:48.34" data-timestamp="408.34">A word about the FARC guerrillas-- they're a heavily-armed fighting force of, at this moment, more than 7,000 rebel soldiers. They wear fatigues, live mainly in jungle encampments.</p><p begin="00:06:58.39" data-timestamp="418.39">They're what you think of when you think organized revolutionary militia. I should say, Jose and his company had done a little bit of political work before this. And Jose himself almost takes the conflict personally.</p><p begin="00:07:09.58" data-timestamp="429.58">It's been going on as long as he's been alive. But instead of getting rid of it, they, in fact, doubled down on it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:47:29.82" data-timestamp="2849.82">They doubled down, but not in the way you'd expect, not by getting even more shrill about the greatness of the nation and the happiness of the people. The party could see that people weren't buying it anymore.</p><p begin="00:47:41.83" data-timestamp="2861.83">And they weren't buying it because the truth was too stark. After four decades of communist rule, people were not as poor as they'd been, though they were still poor. But the thing that was really getting to them was the blatant corruption.</p><p begin="00:47:54.44" data-timestamp="2874.44">They couldn't help noticing that party members and their families kept getting richer and richer. For some in China, a dangerous idea had taken root, that maybe people elsewhere had it better.</p><p begin="00:48:08.22" data-timestamp="2888.22">What the government needed at this point was not some upbeat slogan, but a radical new approach to explaining why foreign notions were a trap and why communism was still the best path for China.</p><p begin="00:48:21.36" data-timestamp="2901.36">This new idea had to be something big. It couldn't admit mistakes or failures of any kind by communist leaders. And it would be great if this new idea had some truth in it, because truth is the magic ingredient in strong propaganda. What the party decided on was--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:48:39.88" data-timestamp="2919.88">Guochi, national humiliation. The term itself is wuwang guochi, never forget national humiliation.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:48:48.85" data-timestamp="2928.85">Humiliation, in this context, has a precise meaning.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:48:52.44" data-timestamp="2932.44">It means defeat at the hands of foreign armies. It means invasion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:48:58.63" data-timestamp="2938.63">It's hard to overestimate how shrewd this move by the party was, to build nationalism by focusing on the country's defeats. I think to Americans this idea makes no sense.</p><p begin="00:49:09.95" data-timestamp="2949.95">In the United States we occasionally talk about moments when we were underdogs-- the American Revolution, for instance. But that's only because we came out on top. In general, we see ourselves as a nation of righteous ass kickers. But we're a very young country.</p><p begin="00:49:27.19" data-timestamp="2967.19">And unlike China, we've never had the terrifying, humiliating experience of being invaded and occupied. China, in the 19th and 20th centuries, had been invaded multiple times, plundered by foreigners year after year after year.</p><p begin="00:49:43.07" data-timestamp="2983.07">National humiliation was not a new idea for China when the party reintroduced it in the 1990s. It was an old idea that had fallen out of fashion.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:49:52.30" data-timestamp="2992.3">They dusted off this old idea from Chinese history that went all the way back to the beginning of the 20th century. And they said here it is, humiliation. Humiliation has been marketed in China, in a way. Quite literally, it's been marketing.</p><p begin="00:50:10.10" data-timestamp="3010.1">At one point, in the '20s, there was a Chinese newspaper that was selling national humiliation towels so that people could wash their face every day and remember national humiliation. Because they're towel would remind them of that.</p><p begin="00:50:28.03" data-timestamp="3028.03">And in the '90s, in the years after Tienanmen Square, the whole apparatus of government was basically enlisted in service of making sure that young people were thinking about the ways in which the country had been harmed by foreigners over the course of their history.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:50:44.14" data-timestamp="3044.14">National humiliation became a central concept in China after 1991-- in educational materials, in films, in textbooks, including a brand new textbook called--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:50:55.29" data-timestamp="3055.29"><i>The</i> <i>Practical</i> <i>Dictionary</i> <i>of</i> <i>Patriotic</i> <i>Education.</i> And it included 355 pages on the subject of China's humiliations. So that included battles that China had lost, and places in which foreign soldiers had killed civilians, for instance.</p><p begin="00:51:14.93" data-timestamp="3074.93">And there were these terms that became very important. So for instance, one of the things that they taught, over and over again, was called the bainian guochi, or the century of national humiliation.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:51:26.67" data-timestamp="3086.67">The century of national humiliation was a name for the 100 years starting from the first Opium War, when Britain forced China to cede Hong Kong, up through the brutal Japanese occupation of parts of China before and during the Second World War.</p><p begin="00:51:42.28" data-timestamp="3102.28">To commemorate the century national humiliation, the Chinese government built museums and designated dozens of sites all over the country in the '90s, including many places of defeat and invasion.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:51:53.86" data-timestamp="3113.86">Places that, really, previously nobody had cared about, the sites of old battle and things. And they started turning these into sacred sites. And that was some of the language they used. They used this really religious language.</p><p begin="00:52:10.62" data-timestamp="3130.62">These became part of normal education. You would go there on field trips. For instance, students were expected to go to a certain number of field trips by the time they graduated from middle school to patriotic education sites.</p><p begin="00:52:26.52" data-timestamp="3146.52">You would go to places in China and you would see references to this subject of guochi, national humiliation. You'd see it inscribed, for instance, on the wall, at the front of a museum.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:52:40.01" data-timestamp="3160.01">Inside the Communist Party, it became a frenzy to see who could support the idea of national humiliation most vehemently, most perfectly.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:52:48.99" data-timestamp="3168.99">The National People's Congress approved a holiday called National Humiliation Day. But then there was too much enthusiasm. There were so many proposals in the Congress for which day that should be. And some people wanted it to be September 18.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:53:04.88" data-timestamp="3184.88">September 18, the beginning of Japan's occupation of Manchuria.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:53:08.66" data-timestamp="3188.66">And some people wanted it to be September 7.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:53:10.83" data-timestamp="3190.83">The day of defeat for a Chinese uprising against the British and others.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:53:14.71" data-timestamp="3194.71">And some people said no, no. It should be in June.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:53:19.05" data-timestamp="3199.05">In June there had been a massacre in the 1920s.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:53:21.63" data-timestamp="3201.63">They couldn't agree on one. And so they didn't end up instituting it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:53:28.49" data-timestamp="3208.49">Are we recording? Yes, we are.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:53:31.06" data-timestamp="3211.06">Great.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:53:31.72" data-timestamp="3211.72">I called a young Chinese woman named Shu Cao Mo. She's part of that first generation of Chinese students who were taught this new, post-Tienanmen curriculum.</p><p begin="00:53:41.47" data-timestamp="3221.47">Shu Cao left China for college. She came to the US, to Duke University. She still lives here. She remembers, as a kid, learning the key phrases, national humiliation, century of humiliation, how foreigners had invaded and abused China, until the Communist Party came to power in a great revolution.</p><p begin="00:54:02.32" data-timestamp="3242.32">Details of the revolution were omitted-- mass starvation, torture, ideological conformity at all costs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:54:10.33" data-timestamp="3250.33">It was really this sense of this humiliation, this century of humiliation, legitimizes revolution. And in the textbooks, what I kept learning was how we had those revolutionary heroes who are supposed to be our role models.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:54:33.07" data-timestamp="3273.07">These stories about revolutionary heroes appeared throughout the curriculum, often without using the words "national humiliation" at all. The phrase was only the most blunt, noticeable part of the campaign.</p><p begin="00:54:46.62" data-timestamp="3286.62">Shu Cao says the concept was embedded all over, especially in stories. In college, she got a scholarship to look at how nationalism is taught in children's books, including textbooks, in three places-- the US, Germany, and China.</p><p begin="00:55:01.69" data-timestamp="3301.69">For her project, she went back to China and looked at the books she had studied growing up. And she also fact checked some of the stories she remembered learning as a kid.</p><p begin="00:55:09.63" data-timestamp="3309.63">And it was a strange experience. One of these hero stories, she found out, had been modified dramatically in textbooks in order to amp up the humiliation. It was the story about the five martyrs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:55:23.44" data-timestamp="3323.44">Who jumped off the cliff in order to save the larger Chinese Army from the Japanese. In Chinese, we call it [CHINESE]. You sacrifice your small self in order to complete the larger whole.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:55:41.63" data-timestamp="3341.63">That's what that phrase means, mm hm?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:55:42.90" data-timestamp="3342.9">Yeah. And I had always thought that they were dead, because they had sacrificed their lives. And it was only two years ago, when I was doing that research, that I found out that half of them were still alive.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:55:58.17" data-timestamp="3358.17">It's definitely a better national humiliation story if all the martyrs died, not just some. Shu Cao remembered another story about a Chinese leader, Zhou Enlai, who as a teenager had seen a Chinese woman being bullied by foreign occupiers. And right then, witnessing that humiliation, he realized what he needed to do.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:56:19.43" data-timestamp="3379.43">Study for the rise of China. And that phrase was being repeated over and over again in that story. So if you ask any Chinese student, they would know the story and how that became his goal for his life, resurrecting China.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:56:37.92" data-timestamp="3397.92">Shu Cao hadn't gone to college with the intent of combing through her childhood education. But once she started, she realized how many questions she had built up over the years.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Shu Cao Mo</h4><p begin="00:56:47.61" data-timestamp="3407.61">Growing up in a well-educated family, I had always questioned what's written in my textbooks. I have always questioned what the authority tells me. So it's this slow process of always wondering what's not being said.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:57:11.67" data-timestamp="3431.67">What's not said is, of course, what's so ingenious about the whole campaign to revive national humiliation. By focusing the country's attention on foreign attacks, the party was diverting attention from its own errors and abuses.</p><p begin="00:57:24.06" data-timestamp="3444.06">It's a classic misdirect. And both Shu Cao and reporter Evan Osnos say it's worked pretty well, especially with the event that the government has wanted to obscure more than just about any other, Tiananmen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:57:38.25" data-timestamp="3458.25">Yeah. A lot of Chinese young people today, to the degree that they know about it-- and the truth is that a lot of Chinese young people know very little about what happened-- what they'll say is that the young people at Tiananmen were naive. And they were tempted by these Western ideas.</p><p begin="00:57:53.88" data-timestamp="3473.88">But those Western ideas aren't suitable for China, and that, had we gone down that path, we wouldn't be where we are today. We wouldn't be this rising superpower.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:58:03.28" data-timestamp="3483.28">So it was a humiliation averted, then, Tiananmen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Evan Osnos</h4><p begin="00:58:07.45" data-timestamp="3487.45">It was. They look at it and they really do say we chose the right path. And it would have been a disaster. So in that sense, the education system has done exactly what it was designed to do.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Nancy Updike</h4><p begin="00:58:22.91" data-timestamp="3502.91">In 2008, when the Olympics were coming to Beijing, there were demonstrations in China again-- lots of young people, like there were a lot of young people at Tiananmen.</p><p begin="00:58:33.09" data-timestamp="3513.09">But in 2008, what the demonstrators were outraged about was foreigners-- foreigners criticizing China in the run up to the Olympics. These protests were in support of the government.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:58:48.56" data-timestamp="3528.56">Nancy Updike is a producer for our program. Evan Osnos's book about China, <i>Age of</i> <i>Ambition,</i> won the National Book Award for 2014. It's out in paperback.</p></div>  </div>

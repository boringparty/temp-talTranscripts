  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.67" data-timestamp="0.67">Amy McGuiness flies people to the North Pole, tourists who pay thousands of dollars for the privilege. It's a six-hour flight in a prop jet. And for most of that time, if you look out the window, all you see is ice. When you get to the Pole, it looks exactly like all the other ice you've been staring at for hours.</p><p begin="00:00:17.68" data-timestamp="17.68">So the tourists climb out of the plane. They're only given half an hour at the Pole. And what do most of them do with the time? Take pictures. And most of them have brought these little global positioning devices, GPSs. And they spend their time looking at the devices which tell them that yes, this is, in fact, the North Pole. Here's Amy McGuiness.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:00:36.71" data-timestamp="36.71">Getting out of the plane and looking around, there'd be nothing to tell you that you're at the North Pole. But there's no such a thing, right?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:00:57.68" data-timestamp="57.68">No, and it seems ridiculous, and I laugh, but I've had passengers ask, &quot;Where's the barber pole?&quot;</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:06.33" data-timestamp="66.33">Oh really?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:01:07.48" data-timestamp="67.48">Yeah. But we usually, with the groups, we take up some kind of a fake barber pole and stick it in the ice somewhere, just for pictures' sake.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:15.40" data-timestamp="75.4">Oh my.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:01:15.87" data-timestamp="75.87">Yeah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:21.79" data-timestamp="81.79">There are the people who always want to know where they are in the world. They move through the world checking their position. And there's a kind of travel that we all do sometimes that's all about that moment of &quot;You are here.&quot; You check where you are. You check your position on the map. You take a snapshot. You move on.</p><p begin="00:01:39.93" data-timestamp="99.93">The North Pole is just an extreme example. Because, of course, there's nothing there, no one to meet, you're only there for half an hour. It costs $30,000 per person. And, until recently, a regular civilian would have no way to confirm that they were at the North Pole since normal compasses, like the one on the airplane that Amy McGuiness flies--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:01:58.97" data-timestamp="118.97">Because we're so close to the magnetic North Pole that it basically tips in the jar it's sitting in and is unreliable. But there is something lost there in always knowing exactly where you are.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:46.61" data-timestamp="166.61">Well, in a way, it's the opposite of adventure.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Amy Mcguiness</h4><p begin="00:02:50.36" data-timestamp="170.36">Sure, sure. From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:03:26.97" data-timestamp="206.97">Act One of our program today, Ishtar Days, Arabian Nights. A man tries to lose himself completely, leave the places charted on maps. And what happens to you, and what you find when you try to leave the known world like this and succeed.</p><p begin="00:03:42.51" data-timestamp="222.51">Act Two, The Game. Writer David Sedaris finds himself in a room full of people in a foreign city. He doesn't speak the language. He doesn't know anyone. One low-paid office worker tries to operate a global positioning apparatus on the map of her entire life. Stay with us, my friend.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Ishtar Days, Arabian Nights</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:17.02" data-timestamp="257.02">Act One, Ishtar Days, Arabian Nights. Back when he was 21, John Bowe decided to visit a friend of his who was in the Peace Corps in Mali, in Africa. But he chose, I have to say, the most difficult possible route to get there. He decided to hitchhike across the Sahara Desert. And not just hitchhike, but hitchhike using a remote route 1,000 miles long that most hitchhikers avoid. He brought no special gear, carried a small bag filled mostly with books, one change of clothes. He wanted to go to places on the map with fewer and fewer roads.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Bowe</h4><p begin="00:04:47.60" data-timestamp="287.6">And I don't know. When you travel a lot, when you're traveling and just sort of drifting around, you spend a lot of time looking at the map. And you exoticize it. And you get this weird relationship to it, where you start just thinking, &quot;Well, god, this was cool getting to this remote area. I bet  you it would be cooler to get to that area because it's that much more remote.&quot; You want to go off the map.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:11.48" data-timestamp="311.48">You want to go off the map? You want to go off the map, meaning you want to go off the area where the roads are on the map?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Bowe</h4><p begin="00:05:17.75" data-timestamp="317.75">Right. But you want to go off the map in a more metaphorical sense, too. You want to get off of the grid of American life, the grid of the modern world. You want to get totally away from white people and American techno living that's defined world culture.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:35.15" data-timestamp="335.15">How easy or how hard was it to get off the map?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Bowe</h4><p begin="00:05:38.67" data-timestamp="338.67">Well, that's one of the really interesting things. You can hop on a plane from anywhere to anywhere, hop on another little plane or train or something else, and then walk 20 feet and be in the middle of nowhere, which is one of the weirdest things about the modern world. You could take a plane probably from New York to Manaus in Brazil, and take a boat, and immediately be lost in the middle of the rainforest. And it would take you all of a day to get there.</p><p begin="00:06:12.52" data-timestamp="372.52">So the idea was to hitchhike across the Sahara Desert. And at first, I had had this idea because of my trusty Michelin map. They indicated that there was still a camel caravan that goes back and forth between southern Morocco and Timbuktu. And I kept asking people around, &quot;Is there still a camel caravan that does this thing?&quot; And people were kind of saying, yes, but only the people who were trying to sell me stuff, like, &quot;Yeah, well, but you're going to need a lot of stuff for the journey. Why don't you buy this $300 worth of stuff?&quot; So it's like, &quot;Well, let me just make sure there's a caravan first.&quot;</p><p begin="00:06:49.39" data-timestamp="409.39">And then it's funny because, later, I found out about some German kid who had actually bought $1,000 worth of gear. And they said, &quot;Oh yeah, just be here.&quot; They sold him a camel.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:58.21" data-timestamp="418.21">And just wait here.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Bowe</h4><p begin="00:06:59.39" data-timestamp="419.39">Right. Be here on the second Sunday of every month, and the guys all come. You can start your journey then. So that seemed like it wasn't going to happen.</p><p begin="00:07:19.52" data-timestamp="439.52">So then I went to Algeria, down to Tamanrasset, which is about a third of the way across the desert. And then the plan was to hitchhike from there for the rest of the way. And it's weird flying over the desert because, at least this day, there were no clouds.

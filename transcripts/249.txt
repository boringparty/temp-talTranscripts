  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.61" data-timestamp="0.61">It's like uncovering a vast secret conspiracy, but one that is right there in front of your eyes.</p><p begin="00:00:05.52" data-timestamp="5.52">5:30 in the morning.  It's still dark outside. And I'm on my way to record some New York City garbage men on their morning route. And the person I'm with, this anthropologist from New York University named Robin Nagle, who's spent years studying how New York City moves its trash. She points to a set of buildings-- these huge, corrugated metal barns-- right on the waterfront at 59th street. This is where the garbage trucks for the island of Manhattan come to dump paper onto barges to be taken away.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robin Nagle</h4><p begin="00:00:33.25" data-timestamp="33.25">I've seen photographs of this as a dumping point-- this exact location-- back dating to the 1890s, when it was wooden planks and horse-drawn carts, dumping directly into scows, where men were waiting with rakes to rake the garbage, which was usually steaming, because it often included ashes and things. And men died by falling off the scows, or getting kicked in the head by the horses, or falling through rotten boards in the planks.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:58.29" data-timestamp="58.29">There are no signs-- nothing to indicate that this is part of city government-- except for one city seal that is actually too faded to read. Which is not unusual.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robin Nagle</h4><p begin="00:01:08.13" data-timestamp="68.13">You'll see a lot of sanitation facilities-- there's no name over the door, there's nothing telling you it's a sanitation facility. The perception of sanitation in the public's eye is generally not great, and so it's easier if they can go below radar.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:25.22" data-timestamp="85.22">On maps of the city, you can see these piers, but it's not labeled what they are. Just like Fresh Kills on Staten Island, the largest landfill on the planet, for years has appeared on maps as a public park.</p><p begin="00:01:35.58" data-timestamp="95.58">People want everything having to do with garbage to be invisible. We want it out of sight. We don't want to think about it, don't want to be associated with it.</p><p begin="00:01:42.82" data-timestamp="102.82">One guy who I interviewed for today's radio program has a kind of high up job in the department of sanitation. Makes tons of money, lots of responsibility. And he got in contact after the interview to ask that I not use his name. His neighbors didn't know that he worked for the sanitation department, and he didn't want them to know.</p><p begin="00:02:00.61" data-timestamp="120.61">One sanitation worker told Robin that one day he was picking up a bag of trash and a lady's walking a poodle. And the lady lets the poodle start peeing on the bag as he's grabbing it. Like he's not even there.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robin Nagle</h4><p begin="00:02:12.26" data-timestamp="132.26">Sanitation workers will tell you that they're invisible. How can a pedestrian let her dog pee on a bag where a man is reaching for that bag and has his face not 18 inches from the dog who then lefts his leg to take a leak? That man must be invisible to that woman, that she would let that happen without giving it a second thought.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:33.47" data-timestamp="153.47">Today on our radio show, we take what's invisible and we make it visible. Stories of garbage, yours and mine. We live in a way that produces more waste than any people have in the history of civilization. We come here today not to feel bad about that, not to condemn that-- I know this is a public radio show, but I make a guarantee to you right now that this is not a radio program today about feeling bad about the immense volume of trash poking around the edges of all of our lives. No, no, no. This is a radio program about understanding the actual fact of it, finally.</p><p begin="00:03:11.60" data-timestamp="191.6">Our program today in three acts. Act One. Oh, Mr. San Man. In which we get our hands on your trash, yours, with the men who do it every day. Act Two. Except For the Smell, I Think I Have a Crush On You. There are squatters who build entire neighborhoods on top of rotting garbage heaps in Mexico. They scavenge in the trash piles for their living. We go there for a little love story. Act Three. I am a Legitimate Businessman... Waste Management. In that act, the secret recordings that ended mob control of New York garbage. We got them. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Oh, Mr. San Man</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:00.45" data-timestamp="240.45"> Act One. Oh, Mr. San Man.</p><p begin="00:04:03.67" data-timestamp="243.67">The government has a number where they take the amount of waste-- residential, commercial, industrial waste-- created in this country, and they divide it by how many of us there are to tell us how much garbage is produced per person by all of us just living our normal lives in this country. That number? 4.8 pounds per person per day.</p><p begin="00:04:27.36" data-timestamp="267.36">One rainy morning I wanted to see exactly what that means on the ground.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robin Nagle</h4><p begin="00:04:31.82" data-timestamp="271.82">We're doing this block, right?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:04:33.24" data-timestamp="273.24">No, the next block.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robin Nagle</h4><p begin="00:04:35.60" data-timestamp="275.6">I'll get on the running board.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:04:36.82" data-timestamp="276.82">OK. You sit there. I get on the other side.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:39.31" data-timestamp="279.31">For more than a year, Robin Nagle-- who you just heard from-- has been riding around with New York City sanitation workers, doing the job alongside them, as part of her research. She wears the green uniform. She slings trash.</p><p begin="00:04:51.83" data-timestamp="291.83">I went out with with her and two guys. Andre Ramos-- who reminds me a little bit of a Latino Billy Crystal-- in his early 40s, goatee, turning a little gray, chatty and outgoing. And his partner, an older, rather dignified man.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roger Patton</h4><p begin="00:05:05.91" data-timestamp="305.91"> My name is Roger Patton. But because my name is Patton, everyone on the job calls me General. But I'm not a general. I don't behave as one.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:17.56" data-timestamp="317.56">The General drives. Ramos rides the running board. And when they tell me the amount of trash that the two of them lift and throw into the back of the truck on their morning route, Ramos gives me the number in tons.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:05:28.21" data-timestamp="328.21">14.4 is our target weight, or better. 14.4.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:32.34" data-timestamp="332.34">So that means that you're personally picking up seven tons of garbage a day?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:05:36.99" data-timestamp="336.99">More or less, yes.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:40.04" data-timestamp="340.04">That seems crazy.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:05:41.30" data-timestamp="341.3">Really? Why?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:43.85" data-timestamp="343.85">Because it just doesn't seem like a person can even pick up--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:05:47.54" data-timestamp="347.54">After a while, all you see is black bags, and you just keep throwing it in there, throwing it in there. Until you fill this truck up.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:55.60" data-timestamp="355.6">The sheer athleticism that this requires is something that most of us don't think about, usually. When Ramos talks about what it was like to first do this job, throw trash all day for a living, he sighs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:06:06.94" data-timestamp="366.94">It was hard. It really hard. Because you're not used to the lifting, the dragging, the pulling. Everything is different.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roger Patton</h4><p begin="00:06:18.29" data-timestamp="378.29"> Because on this job, everyone hurts. Your back, your shoulders, your legs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:06:23.94" data-timestamp="383.94"> My body, we saw plenty of Tiger Balm, plenty of Ben-Gay.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:29.40" data-timestamp="389.4">So how many months did it take before you were able to lift comfortably, and at the end of the day, you weren't wiped out?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andre Ramos</h4><p begin="00:06:34.34" data-timestamp="394.34">I tell you, it took more than a few months. It took the years. It really did. Yes. Because this is something, you know, you've got to tailor your body into. It's not something that you get used to overnight, no matter if you were a hard worker all your life.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:48.46" data-timestamp="408.46">As with a lot of physically demanding jobs, it's all about pacing yourself, conserving your energy where you can.</p><p begin="00:06:54.17" data-timestamp="414.17">A typical stop. The General pulls up the truck to 30 trash bags set out in front of an apartment building on the Upper West Side. He tries to get the truck close to the bags.</p><p begin="00:07:03.41" data-timestamp="423.41">Most of the bags are at 60, maybe 70 pounds. Small enough for Ramos or the General or Robin to carry alone. Some buildings use these big, black sausage bags packed by compactors.

  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.47" data-timestamp="0.47">When he was 15, Lee was living illegally in the United States. He'd come over from China, and he worked in a series of Chinese restaurants in small towns in Pennsylvania, Ohio, New York. He didn't speak English, though he wanted to. He didn't go to school, though he wanted to. Instead, he and the other illegal immigrants working in these restaurants lived in tiny apartments, sometimes in the back of the restaurants, sleeping on bunk beds, working 12 hours a day, six days a week.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lee Qi</h4><p begin="00:00:25.01" data-timestamp="25.01"> The one thing that I can tell you is that we had really little contact with other people, you know what I mean?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:33.30" data-timestamp="33.3"> The thing that must have been so strange, though, is that you're working in a restaurant, and so you would see all these people come in, and you would see families with kids and people your own age. And it must've just seemed like they were on the other side of some sort of wall that you could never climb over.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lee Qi</h4><p begin="00:00:49.94" data-timestamp="49.94"> Yeah. I feel like it's a different world. It's like people don't even see you sometimes, if you're working behind a restaurant. Sometimes you feel mad, you feel angry, that you can't be like them. And then you try to think about why. I can't find a reason.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:11.43" data-timestamp="71.43"> We talked about this a while. And finally, I told Lee that it seemed like he was living in a kind of limbo-- not his old life in China, not a real American life. And he agreed.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lee Qi</h4><p begin="00:01:21.21" data-timestamp="81.21"> Limbo, yeah. Right between, somewhere in between, and you don't know where it is, actually.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:27.18" data-timestamp="87.18">You know, the idea of limbo comes from the church. Yeah.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:41.40" data-timestamp="101.4"> Which country is the heaven and which country is the hell?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lee Qi</h4><p begin="00:01:46.99" data-timestamp="106.99"> Well, I don't know if you've heard-- there's a TV show in China. Beijing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lee Qi</h4><p begin="00:02:00.08" data-timestamp="120.08"> Beijing. OK. So it's the title of the TV show. And every time the TV show comes out, every time an episode comes out, there's a code. So if you like the person, send the person to New York, because it is heaven. If you hate a person, also send the person to New York, because it is hell. Every time you see that episode, you see this code. [UNINTELLIGIBLE PHRASE] I thought it was fun.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:30.70" data-timestamp="150.7"> Yes, America can be heaven. It can be hell.   <h3>Act One: The Family That Flees Together, Trees Together</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:28.52" data-timestamp="208.52">Act One. This is the story of a family that drops out entirely from society, goes on the run after they get in trouble with the law. Two parents, six kids, no money, making do as they can for seven years. The family is called the Jarvises. <i>This</i> <i>American Life</i> producer Alex Blumberg visited them in Florida.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:03:53.50" data-timestamp="233.5"> Here's a typical scene in the life of the Jarvis family. Rescue the poor thing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jarvis Son</h4><p begin="00:04:06.13" data-timestamp="246.13">Poor thing, eh?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anna Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:04:07.05" data-timestamp="247.05"> [? James, ?] just reach your hand down there and pull it out.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jarvis Son</h4><p begin="00:04:09.93" data-timestamp="249.93">No, I don't want to do that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Cy Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:04:12.12" data-timestamp="252.12">Well, he just keeps whipping so fast.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anna Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:04:12.93" data-timestamp="252.93"> Stick your hand in his mouth and see what he does.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:04:15.27" data-timestamp="255.27"> That's Anna, 14, the youngest girl, advising her brother Cy, 21, to stick his hand in the alligator's mouth. Come on, there, gator, just relax. Just relax.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:04:45.05" data-timestamp="285.05"> For seven years, the Jarvis family lived on the run-- in a treehouse in a cypress swamp, in the rotting hull of a boat, anywhere they could hide. This is the mom, Eileen Jarvis.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Eileen Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:05:12.65" data-timestamp="312.65"> We just wanted to make our own little world, really, and have our handle on it. How so?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Eileen Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:05:27.17" data-timestamp="327.17">They had 140 acres plus to run around and play in and they used every bit of it. They had a treehouse or a fort in every tree.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:05:42.75" data-timestamp="342.75"> Eileen and her husband Ron were '60s back-to-the-land dropouts who'd actually stayed dropped out. Even the crops they grew were the same, which in 1992 became a problem when a helicopter spotted a marijuana patch Ron was growing on the property.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Eileen Jarvis</h4><p begin="00:06:04.62" data-timestamp="364.62"> My son Asa was going to cut the grass and he had the lawn mower on, and I had dinner cooking. And Asa came running in the house and he said, &quot;Dad said to get in the van.&quot; So we just left-- left the stove on, the pressure cooker on. Dinner in the-- coffee, I had just made a pot of coffee and poured it up. And we took nothing with us. The kids didn't have shoes on their feet. We just walked out of the house and that was it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:06:47.60" data-timestamp="407.6"> If you ask Ron Jarvis about the marijuana patch today, he says he planted it earlier in the year, right after his wife was diagnosed with cervical cancer, to sell and pay for her treatments.

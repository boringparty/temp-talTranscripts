  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.96" data-timestamp="0.96">As a species, something drives us to want to make inanimate things seem alive. There are statues where the whole idea seems to be to create a simulation of soft skin and sheer billowing cloth out of rock-- literally, out of a rock-- as if to prove this, too, can be human. There is just something about that moment when Pinocchio stops being a few strung together pieces of wood and starts to seem like he's really a little boy.</p><p begin="00:00:27.68" data-timestamp="27.68">This week I caught a guy named Ronn Lucas, a ventriloquist who is best known for being able to make anything talk-- a banana, a box of breakfast cereal, a sock-- to ask him what he makes of all this. His dummies are named Buffalo Billy and Scorch.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ronn Lucas</h4><p begin="00:00:42.98" data-timestamp="42.98">When I'm on stage talking to my puppets, I believe they're real. I believe to the point, as an actor believes in his character.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:50.86" data-timestamp="50.86">Charles Ludlam, of the Ridiculous Theater, had a dummy called Walter. And he wrote at one point about Walter, he sometimes says unexpected things. Does that happen with you, with Billy or Scorch?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ronn Lucas</h4><p begin="00:01:03.79" data-timestamp="63.79">It has happened to me. There are a couple moments, too. I started to get through a breakthrough as a comedian. I was working a series of comedy clubs. And someone asked me if I would do a military club. And I said, yeah, sure, not knowing what I was getting into. I worked for the enlisted men. And they were just really into what I was doing.</p><p begin="00:01:19.51" data-timestamp="79.51">But then I went over to the officer's club. And these guys were wild. I guess it's part of the responsibility of command, is that when you get to hang out just with other officers, you can let yourself go. But performing, I couldn't do my script. I couldn't do the show I had planned. So I just started doing question and answer stuff. Don Rickles, ethnic jokes, nothing that would really hurt anybody. But I was picking on everybody equally.</p><p begin="00:01:43.82" data-timestamp="103.82">I had to preface that before I got to where I-- So one guy was an officer. And he was from Puerto Rico. I said hey, where are you from? And the guy said, Puerto Rico. And the dummy goes, how many Puerto Ricans does it take to screw in a light bulb? And I'm thinking to myself, why did I have him say that? I don't have an answer.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:04.89" data-timestamp="124.89">Then, in a wink, the dummy came up with an answer. One funny enough to make an audience laugh in an officer's nightclub. But I have to say, not so funny that I want to alienate our lovely multicultural audience.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:22.12" data-timestamp="142.12">And why is it so pleasurable for something inanimate to suddenly become alive through a voice coming out of it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ronn Lucas</h4><p begin="00:02:29.25" data-timestamp="149.25">It is fascinating to hold a fork in your hand and wonder what it would say. I found myself doing that not too long ago around a table with some other ventriloquists.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:39.87" data-timestamp="159.87">And what did it say?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ronn Lucas</h4><p begin="00:02:41.07" data-timestamp="161.07">And it said, your thumb is on my butt.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:44.32" data-timestamp="164.32">Right. Of course. Exactly.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ronn Lucas</h4><p begin="00:02:48.08" data-timestamp="168.08">I treated it more like a person. I didn't think of it as a utility, or what it was going to do. I saw it as a human form. And my thumb was in the wrong place.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:01.21" data-timestamp="181.21">Which is exactly the point. Every object Ronn Lucas makes speak, he pretends is a person. This is exactly what we're doing as a species all the time. Turning things into people. And it begins when we're children, playing with dolls. That's true for men and women. Today on our program, what that's all about.</p><p begin="00:03:21.90" data-timestamp="201.9">From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Today's show, stories of dolls, dolls of all sorts, and what is it all about?</p><p begin="00:03:37.07" data-timestamp="217.07">Act One, Thank Heaven for Little Girls-- Made of Plastic. The story of a company that has figured out all the different ways that girls like dolls, and is trying to sell to nearly every single way they can think of. Act Two, You Know What Mr. Bear Would Say. The author of a once-famous series of children's books about a doll, and how her life came to resemble a doll's. Act Three, The Boy Who Would Be a Helicopter. Kindergarten teacher Vivian Paley tells the story of how, in this age when schools are eliminating the doll corner at younger and younger ages, it was playing with dolls that saved a kid in her class. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Thank Heaven For Little Girls — Made Of Plastic</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:22.31" data-timestamp="262.31">Act One, Thank Heaven for Little Girls-- Made of Plastic. About a year ago, here in Chicago, just around the corner from all the fanciest stores that you can find for a three state radius-- Niketown, and the Sony Gallery, and Neiman Marcus, and all those kind-- a company began an experiment in marketing dolls to little girls. They opened a store, the only one that they have opened anywhere. <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> producer Susan Burton headed down to American Girl Place.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Susan Burton</h4><p begin="00:04:48.33" data-timestamp="288.33">It's American Girl Place, not American Girl store. The word place was what thrilled me before I had ever set foot inside. It seemed to connote a project of such grand scale. Build a monument on the Magnificent Mile, and they will come.</p><p begin="00:05:03.58" data-timestamp="303.58">You see them on Saturday afternoons downtown, swarms of little girls. What you notice first is their shopping bags, which are bright, Crayola marker red with American Girl Place stamped in white across the side. Austere, grown-up looking bags. Even if the bags are large, banging against their legs, the girls tend to carry them by themselves.</p><p begin="00:05:24.75" data-timestamp="324.75">In good coats and tights and Mary Janes, they look like little girls pretending to be little girls. The question of what to wear to American Girl Place has clearly been given careful consideration. Some girls are dressed like their dolls.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Whitney Robertson</h4><p begin="00:05:39.90" data-timestamp="339.9">I have the purple 2000 millennium outfit. There are purple tights that  are sparkly and a purple skirt that goes down to about my knees. I have a purple tank top with a purple cardigan.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Susan Burton</h4><p begin="00:05:55.41" data-timestamp="355.41">Whitney Robertson is 11 years old and came here from Saint Louis with her mother, Laura. She has short blond hair, braces. She moves with a heightened formality. It's as if she is wearing something magical and doesn't dare disturb the spell.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Laura Robertson</h4><p begin="00:06:09.54" data-timestamp="369.54">The most interesting thing is when she got her braces this year at Christmas time, she had to have purple-- what do you call them, spacers?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Whitney Robertson</h4><p begin="00:06:18.62" data-timestamp="378.62">The purple rubber bands. Because I got my braces a few days after Christmas, so I knew I got the 2000 outfit. So that's the only reason I got purple. Otherwise would have gotten my favorite color, blue.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Laura Robertson</h4><p begin="00:06:30.38" data-timestamp="390.38">So she was totally American Girl.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Susan Burton</h4><p begin="00:06:32.99" data-timestamp="392.99">Whitney and her mother have come to American Girl Place, not to Chicago, in the same way that you go to Disney World, rather than to Orlando. This is Whitney's first time here. Her parents gave her the trip for Christmas.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Whitney Robertson</h4><p begin="00:06:45.32" data-timestamp="405.32">There was a series of presents. The first one was a necklace that said, northern expressions. And I really didn't get it at the time, but it was a clue there we go north from Saint Louis. And then the next one was something from AG. I think it was a necklace.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Susan Burton</h4><p begin="00:07:01.63" data-timestamp="421.63">AG. American Girl.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Whitney Robertson</h4><p begin="00:07:03.93" data-timestamp="423.93">And then the next one was airline tickets to the AG Place.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Laura Robertson</h4><p begin="00:07:06.69" data-timestamp="426.69">Whitney had this questioning look on her face, like, what? And then it dawned on her. And she just went, oh my gosh, and started to cry.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Whitney Robertson</h4><p begin="00:07:14.85" data-timestamp="434.85">True.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Susan Burton</h4><p begin="00:07:16.40" data-timestamp="436.4">In the store's first year, about 40% of it's million visitors have come from out of town. This is retail as theme park. Destination shopping. There is a concierge desk, coat checks on both the first and third floors. There is a cafe, and a clothing department, a museum, and a live musical, loads of furniture and accessories, shelves of souvenirs, and, of course, the dolls themselves, which cost $82 each.</p><p begin="00:07:44.60" data-timestamp="464.6">A former school teacher with a storybook name, Pleasant Rowland, thought up the American Girl dolls about 15 years ago. There are six historic dolls.

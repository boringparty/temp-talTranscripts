  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:12.05" data-timestamp="12.05"> I've had a bunch of bike accidents in the last year and a half. And the moment that things go wrong is always the same. These accidents happen during my morning commute. And they always happen when it rains.</p><p begin="00:00:21.79" data-timestamp="21.79">I have this very light bicycle I got. And when it rains, something about this bike on the city streets-- a metal grate, a slippery patch of any sort at all, can make the bike slip out from under me. I have broken a clavicle. I banged up my wrist. I've given myself some kind of bruise on my right arm that still wakes me up at night, months later.</p><p begin="00:00:42.46" data-timestamp="42.46">And in each of these three accidents-- you know, like, when you're falling, there's that second or two when you realize you're going down, and you're on your way down, but you haven't actually hit the ground yet? Weirdly, it is enough time for a bunch of thoughts to go through your head. And all three times, like I say, it's been the same. Somehow I get very calm and I think, oh, this isn't going to be so bad. I'm not going very fast. The ground isn't that far. Is a car coming? OK, no car is coming. I figured out how to fall. It's going to be fine-- this time.</p><p begin="00:01:12.16" data-timestamp="72.16">And I think everybody knows how a person reacts under duress, it can tell you something about them. And I think what this set of thoughts says about me is I am optimistic even when there is no factual basis for it at all. Like, at the exact moment when I'm going to collide with the wet surface of the street, a moment when things are definitely not going to be fine, I'm thinking, things will be fine.</p><p begin="00:01:35.89" data-timestamp="95.89">I think this optimism has kept me in faltering relationships. When I mentioned all this at our radio show story meeting this week, one of my coworkers, somebody who I've worked with closely for years, told me-- he has never said this-- that he thinks my optimism is the single most important quality I have. He said he'll tell me there are 17 things that can go wrong with some plan that we have. And I'll just go, eh, let's go for it. Though I have to say, after three accidents, I have learned not to ride this particular bike in the rain. Thank you very much.</p><p begin="00:02:04.96" data-timestamp="124.96">Today on our program, we have stories where things go very, very badly for people. They are on the way down and in that moment of crisis. What they're thinking reveals so much about who they are. Some people, it leads to new insights. Some, it leads to no insights at all. And some are hit with a lightning bolt of inspiration and figure out how to save themselves. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Pirates of the Cari-BEAN-TOWN</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:41.15" data-timestamp="161.15"> Act One, "Pirates of the Cari-BEAN-TOWN." One summer, a while back, a group of friends got in trouble, got in over their heads, almost literally, in the middle of Boston Harbor. Our producer, Ike Sriskandarajah, tells the tale-- though, for the purposes of today's story, call him Ishmael.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ike Sriskandarajah</h4><p begin="00:02:59.64" data-timestamp="179.64"> I've been friends with you this whole time and I didn't know that this is your most told story.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sophie Tintori</h4><p begin="00:03:04.89" data-timestamp="184.89"> Well, I wouldn't tell it to you. You were there.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ike Sriskandarajah</h4><p begin="00:03:07.24" data-timestamp="187.24"> I was there. It was over 10 years ago and I was a new resident in the Boston area. My good friend, Sophie Tintori, introduced me to some people she knew well. We liked the same kinds of jokes and could only afford the same kind of rent. So we moved into a big house together. And soon, we got to the stage in any relationship where someone suggests taking things to the next level.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sophie Tintori</h4><p begin="00:03:33.44" data-timestamp="213.44"> So I don't know exactly how it came up, but maybe eight friends or so decided to go in, collectively, and buy a used boat. And to my understanding, none of them knew a single thing about boats.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ike Sriskandarajah</h4><p begin="00:03:46.48" data-timestamp="226.48"> True. But did you know the word "catamaran" comes from Tamil? Kattu maram, "wood tied together." Those are my guys. So that's one thing I know about boats. And maybe more ancestral knowledge was just waiting to surface if I owned a boat.</p><p begin="00:04:05.80" data-timestamp="245.8">Here's how it happened. Our friend Max brought us the opportunity. He knew a person who knew a person who needed to get rid of their boat. For $400 each, Max could make us Massachusetts boat people. Having double that in my bank account, I signed a check, sight unseen. And when I finally got to meet our boat down at the harbor, it felt like we had gotten away with larceny.</p><p begin="00:04:35.02" data-timestamp="275.02">I wish you could have seen it, a 1950s Hiliner, a glamorous little wooden motorboat with an engine hanging off the back, polished wood, two rows of white vinyl seats, chrome finishes. Think Italian vacation, like <i>The</i> <i>Talented</i> <i>Mr</i> <i>Ripley,</i> but right here on the Boston Riviera. Also, like Boston's own Matt Damon in <i>The</i> <i>Talented</i> <i>Mr</i> <i>Ripley,</i> a new high-society life was waiting for us.</p><p begin="00:05:12.55" data-timestamp="312.55">The first time I got on the boat, it really felt like magic. We honked just the right number of times and a bridge opened up for us like a bouncer unclipping a red velvet rope to the VIP section. We entered the Charles River and stopped to admire the sailboats swirling around us.</p><p begin="00:05:32.68" data-timestamp="332.68">A small motorboat pulled up to us. The man asked if we were stranded.</p><p begin="00:05:37.66" data-timestamp="337.66">No, sir, just enjoying this beautiful day. Thanks for asking.</p><p begin="00:05:42.43" data-timestamp="342.43">Well, you know, you're parked in the middle of a regatta.</p><p begin="00:05:47.23" data-timestamp="347.23">Oh.</p><p begin="00:05:49.15" data-timestamp="349.15">He looked us over. Do you guys work for Google?</p><p begin="00:05:53.86" data-timestamp="353.86">The plan to cosplay our way to high society was already working.</p><p begin="00:06:07.39" data-timestamp="367.39">Max knew the next step. We had to announce our old boat as the newest member of Boston's watery glitterati, which meant a christening party. Max had been practicing for this moment his whole life. The man loves extravagant gestures.</p><p begin="00:06:25.83" data-timestamp="385.83">He invited friends from near and far to the Charles River Esplanade and asked everyone to wear white. He showed up in black, a rented black tuxedo. Max stood on the front of the boat, named it <i>Marjorie,</i> after his own mother, and smashed a purple bottle of Andre sparkling wine on the nose.</p><p begin="00:06:54.83" data-timestamp="414.83">Now I don't know if you believe in curses, and we didn't know it right away, but Max invoked roughly three curses in that moment. Wearing a black tuxedo to a white party of your own design, that's more of a faux pas, not a curse. But number one, renaming a boat, that's a curse. Number two, Jews like Max don't name children after living relatives. Now, the prohibition doesn't specify boats, so let's call that a half curse. Number three, smashing champagne on a used boat-- I heard that's a curse, but only if you consider Andre sparkling wine to be champagne.</p><p begin="00:07:42.37" data-timestamp="462.37">Now it's impossible to say which curse attached itself to the <i>Marjorie.</i> But moments later, the sky darkened and we all scattered as sheets of rain fell upon us.</p><p begin="00:08:04.51" data-timestamp="484.51">The next day, the clouds cleared. Our friends were still in town so we tried to take them out again. Now this is where the story really begins.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sophie Tintori</h4><p begin="00:08:15.67" data-timestamp="495.67"> It was a beautiful day. I think it was early summer and really sunny and gorgeous. And there were maybe 12-- 12 of us on the boat? Does that sound right?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ike Sriskandarajah</h4><p begin="00:08:28.69" data-timestamp="508.69"> Nine in a boat that was maybe made for six. But our captain, Max, had just completed a boat safety course. And he told us the only limit for boat capacity is having enough life jackets for everyone, which isn't true. But we didn't know that and Max denies ever saying it. Squished together, two in the front seat, four in the back, three others perched on the sides, we headed out.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sophie Tintori</h4><p begin="00:08:58.89" data-timestamp="538.89"> People are taking turns driving, which is really fun. I got to take the wheel for a little bit. And I was cutting across the waves, in a way, where we'd like catch a little bit of air and slam down and catch some air and slam down, which I thought was very fun. And then at one point, somebody leaned over and said to me, Sophie, this is a wooden lake boat.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ike Sriskandarajah</h4><p begin="00:09:19.50" data-timestamp="559.5"> The <i>Marjorie</i> wasn't built for high-octane thrills. Someone says, we should get back soon. But Sophie overrules them. And then, just like the American munitions that rebelled British ships in these very waters-- cannonball!</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sophie Tintori</h4><p begin="00:09:36.92" data-timestamp="576.92"> I jump off the boat because it's a beautiful day and I like to swim.

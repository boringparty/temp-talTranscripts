  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.67" data-timestamp="0.67">Today, we have this story for you about cars-- American cars. And like a lot of stories about American cars, this one is more tragedy than comedy, although there is some comedy in there, too. The good news, of course, about American cars is that American cars are getting better, especially in the last few years. You've probably heard that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michelle Krebs</h4><p begin="00:00:16.53" data-timestamp="16.53">Well, at Ford, the Ford Fusion-- <i>Consumer</i> <i>Reports</i> named it a better car than the Toyota Camry and Honda Accord a few years ago. I think that was a major turning point.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:28.74" data-timestamp="28.74">Michelle Krebs has been covering the auto industry for 35 years. She's now at autotrader.com. She says the reason American cars finally improved--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michelle Krebs</h4><p begin="00:00:36.93" data-timestamp="36.93">I think when you're on the brink of possible extinction, it wakes people up, and they started changing things. But that's what it took. I mean, that's why Ford came out with the Ford Fusion. They spent $23 billion-- borrowed $23 billion, because they were almost out of business.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:58.35" data-timestamp="58.35">So basically, it's deathbed conversions.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michelle Krebs</h4><p begin="00:01:01.11" data-timestamp="61.11">That's a good way to describe it. They decided, we have got to bet the ranch on building great cars that people really want to buy. They're still not all the way there yet. I mean, I think that's the simple fact of the matter. There's been progress, but it's been spotty.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:18.53" data-timestamp="78.53">How spotty? When <i>Consumer</i> <i>Reports</i> rated the top 10 most reliable car brands this year, only one was American. 9 were foreign. 4 of the top 5, no surprise, were Japanese-- Toyota, Honda, Mazda, and Lexus.</p><p begin="00:01:32.99" data-timestamp="92.99">Why are most American cars still not as good as foreign cars? Why is it still taking Detroit so long to learn this lesson? It's not like this reliability problem just snuck up on them, right?</p><p begin="00:01:44.76" data-timestamp="104.76">General Motors, the biggest American car maker, started losing market share 50 years ago. Back in the 1960s, GM sold over half the cars in the United States, and then that number just slid lower and lower and lower for decades, until now, it's 18% of all cars sold in the US.</p><p begin="00:02:03.16" data-timestamp="123.16">Today's program came out of a question that we had here at the radio show. We genuinely did not understand why American car makers waited until they were nearly extinct-- two of them bankrupt, one of them nearly bankrupt. Why didn't they get on the ball and start making reliable cars that could compete with the Japanese decades ago?</p><p begin="00:02:20.27" data-timestamp="140.27">It seemed like, OK, you're running a business. You're losing sales in such a huge way. Why wouldn't you change? It really made no sense.</p><p begin="00:02:28.17" data-timestamp="148.17">And we looked around for an explanation, and we learned about this one car factory in California. Here.</p><p begin="00:02:38.87" data-timestamp="158.87">What happened at this factory not only illustrates some very interesting answers to that question, it captures a lot of what went wrong in the American car business in the last 35 years. This is a plant in Fremont, California, called NUMMI. NUMMI stands for New United Motor Manufacturing Incorporated.</p><p begin="00:02:58.99" data-timestamp="178.99">A modern car plant, by the way, sounds a lot like a casino. The automated gear plays these little songs to let you know that it's running. Driverless robotic carts glide up and down the aisles like R2D2 delivering doors and other parts. Their song is supposed to warn you that they're coming.</p><p begin="00:03:16.79" data-timestamp="196.79">The story of this particular factory is actually a famous story among car people. It's taught at business schools. NUMMI was opened in 1984 by GM and Toyota together. The two companies decided that this would be a place that they would build cars with each other.</p><p begin="00:03:32.56" data-timestamp="212.56">And if it seems strange to you that two competitors would do this, it seemed strange at the time, also. A headline in <i>Car</i> <i>and</i> <i>Driver</i> about the partnership read, "Hell Freezes Over." But both companies had their reasons. We'll get to those.</p><p begin="00:03:45.08" data-timestamp="225.08">At NUMMI, Toyota showed GM all of its secrets. Showed it exactly how it made some of the best-built, most reliable cars in the world back then. GM, at the time, hoped to take these lessons from NUMMI and carry them into all their other plants, all over the country and over the world. That was the 1980s.</p><p begin="00:04:03.85" data-timestamp="243.85">And then, well, you know, it didn't go well. GM was one of the car makers who went bankrupt. US taxpayers, you and I, we bailed them out for $50 billion.</p><p begin="00:04:13.56" data-timestamp="253.56">And NUMMI closed. This GM plant that seemed like it might be able to teach American car makers how to catch up to their competitors-- it stopped making cars for GM. The sounds that you're hearing right now-- they were recorded in 2010, in the last months before GM shuttered the place, on April Fools' Day that year.</p><p begin="00:04:30.34" data-timestamp="270.34">Back then, we put together this story that we're about to play for you today. For a few months before NUMMI closed, Frank Langfitt, who at the time was NPR's automotive correspondent, and one of our producers, Brian Reed, travelled around the country talking to GM workers and managers about what had happened at NUMMI. General Motors and the UAW-- they are secretive cultures. But as you're going to hear, people were blunt when they talked to Frank about this. They were blunt in a way that you rarely hear with auto executives and UAW reps when they're being interviewed by the press.</p><p begin="00:04:58.94" data-timestamp="298.94">Act One of our show is going to be about the rise of NUMMI. Act Two is where we get the answer to our question-- what prevented GM from learning from NUMMI for so many years? For WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Our show today produced with our colleagues at NPR News.</p><p begin="00:05:14.69" data-timestamp="314.69">And now it is my pleasure to turn the story over to Frank Langfitt.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Frank Langfitt</h4><p begin="00:05:18.62" data-timestamp="318.62">When NUMMI was conceived, General Motors was far and away the world's largest car company. Its US market share was seven times that of Toyota. But each company had a problem that the other company could help them solve.</p><p begin="00:05:31.21" data-timestamp="331.21">On GM's side, the problem was small cars. Under government emission guidelines, General Motors had to build them, but they always lost money for GM, and they were lousy quality. So Toyota suggested a joint venture that offered two things. First, Toyota would build a quality small car for GM-- one that would finally turn a profit.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jeffrey Liker</h4><p begin="00:05:51.50" data-timestamp="351.5">And the second thing was, we have this thing called the Toyota production system, and it produces the quality you see. We could teach you the Toyota production system.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Frank Langfitt</h4><p begin="00:06:00.41" data-timestamp="360.41">Jeffrey Liker is a professor of industrial engineering at the University of Michigan. He's been studying Japanese auto manufacturing, including NUMMI, since the 1980s, and wrote a book about it called <i>The</i> <i>Toyota</i> <i>Way</i>. He says even though Toyota was offering GM something quite remarkable-- to teach a competitor its secrets, to show them exactly how Toyota achieved such phenomenal reliability-- GM wasn't that interested.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jeffrey Liker</h4><p begin="00:06:25.59" data-timestamp="385.59">Some people in GM, I think, had some foresight to realize this is important. I think for many of the senior leaders, it was more, OK, well, maybe I'll learn something, maybe I won't, but it won't hurt.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Frank Langfitt</h4><p begin="00:06:37.54" data-timestamp="397.54">Why wouldn't they be really interested in the Toyota production system? Because at that point, wasn't Toyota building higher quality cars?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jeffrey Liker</h4><p begin="00:06:44.36" data-timestamp="404.36">Toyota was building higher quality cars. I'm not sure it was 100% accepted at that time by senior management that Japanese quality was really better.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Frank Langfitt</h4><p begin="00:06:53.23" data-timestamp="413.23">Why?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jeffrey Liker</h4><p begin="00:06:53.87" data-timestamp="413.87">I think there was pride and defensiveness. I'm proud because I'm the biggest automaker in the world. I've been the best. I've dominated the market. You can't teach me anything, you little Japanese company.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Shook</h4><p begin="00:07:13.59" data-timestamp="433.59">I'm John Shook. Toyota and Toyota City was completely Japanese when I joined them. I was the first non-Japanese to join them as a regular employee in Toyota City.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Frank Langfitt</h4><p begin="00:07:23.70" data-timestamp="443.7">John Shook moved to Japan in the early 1980s because of his fascination with Japanese management techniques. He read books about them back home in east Tennessee. And he decided to fly halfway around the globe, learn to speak the language, and hunt for a job with the biggest Japanese company that would have him. Toyota hired him to train American workers in the joint venture.</p><p begin="00:07:43.40" data-timestamp="463.4">Now, here was the problem that pushed Toyota into the deal. They needed to learn how to build cars in America. For years, the Japanese had been winning away customers from Detroit-- so many that now the US Congress was threatening to restrict car imports. Toyota could avoid that by making cars in the US.</p><p begin="00:08:00.37" data-timestamp="480.37">But they wanted a partner.

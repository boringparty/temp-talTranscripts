    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:11.49" data-timestamp="11.49"><i></i> <i></i> <i></i> OK, so you call a hotline, and then a complete stranger tries to figure out how to help you, on the spot. That idea seems to have begun in the 1950s. The first suicide hotline in the United States was created in the early '60s by a guy in San Francisco, who was a priest and also a journalist. And it was just him answering the phone at first.</p><p begin="00:00:35.23" data-timestamp="35.23">Ads on matchbooks and sides of buses said, "Thinking of ending it all? Call Bruce," which, by the way, was not his real name. His real name was Bernard Mayes. But of course, the power of anonymity is so important to any hotline. People would call. And sometimes he could help them precisely because he had no connection to their life at all. Like, they could say anything to him. In those pre-internet days, that was completely new, to harness that kind of anonymity, the intimacy of it, this way, over the phone.</p><p begin="00:01:10.40" data-timestamp="70.4">These days, of course, there are all kinds of hotlines for people in all sorts of situations-- prayer hotlines, psychic hotlines, also hotlines for homework help, for new moms. There's a hotline for owners of three-legged dogs, and another one specifically for anybody who swallows one of those-- you know those little, round button batteries? That one also handles any kid who pushes it up their nose.</p><p begin="00:01:32.69" data-timestamp="92.69">Today we're going to devote our entire show to one phone call that happened on one hotline, a very unusual hotline. And then we have everything that followed from that one call. It takes you inside this world that I think either you're already in this world, or it's totally invisible to you. Like it's all around you. You don't even register that this world is there.</p><p begin="00:01:54.71" data-timestamp="114.71">Mary Harris tells what happened. She's the host of Slate's daily news podcast, <i>What</i> <i>Next.</i> One quick note, some parts of this phone call might not be great for young children to hear. I suppose I could have given you this warning before mentioning that part about pushing batteries up your nose. But anyway, here's Mary with Act 1, "The Call."</p></div>  </div>

And now it's about the entire town not dying.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:45:49.87" data-timestamp="2749.87"> When Jessie took that call from Kimber, listened to her OD on the line, she knew better than to expect that moment to change Kimber's life. And the next day, when Kimber thanked Jessie for saving her and then quickly followed that up with, "I want to use again," it didn't surprise Jessie. In fact, for Jessie, this was good news. It meant she could encourage Kimber to keep using the line, and it was an invitation to stay in touch.</p><p begin="00:46:14.66" data-timestamp="2774.66">So she did. She friended Kimber on Facebook, texted her for no reason. A few months after her OD, Kimber started calling Jessie "Mama." But Kimber would also drift away. When she did that, Stephen and Jessie would try to keep track of Kimber online, what she was liking and commenting on. "Messenger shows her active 6 minutes ago," Jessie texted Stephen at one point. "She hasn't even read my message. I text her phone-- nothing." "Yeah," he replied. "She's been leaving me on read," like she'd seen his message but hadn't replied.</p><p begin="00:46:50.16" data-timestamp="2810.16">A month later, Jessie tried Kimber again. "Hey, I haven't heard from you in a while. I hope that means you're good," she texted. Two days later, "hello? Are you good"? A day after that-- "hi, this is Jessie B., in case you lost my contact. It's not like you not to respond. I'm growing concerned." She added a heart emoji, pressed send, and hoped for the best.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act4">
  <h3>Act Four: Kimber</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:47:23.76" data-timestamp="2843.76"> Act 4, "Kimber." While Jessie was checking in with Kimber by text and Facebook, Stephen, the paramedic, he was trying to help out IRL. After all, he and Kimber lived in the same town. Stephen got Kimber back into rehab. Then he offered to get her into job training. A few weeks after her overdose, he posted a picture of the two of them on Twitter. Kimber had shown up at Stephen's July 4th barbecue.</p><p begin="00:47:52.63" data-timestamp="2872.63">A few months after that, though, is when Kimber went silent for Stephen and Jessie. Weeks went by, then months. And then Jessie got a text from a number she didn't recognize. It read, "hey, Jessie, it's Kimber. I just wanted to give you my new number." Jessie replies, "oh, hey, hi," four exclamation marks. Kimber was alive.</p><p begin="00:48:17.44" data-timestamp="2897.44">[KNOCKING]</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:48:19.57" data-timestamp="2899.57"> Is this going to bug you?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:48:20.62" data-timestamp="2900.62"> No, you're good. [LAUGHS]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:48:22.66" data-timestamp="2902.66"> This, of course, finally, is Kimber. Kimber lives in Vermont now. She's been sober for one year. The place she calls home is a tidy duplex where she lives with her little gray dog, Luna, and not a lot else. She didn't bring anything with her when she left Massachusetts, where she OD'd two years back. There aren't any family pictures on the wall, not many mementos. Kimber is growing plants, though. Nature has always been her thing. As a kid, she was the one who was always bringing creatures home.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:48:54.58" data-timestamp="2934.58"> Do you remember Elmyra from <i>Looney</i> <i>Tunes?</i></p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:48:56.74" data-timestamp="2936.74"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:48:57.34" data-timestamp="2937.34"> So my family called me Elmyra because her thing on the show was like, she would kiss and hug and squeeze animals until they died. And so that's what they called me because I always had some kind of critter or stuff like that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:49:13.45" data-timestamp="2953.45"> In her new apartment, there's a little sunroom off the kitchen. Kimber wanted to make it into a greenhouse, but the radiator started leaking there, and the floor got soft. Still, this is a fresh start.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:49:26.21" data-timestamp="2966.21"> Do you keep Narcan around still?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:49:27.56" data-timestamp="2967.56"> I do. I do. I carry it with me. You just never know when you're going to need Narcan.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:49:34.75" data-timestamp="2974.75"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:49:36.45" data-timestamp="2976.45"> I just had to revive somebody the other day here at an AA meeting.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:49:40.86" data-timestamp="2980.86"> It was different, being on that side of things.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:49:43.32" data-timestamp="2983.32"> You don't even think about the fact that you almost died. That's the crazy part. Like, I forgot about a couple of my overdoses because you kind of just fall asleep and don't even know that you fell asleep. I'd always get mad. You get mad at the person. You're like, what are you stressing out about? I'm here. I'm fine. What is the problem?</p><p begin="00:50:01.20" data-timestamp="3001.2">And then I watched people overdose, and it's like three minutes between when you Narcan them, and they'll come back. It feels like an eternity, and all those things run through your head. This person's dead. I don't know if this person's coming back. And then you realize, oh. You're like, oh. OK, this was pretty scary.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:50:25.08" data-timestamp="3025.08"> On that first day she called the line, the day Jessie answered and Stephen revived her, Kimber remembers waking up to a cold shiver rolling down her spine. She was looking down the hallway to the bathroom where she last remembered being, a bathroom now filled with cops. There was the suitcase she'd just lugged home from rehab. And then she got really, really sick.</p><p begin="00:50:47.40" data-timestamp="3047.4">After Stephen forced her to go to the hospital, she checked herself out within an hour and walked home, puking along the side of the road. She picked up more drugs pretty much right away. The whole idea of Never Use Alone's approach is that, as Jessie puts it, you give the callers time, one more day to fight their demons. And Kimber had a lot of demons. She says her parents both used drugs. When she was 17, her brother was killed by a drug dealer. The whole family kind of crumbled after that, Kimber included.</p><p begin="00:51:20.85" data-timestamp="3080.85">Kimber went to rehab on and off for years. She'd use a ton of drugs, realize things were getting a little out of control, and check in for a couple of weeks to "spin dry," as she put it. She'd get clean enough to go back to work and pay her rent, and then the cycle would start back up again. So the call with Jessie was not the moment things changed. That moment came over a year later. Things had gotten bad.</p><p begin="00:51:45.22" data-timestamp="3105.22">She says the sheriff had kicked her out of her apartment, the one where Stephen had revived her. Her car had been stolen and totaled. She was carrying around a backpack with her passport and birth certificate in it. But then it disappeared. Her cell phone was gone too. She had a friend who would let her crash, but the friend had a condition. Kimber had to call detox every day and try to get a bed, which eventually she did.</p><p begin="00:52:11.56" data-timestamp="3131.56">The hotline is called Never Use Alone, but walking into rehab, Kimber was utterly and completely alone. And maybe for this part, you have to be. When she arrived, the nurse asked if she wanted medication. Methadone? To make getting straight a little easier? For the first time, she said no.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:52:31.83" data-timestamp="3151.83"> So I went up to detox and detoxed with no medication.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:52:36.93" data-timestamp="3156.93"> What did that feel like?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:52:38.04" data-timestamp="3158.04"> It was awful. I just remember going in, and I couldn't lay in the bed. It was so cold. They had the AC blasting. It was summertime. Of course, the AC is on. But you're going through withdrawal, so you're so cold. And I couldn't lay in the bed because they gave you like these thin blankets. So I went in the bathroom, where there's a heater thing in there, and laid on the floor. And they dragged me out. They wouldn't let me stay in the bathroom. They're like, you can't lay in here.</p><p begin="00:53:01.96" data-timestamp="3181.96">And so then I waited for them to leave, and I went into the bathroom. The only thing that felt good was I stripped down naked. It was a really small bathroom with like tile floors, so I had to throw my clothes out of the door back into my room and just lay on the tile. And I had the blanket over the heating vent, and I just like had the heat on there.</p><p begin="00:53:23.37" data-timestamp="3203.37">But the coolness on my back and then the heat-- oh, my God. It was the only thing that kept me being able to sit through it. And I mean, I was throwing up the whole time. And I don't know how long I was there. It was a while. It just felt like days and days went on. And I didn't think it was ever going to stop. And I just kept telling myself that I could do it, that I really had the strength to do it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:53:48.63" data-timestamp="3228.63"> Kimber remembers all the details because she's really proud of what she did. Four days after she checked in, she sent Jessie that text from a new phone. "I feel like I'm in a stable enough place right now to reach out and let you know I'm back," Kimber wrote tentatively.</p><p begin="00:54:04.74" data-timestamp="3244.74">Jessie said, "everything that you've been through has prepared you for today. You haven't been wasting time. You've been getting ready. I'll support you however you need me to, every step of the way." For Kimber, it's been a year of big changes. She's in a new town, but she still doesn't really trust herself around her old crowd.</p><p begin="00:54:34.02" data-timestamp="3274.02">It's interesting how this one call brought these three people together. Stephen told me when he first met Jessie, they didn't really like each other-- too similar maybe. But the call bonded them somehow. And Kimber, right after that call, she avoided Stephen, even though he kept reaching out. She was embarrassed. He lived in her town, knew all the police. She said she got to this point where she knew other people were sick of her bullshit because she was sick of her bullshit.</p><p begin="00:55:04.27" data-timestamp="3304.27">That passed eventually. Kimber took a trip with Stephen's family to the beach this summer. There are these photos of them, hanging out in the sand. Jessie, to this day, hasn't met Kimber in person, but they text all the time. On Mother's Day, Kimber sent Jessie a card. There was a picture inside of it, an ultrasound. Kimber is pregnant.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:55:27.52" data-timestamp="3327.52"> I always said my body was too poisonous for anything to live inside. I had done so much to myself and just berated my body. And there was no way anything was going to be able to live in this toxic environment.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:55:41.75" data-timestamp="3341.75"> Kimber's got a partner, Mikey. She met him in AA. They moved in together. This pregnancy was a surprise, but a happy one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kimber</h4><p begin="00:55:51.09" data-timestamp="3351.09"> And I think there was definitely a part of me that thought I didn't know how to be a mother. So when I found out I was pregnant, like even if I had to do it by myself, like I was going to do it. I'm 31 years old. I was sober. I moved to a brand new place, started with nothing. And I knew I could do this. If I could do everything I was doing, I could do this too. So I never for a second thought that I was going to not keep the baby.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Mary Harris</h4><p begin="00:56:23.42" data-timestamp="3383.42"> Kimber still dreams about using drugs. These are absurd dreams, vivid dreams, dreams about smoking dope that turns into vanilla frosting. And most of the people she went to rehab with, they've gone back to using. These days, Jessie's still taking calls, sometimes 10 calls a day. She doesn't hear from her daughter Kaylen very much, but I did reach Kaylen. She told me she's trying to use less. Mostly, she said, she's sticking with weed.</p><p begin="00:56:57.07" data-timestamp="3417.07">As for Stephen, he says reviving Kimber changed his life. He'd been having nightmares about all the people he failed to save. After Kimber survived, he realized he didn't have to be the one pumping oxygen into someone's lungs to keep them alive. He could work more on the hotline instead. So he's doing that. He's even started taking calls.</p><p begin="00:57:17.74" data-timestamp="3437.74">Stephen and Jessie wanted Kimber to come work on the hotline too. So she went through the training and just took her very first call. Now she's one of the voices on the other side of the phone, saying, put me on speakerphone, lay out your Narcan, unlock your door.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:45.39" data-timestamp="3465.39"> Mary Harris, she's the host and managing editor of Slate's daily news podcast, <i>What</i> <i>Next.</i> They were our collaborators in making this story. If you liked what you just heard, Mary is such an amazing interviewer. And her daily show gets into the news in ways other people don't. You might check it out.</p><p begin="00:58:01.76" data-timestamp="3481.76">["TALK TO ME" BY CAVETOWN]</p><p begin="00:58:03.96" data-timestamp="3483.96"></p></div>  </div>

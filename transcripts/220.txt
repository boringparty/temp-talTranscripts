  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.30" data-timestamp="0.3"> Well, from WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. And I'll say right here at the top. Today's show is a rerun. One of our favorite episodes we've ever broadcast. We haven't rerun this in years. It was first broadcast 15 years ago. And the way the show originally opened is to explain what we were doing on the show and why we were doing it the way we were doing it. I was joined in the studio by one of our producers at the time, Alex Blumberg.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:00:22.57" data-timestamp="22.57"> Hello, Ira.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:24.06" data-timestamp="24.06"> Alex.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:00:24.63" data-timestamp="24.63"> Yes?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:25.89" data-timestamp="25.89"> What's the reason for this week's show?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:00:28.23" data-timestamp="28.23"> Well, I've been thinking about that. And for me, the reason for today's program goes back to a book I read when I was 15 called <i>The</i> <i>Women's</i> <i>Room.</i> Maybe people listening will remember <i>The</i> <i>Women's</i> <i>Room.</i> It was a seminal feminist novel from the '70s. And I first came across <i>The</i> <i>Women's</i> <i>Room</i> when I was digging through my parents' bookshelves, looking for porn-- books with naughty parts in them, like <i>Fear</i> <i>of</i> <i>Flying.</i> And so I picked up, and I read it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:03.26" data-timestamp="63.26"> Because the word "women" was in the title.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:01:04.86" data-timestamp="64.86"> In lipstick, probably. Or maybe not. Yes.</p><p begin="00:01:14.35" data-timestamp="74.35">It's incredibly polemical. It describes, basically, a group of women going from the '50s through the '60s. And they all suffer at the hands of the various men in their lives. And there's constantly-- women are slowly being driven crazy by their husbands' incessant criticism, or they're being called ugly after the get mastectomies, or they're being stifled by their husbands' emotional shallowness.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:41.50" data-timestamp="101.5"> And I notice you happen to have brought into the studio with you today a copy of <i>The</i> <i>Women's</i> <i>Room.</i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:01:45.25" data-timestamp="105.25"> Yeah. I recently reread it just to see-- for example, I bookmarked a couple of these things. On page 198, the narrator comes in and she says, "you think I hate men. I guess I do." And then a little bit further down she says, "my feelings about men are the result of my experience. I have little sympathy for them. Like a Jew just released from Dachau, I watch the handsome young Nazi soldier fall writhing to the ground with a bullet in his stomach, and I look briefly and walk on. I don't even need to shrug. I simply don't care."</p><p begin="00:02:23.84" data-timestamp="143.84">And then a little bit later, "my hatred is learned from experience. That is not prejudice. I wish it were prejudice. Then, perhaps, I could unlearn it." There's not very much pornographic about that.</p><p begin="00:02:46.34" data-timestamp="166.34">It's an incredibly strange thing to read this book at that age because, at that age, I felt like there's one other woman who sort of comes into play at this point. And that's-- I probably shouldn't say her name. Let's just call her Kelly. And she was in eighth grade with me. And she sat two rows away from me.</p><p begin="00:03:06.43" data-timestamp="186.43">And I remember one time sitting in homeroom with her and seeing her button-down shirt was open a little bit, and I could catch the barest sliver of her little training bra. And it was perhaps the most desire I've ever felt in my life, maybe. It was all-consuming. I couldn't concentrate for the rest of the day. I remember it to this day.</p><p begin="00:03:39.29" data-timestamp="219.29">And so at the time I was reading this book, I felt like, oh my god, this is what-- I could see myself becoming the people that she was describing, the men she was describing. And it was really terrifying.</p><p begin="00:03:54.06" data-timestamp="234.06">My testosterone, and how it affects me, and how I react to it, I think about on a daily basis all the time. It often feels like there's something in my body giving me instructions that I probably shouldn't follow.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:22.25" data-timestamp="262.25"> OK. Which brings us to today's program. A while back, Alex tried to convince all of us who work on the radio show that we should do an entire program about testosterone. And I have to say, none of us were really buying it, especially the women, who found the subject dull, even though, as Alex tried to point out to them, women have testosterone and it affects them pretty much exactly the same way that it affects men.</p><p begin="00:04:44.81" data-timestamp="284.81">I think all of us were fearing a kind of <i>Men</i> <i>Are</i> <i>from</i> <i>Mars,</i> <i>Women</i> <i>Are</i> <i>from</i> <i>Venus</i> sort of thing, how hormones make men be all aggressive and women are non-aggressive in very stereotyped ways, which all seem kind of reductionistic and dumb-headed.</p><p begin="00:04:59.27" data-timestamp="299.27">And then Alex started reading and calling people. And one of the people that he called was this guy who wrote an article for <i>GQ</i> magazine about what happened to him when, because of medical reasons, he stopped producing testosterone. And for four months, he lived without testosterone before they caught the problem. And the way he described living without testosterone was really incredible.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:05:20.61" data-timestamp="320.61"> Everything that I identify as being me, my ambition, my interest in things, my sense of humor, the inflection in my voice, the quality of my speech even changed in the time that I was without a lot of the hormone. So yes, the introduction of testosterone returned everything. There were things that I find offensive about my own personality that were disconnected then. And it was nice to be without them-- envy, the desire to judge itself. I approached people with a humility that I had never displayed before.</p><p begin="00:06:27.12" data-timestamp="387.12">I grew up in a culture, like all of us, that divides the soul from the body, and that that is your singleness. That is your uniqueness. And nothing can touch that. And then I go through this experience where I have small amounts of a bodily chemical removed and then reintroduced, and it changes everything I know as my self. And it violates the sanctity of that understanding, that understanding that who you are exists independent of any other forces in the universe. And that's humbling. And it's terrifying.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:21.74" data-timestamp="441.74"> I think when it comes to this stuff, most of us do not know what to believe. We're caught between thinking that our hormones and body chemistry can determine so much about our personalities, and wanting to believe that they don't. And so today on our program, we bring you four stories exploring that question. How much does testosterone determine?</p><p begin="00:07:40.27" data-timestamp="460.27">Act One of our show, Life at Zero, in which we hear about what it means to lose all your testosterone.</p><p begin="00:07:46.33" data-timestamp="466.33">Act Two, Infinite Gent. A woman gets pumped up with several times the testosterone that most men have and describes some surprising changes.</p><p begin="00:07:54.93" data-timestamp="474.93">Act Three, Contest-osterone. In that act, at Alex's suggestion, all of us on the staff of this radio program decide to find out who has the most and who has the least testosterone with lab tests to see how the levels match with our personality traits-- an exercise, I have to say, most of us at this point would not recommend that you try at your workplace or with your friends.</p><p begin="00:08:18.47" data-timestamp="498.47">Act Four, Learning To Shut Up. In that act, a mother asks her teenage son all her questions about what it means to be a boy, which is, of course, exactly the sort of thing you do not want your mother asking you on tape at 15.</p><p begin="00:08:31.10" data-timestamp="511.1">Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Life At Zero</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:08:41.00" data-timestamp="521"> Act One, Life at Zero.</p><p begin="00:08:44.69" data-timestamp="524.69">Testosterone is the hormone of desire. And by that, I don't mean sexual desire. I mean desire, period. In this act, we continue with Alex's interview with that magazine writer from <i>GQ.</i> He wrote about his experiences with testosterone anonymously.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:08:58.97" data-timestamp="538.97"> When you have no testosterone, you have no desire. And when you have no desire, you don't have any content in your mind. You don't think about anything.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:09:13.23" data-timestamp="553.23"> And during those months, how are you behaving? What was different?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man</h4><p begin="00:09:17.40" data-timestamp="557.4"> It wasn't that I was behaving. It was that I was not behaving at all. I was, when I was awake, literally sitting in bed and staring at the wall with neither interest nor disinterest for three, four hours at a time. If you'd had a camera in the room, you would have thought I was comatose.</p><p begin="00:09:37.06" data-timestamp="577.06">I would go out. I would buy some groceries early in the morning. And that would be it. My day had no content. I had no interest in even watching TV, much less reading the newspaper or a book. Food-- I didn't want my food to taste good or interesting. And when you're blessed with that lack of desire, you can eat a loaf of Wonder Bread with mayonnaise. And that will be your day.</p><p begin="00:10:06.90" data-timestamp="606.9">And I only saw my girlfriend on weekends, since she was living in New York and I was living in Philly. His 12-year-old sister, Georgia, claimed the very back seat of the minivan and was content to lie there, surrounded by nail polish and beads and thread and Hershey Kisses and <i>Archie</i> comics and listen to her music on her Discman-- mostly R&B stuff, Destiny's Child, and the <i>Save</i> <i>the</i> <i>Last</i> <i>Dance</i> soundtrack.</p><p begin="00:50:03.78" data-timestamp="3003.78">My husband, Cassidy, and I sat in the front and Owen sat on the seat right behind us so he could listen to his CDs on the stereo. We'd tell him to listen to them on his Discman, which he did sometimes, but then he'd miss out on our sporadic conversations, which he enjoyed either participating in or mocking.</p><p begin="00:50:19.76" data-timestamp="3019.76">Sometimes he'd give us a choice of his CDs to play. "Nirvana?" he'd say. "You guys like Nirvana, right?" "Yeah, but the unplugged one," we'd say. "Oh, Lord, help me now," he'd say. He'd pop a dozen CDs into the player-- They Will Know Us by the Trail of Dead, Black Rebel Motorcycle Club, At the Drive-In, other stuff-- and we'd listen to half a song from each one and go, "nope. Nope. Nope."</p><p begin="00:50:44.09" data-timestamp="3044.09">I had wanted to learn more about him from his music. I was pretty sure his lyrics would tell me more about him than he would. But the problem was I couldn't understand most of the lyrics, and none of his CDs, which he'd bootlegged, had liner notes. We asked him if he didn't miss having liner notes, and he said, "man, liner notes. You guy have this unnatural relationship with your liner notes."</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:06.84" data-timestamp="3066.84"> What are some of your favorite lyrics?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:13.26" data-timestamp="3073.26"> I like some of the lyrics from the Pixies.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:16.56" data-timestamp="3076.56"> Can you say some of the lyrics from the Pixies?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:22.03" data-timestamp="3082.03"> "You're so pretty when you're unfaithful to me."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:25.36" data-timestamp="3085.36"> So what does that mean to you?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:28.77" data-timestamp="3088.77"> I don't know. I just thought it was funny.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:51:33.27" data-timestamp="3093.27"> When Owen was little, I discovered I could get him to do anything by timing him. "Go get your jacket," I'd say, or "clean up your toys, quick. I'll time you." He loved competing with himself and beating his old records, even if I just made up times. And it evolved into a huge, competitive, sports-loving thing that I still don't really understand or enjoy.</p><p begin="00:51:53.47" data-timestamp="3113.47">That timing thing didn't work at all with Georgia. She'd just look up at me and say, "what do you mean, time me? What for?"</p><p begin="00:52:07.53" data-timestamp="3127.53">The difference between him and Georgia in the talking department is huge. Georgia will come home from school and literally reenact her entire day so that she's telling me about it almost in real time, hour after hour. Owen, on the other hand, might tell me that his day was good even if it was bad, but usually he just shrugs and makes an "I don't know" kind of sound. And then he'll head down to the basement and play his guitar.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:31.14" data-timestamp="3151.14"> So do some of your friends have girlfriends? And do you ever think that you'd like to have a girlfriend?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:38.19" data-timestamp="3158.19"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:39.57" data-timestamp="3159.57"> What do you think they think? Like, how would they describe you to their friends, do you think?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:52.10" data-timestamp="3172.1"> I don't know.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:56.01" data-timestamp="3176.01"> Do you think you do know, but you just don't really want to talk about it with me?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:52:58.87" data-timestamp="3178.87"> I think that, yeah. I think so.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:53:01.81" data-timestamp="3181.81"> I can dig it.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:53:09.84" data-timestamp="3189.84"> I've learned over time not to worry too much about his silence, although it used to drive me crazy. I wondered if maybe he'd turn out to be a psychopath, although I'm not sure why I associate evil with silence. Anyway, I'm getting better with it.</p><p begin="00:53:23.90" data-timestamp="3203.9">Cassidy often reassures me by saying stuff like, "it's a guy thing," or "he's a 15-year-old boy. What do you expect?" He told me that, when he was Owen's age, he sat in his bedroom by himself for hours on end, typing page after page of angry vitriol against the world on an old manual typewriter until his fingers bled, and he seems really happy now.</p><p begin="00:53:44.30" data-timestamp="3224.3">I realize my kids sound like boy and girl stereotypes. Owen is taciturn and into sports, and Georgia's chatty and prone to the occasional crying jag. But it really just is that way. I don't know why. It's not like we planned it. I don't want to think it's because he's a boy and she's a girl, but if it is, who cares?</p><p begin="00:54:03.05" data-timestamp="3243.05">When Georgia was upset about something at school the other day and facedown on the couch, he sat on the floor beside her and said, "it's hard being a girl, isn't it?" I'm not sure that it's any easier being a boy. And I don't know what he does exactly when he's sad, other than listening to music. Except for once, it's been years since I've seen him cry. I'd kill to read his notebook.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:24.78" data-timestamp="3264.78"> Is there anything about girls that you envy?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:30.03" data-timestamp="3270.03"> No. Nothing.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:31.19" data-timestamp="3271.19"> Nothing?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:31.62" data-timestamp="3271.62"> Nothing.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:32.67" data-timestamp="3272.67"> What about their-- it seems, their ability to talk about how they feel about things easier?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:41.88" data-timestamp="3281.88"> Sure, yeah.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:43.84" data-timestamp="3283.84"> You envy that?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Owen Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:45.78" data-timestamp="3285.78"> I definitely envy that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miriam Toews</h4><p begin="00:54:52.64" data-timestamp="3292.64"> We were all pretty quiet as we drove up to Colorado Springs on our way to Denver. The sun was shining, but it was raining a bit. And we saw not one, not two, but three dead cows in fields, stiff, with their legs in the air, and it bothered us.</p><p begin="00:55:07.01" data-timestamp="3307.01">After the third dead cow, I played my favorite song of the summer, Gillian Welch's "Elvis Presley Blues". It's a wistful song about the King as a 15-year-old boy, just starting out with a big rock-and-roll dream in a shirt his mother made. And it was kind of miraculous because Owen actually liked it, too.</p><p begin="00:55:24.50" data-timestamp="3324.5">For me, it was a song about a boy becoming a man and leaving home, and it made me a little sad. I think Owen was probably hearing it as a song about a cool 15-year-old who was about to conquer the world. But really, I haven't got a clue how he heard the song or how it made him feel. All he said was that it had a decent pop sensibility. And then he was quiet.</p><p begin="00:55:49.77" data-timestamp="3349.77">These days, the more I press him for information, the faster he shuts right down. The other day, I tried a new approach-- his. I was annoyed by a bunch of things, and rather than broadcast it all over the house, I sat on the couch and glared at the TV and sighed periodically.</p><p begin="00:56:05.90" data-timestamp="3365.9">And strangely, Owen responded. He observed my weirdness for a while and then left. Then he came back and looked at me again and said one word, "Mom," but in such a nice way that I smiled and said, "Owen." And that was it. And then we sat there for a while, silently. I was surprised by how completely comfortable it felt. And then, finally, wordlessly, he got up and took his laundry upstairs.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:56:41.61" data-timestamp="3401.61"> Miriam Toews. She's a writer in Toronto. Her son Owen is now a grown man and, Miriam tells us, is long past his taciturn days. Her latest book is called <i>All</i> <i>My</i> <i>Puny</i> <i>Sorrows.</i></p><p begin="00:56:52.95" data-timestamp="3412.95"></p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.73" data-timestamp="0.73">Here's my seventh grade teacher's sad fate-- he trained as a classical musician, loved music, truly loved it. And then every working day, he surrounded himself with 13-year-olds who massacred the living guts out of it. Could not play it and did not care. It made him the angriest teacher I ever saw. He yelled all the time. This is what happens if you believe that music means something, is important, and you're surrounded all day long with 13- and 14-year-olds who demonstrate through their actions that maybe it's not.</p><p begin="00:00:29.89" data-timestamp="29.89">I told Durrell Daniels about him and Durrell understood immediately. Durrell was once paid to teach band, but he had to quit because he looked at the facts at hand and came to the simple conclusion--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:00:38.54" data-timestamp="38.54">I got out of band teaching because I noticed that all the band teachers I knew had went a little crazy. I mean, no disrespect intended, but you don't find too many, like, totally sane band teachers who have taught band for a long time. And I sort of saw myself going on the edge. And I said, well, no. I don't want to be whistling to myself at a wall, looking at apples that are not there, or something like that. Because it's sort of like-- no instrument sounds good when it's not played well. Trumpet was a pain. Saxophone was a pain. Clarinet was hell.</p><p begin="00:01:22.79" data-timestamp="82.79">And another thing. The kids, when they get a trumpet, there's a difference between a kid singing and a kid getting a trumpet. You might can't sing, and you might be a little shy. No kid's going to be shy about blowing that horn no matter how bad they sound.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:39.85" data-timestamp="99.85">As a professional musician, Durrell played jazz and funk and soul, keyboards and vocals. He's toured Asia and Brazil and Canada. He's done gigs in Paris. But four years ago, with kids to support of his own, he returned to a music job in the Chicago Public Schools in an elementary school.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:01:53.61" data-timestamp="113.61">And we get these little records, Barney records. And we get up and do a little Barney dance. I'm like, this sucks.</p><p begin="00:02:01.63" data-timestamp="121.63">[LAUGHTER]</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:02.89" data-timestamp="122.89">And you had already, at that point, you had toured. God, there should be a name for this, like for that moment.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:02:10.22" data-timestamp="130.22">Misery. That's the name.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:17.00" data-timestamp="137">Well, from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass, coming to you today from the Yerba Buena Center for the Arts in San Francisco, California, thanks to public radio station KQED in San Francisco. Today on our program, Music Lessons. What's frustrating about them, what's miraculous about them.</p><p begin="00:02:33.46" data-timestamp="153.46">Act One, Papa Was not a Rolling Stone. David Sedaris tells what happens when a parent does not play an instrument and insists that his children take lessons. Act Two. Toccata and Fugue in Me, a Minor. Sarah Vowell recalls all the things that she learned in music class that had nothing to do with music. Act Three, Knockin' on Heaven's Door, in which Annie Lamott provides some much needed spiritual uplift to our program with an example of what we can learn from music now, all of us. Stay with us.</p><p begin="00:03:04.88" data-timestamp="184.88">[APPLAUSE]</p><p begin="00:03:18.52" data-timestamp="198.52">So imagine that you're an adult and you decide you want to express yourself through music. And the medium that you have to express yourself is 13- and 14- and 15-year-old kids. That is a very imprecise medium in which to apply your art. This week was especially hard on Durrell. He had to get one group of kids rehearsed to sing at one graduation ceremony and another group ready for another graduation ceremony and then there were some recitals to do.</p><p begin="00:03:43.34" data-timestamp="223.34">And then the gospel choir, who usually is pride and joy kids, sort of fell apart on him. They were singing badly. They were arguing with him. He sat them down. He told them he was probably going to have to cancel their last concert of the year. But he would decide about it after one last rehearsal.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:04:00.67" data-timestamp="240.67">My kids-- I was like, look, I'm tired. I'm tired. I've been working this stuff for you guys. And look, I can't sit here and argue with all you almost-grown kids all day long.</p><p begin="00:04:13.90" data-timestamp="253.9">[KIDS REHEARSING]</p><p begin="00:04:19.19" data-timestamp="259.19">All right. Come on. Let's get together.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:20.65" data-timestamp="260.65">After school Tuesday, the gospel choir gathered in Lindblom's Auditorium, 22 girls and three boys, one boy who never seemed to sing at all.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:04:27.11" data-timestamp="267.11">All right, you guys. Let's get into sections.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:29.67" data-timestamp="269.67">Everybody was on their best behavior. They wanted to show Mr. Daniels that they could do the concert. And they began their practice the way that they always do, with a prayer.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:04:37.71" data-timestamp="277.71">Father God, right now in the name of Jesus, God, we come thanking you, O God. We want to thank you for gathering us here, O God. We come against every spirit that's trying to hinder this choir right now in the name of Jesus.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:48.55" data-timestamp="288.55">And then from the moment that they started to sing, they just soared. They were great.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gospel Choir</h4><p begin="00:04:52.47" data-timestamp="292.47">[SINGING] Clap your hands.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Durrell Daniels</h4><p begin="00:04:54.08" data-timestamp="294.08">One, two, three, four!</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Gospel Choir</h4><p begin="00:04:57.52" data-timestamp="297.52">Hey!</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:01.94" data-timestamp="301.94">Teaching music is like any teaching, except that when you fail, it is loud. And failure is so easy. Every note is a chance to mess up. After three songs, Mr. Daniels told the chorus that he would not cancel the concert. Now he just had to get through two graduations, their concert, and a couple of recitals, and he could have the summer to promote his new CD, which, like any real professional musician, he asked me to mention here on the radio.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Papa Was Not A Rolling Stone</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:36.49" data-timestamp="336.49">Act One, Papa Was not a Rolling Stone. Children are asked to live the unlived lives of their parents nowhere more than music lessons. There the eager music students and then there are the reluctant music students. This is the story of somebody in the second group. Please welcome writer David Sedaris.</p><p begin="00:05:54.77" data-timestamp="354.77">[APPLAUSE]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Sedaris</h4><p begin="00:06:06.55" data-timestamp="366.55">My father loves jazz and has a substantial collection of records and reel-to-reel tapes he used to listen to after returning home from work each evening. He might have entered the house in a foul mood, but once he had his Dexter Gordon and a vodka martini, everything was beautiful, baby. Just beautiful. The moment the needle hit that record, he loosened his tie and became something other than a mechanical engineer with a pocket full of IBM pencils embossed with the word &quot;think.&quot;</p><p begin="00:06:36.76" data-timestamp="396.76">&quot;Man, oh, man. Will you get a load of the chops on this guy? I saw him once at the Blue Note, and I mean to tell you that he blew me right out of my chair. You could take a hatchet and cut the man's lips right off his face, and still he would have played better than anyone else out there. That's how good he was!&quot;</p><p begin="00:06:54.33" data-timestamp="414.33">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:06:57.52" data-timestamp="417.52">I'd nod my head, envisioning a pair of lips lying forsaken on the floor of some dressing room. The trick was to back slowly towards the hallway, escaping into the kitchen before my father could yell, &quot;hey, get back here! Where do you think you're going? Sit down for a minute and listen. I mean, really listen.&quot;</p><p begin="00:07:17.90" data-timestamp="437.9">How could you prove you were listening? It was as if he expected us to change color like the fuzz-colored ceramic rabbit our neighbor used to predict the weather. I often thought that my father might have been a musician himself had he not been born to immigrant parents who considered even wax-tipped shoelaces to be an extravagance. They listened only to Greek music, an oxymoron as far as the rest of the world was concerned.</p><p begin="00:07:45.45" data-timestamp="465.45">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:07:46.88" data-timestamp="466.88">Jazz was, in a sense, my father's own personal discovery. Denounced and forbidden by his parents, he hid his 78s and secretly drove into New York City, where he'd sit alive and enraptured at the feet of his heroes. The move to North Carolina was difficult, and he felt cut off with nothing but the Country and Top 40 radio stations Raleigh had to offer in the mid to late 1960s. Limited to his record collection, it became his dream that his children might fill the void by someday forming a jazz combo.</p><p begin="00:08:17.98" data-timestamp="497.98">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:08:21.71" data-timestamp="501.71">His plan took shape shortly after escorting my sisters Lisa, Gretchen, and I to the local state university to see Dave Brubeck, who was currently touring with his sons. To take nerdiness to its most dizzying, &quot;Willow, Willow,&quot; &quot;Tico-Tico&quot; extremes--</p><p begin="00:36:59.03" data-timestamp="2219.03">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:37:00.94" data-timestamp="2220.94">--and stand before my peers with my head held high, to stick out my tongue at the Andy Heaps of the world, run back to the baritone horn of life, and blow mighty and proud.</p><p begin="00:37:13.08" data-timestamp="2233.08">[APPLAUSE]</p><p begin="00:37:18.48" data-timestamp="2238.48">(HOST) SARAH VOWELL: Thank you.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:37:27.83" data-timestamp="2247.83">Sarah Vowell is the music columnist for the online magazine <i>Salon</i> and the author of <i>Radio</i> <i>On</i>.</p><p begin="00:37:33.36" data-timestamp="2253.36">[APPLAUSE]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Bandleader</h4><p begin="00:37:36.30" data-timestamp="2256.3">One, two, three--</p><p begin="00:37:38.04" data-timestamp="2258.04">[MUSIC - CHILDREN FROM THE SAN FRANCISCO UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT]</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act3">
  <h3>Act Three: Knockin' On Heaven's Door</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:37:57.38" data-timestamp="2277.38">Act Three, Knockin' on Heaven's Door. In this act, we turn from formal music lessons to what it is that music can actually teach us today now, outside the classroom. Anne Lamott is the only writer in our show today who actually lives in the San Francisco area. The author of many books, please welcome her.</p><p begin="00:38:14.64" data-timestamp="2294.64">[APPLAUSE]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Anne Lamott</h4><p begin="00:38:22.55" data-timestamp="2302.55">Thank you. So there I was on a plane returning home from St. Louis. Or rather, there I was in a plane on the runway at the airport in St. Louis, with, I think, the not unreasonable expectation that we would be in the air soon as our flight had already been delayed two hours. I was anxious to get home, as I had not seen Sam in several days. But all things considered, I thought I was coping quite well, especially because I am a skeptical and terrified flier. In between devouring Hershey's chocolates and $13 worth of trashy magazines, I had spent the two hours of the delay trying to be helpful to the other stranded passengers. I distributed all my magazines and most of my chocolates, I got an old man some water, I flirted with babies, I mingled, I schmoozed, I was Geoffrey Rush in <i>Shine.</i></p><p begin="00:39:17.91" data-timestamp="2357.91">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:39:21.13" data-timestamp="2361.13">I had seen what may have been a miracle at my church recently. I had been feeling ever since that I was supposed to walk through life with a deeper faith, a deeper sense of assurance that if I took care of God's children for God, He would take care of me. My only hope was that nothing else go wrong, that once we were on board, everything runs smoothly. My idea of everything running smoothly on an airplane is that once it is in the air, A, I not die in a slow-motion fiery crash or get stabbed to death by terrorists. And that B, none of the other passengers try to talk to me.</p><p begin="00:39:59.66" data-timestamp="2399.66">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:40:04.46" data-timestamp="2404.46">We finally got to board. I was in row 38 between a woman slightly older than I with limited language skills and a man my own age who was reading a book by a famous right-wing Christian novelist about the apocalypse. A newspaper had asked me to review it when it first came out because this author and I are both Christians, although, as I pointed out in my review, he's one of those right-wing Christians who think that Jesus is coming back sometime next Tuesday right after lunch, and I am one of those left-wing Christians who think that perhaps the author is just spiritualizing his hysteria.</p><p begin="00:40:43.19" data-timestamp="2443.19">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:40:49.03" data-timestamp="2449.03">Also, I suggested in my review that he has the tendency to be a little rigid. &quot;How is it?&quot; I ask, pointing jovially to his book, partly to be friendly and partly to gauge where he stood politically.</p><p begin="00:41:02.81" data-timestamp="2462.81">&quot;This is one of the best books I've ever read,&quot; he replied. &quot;You should read it.&quot; I nodded. I remember saying in the review that it was hardcore, right-wing paranoid, anti-semitic, homophobic, misogynistic propaganda.</p><p begin="00:41:17.09" data-timestamp="2477.09">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:41:20.03" data-timestamp="2480.03">Not to put too fine a point on it.</p><p begin="00:41:22.11" data-timestamp="2482.11">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:41:24.68" data-timestamp="2484.68">He smiled and went back to reading. I couldn't begin to guess what country the woman was from, although I think it's possible that she had one Latvian parent and one Korean. She sounded a little Latka, the Andy Kaufman character on <i>Taxi</i>, except when things began to fall apart, when she sounded just like the space men in <i>Mars</i> <i>Attacks!</i> &quot;Ack! Ack! Ack!,&quot; she'd cry. But I'm getting ahead of myself.</p><p begin="00:41:56.20" data-timestamp="2516.2">As we sat there on the runway, the man reading the book about the apocalypse commented on the small gold cross I wear on a necklace. &quot;Are you born again?&quot; He asked as we taxied down the runway. He was rather prim and tense, maybe a little like David Eisenhower with a spastic colon.</p><p begin="00:42:14.64" data-timestamp="2534.64">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:42:19.37" data-timestamp="2539.37">I did not know how to answer for a moment. &quot;Yes,&quot; I said, finally, &quot;I am.&quot; My friends like to tell each other that I am not really a born-again Christian. They think of me more along the lines of that old Jonathan Miller routine when he said, &quot;I'm not really a Jew. I'm Jew-ish.&quot;</p><p begin="00:42:36.46" data-timestamp="2556.46">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:42:44.88" data-timestamp="2564.88">They think I'm Christian-ish. But I'm not. I'm just a bad Christian. A bad born-again Christian. And certainly, like the apostle Peter, I am capable of denying it, of presenting myself as a sort of leftist, liberation theology enthusiast, and maybe sort of vaguely Jesus-y bon vivant.</p><p begin="00:43:05.86" data-timestamp="2585.86">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:43:08.74" data-timestamp="2588.74">But it's not true. And I believe when you get on a plane, if you start lying, you are screwed unto the very Lord.</p><p begin="00:43:16.86" data-timestamp="2596.86">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:43:22.90" data-timestamp="2602.9">So I told the truth, that I'm a believer, a convert. I'm probably about three months away from slapping an aluminum Jesus fish on the back of my car. Although I first want to see if the application or Stickum in any way interferes with my lease agreement.</p><p begin="00:43:39.79" data-timestamp="2619.79">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:43:49.56" data-timestamp="2629.56">I just love the guy. I just love Jesus. It's that simple. Anyway, as the plane taxied out to the runway, the man on my right began telling me about how he and his wife were home schooling their children. And he described with enormous acrimony the radical, free-for-all, feminist, artsy-feely philosophy of his county school system. And I knew instantly that this description was an act of aggression against me, that he was telepathic onto me, could see that I am the enemy, that I will be on the same curling team in Heaven as Tom Hayden and Vanessa Redgrave.</p><p begin="00:44:29.75" data-timestamp="2669.75">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:44:35.31" data-timestamp="2675.31">But suddenly the plane braked to a stop. We all looked around for a moment, and then the captain came on the PA system and announced calmly that two passengers wanted to get off the plane right then and there. We were headed back to the gate. &quot;What?&quot; we all cried. The good news was that this was only going to take a minute or so, since in the past two hours we'd only gotten about 500 feet. The bad news was that FAA regulations dictated that all of the stowed luggage had to be gone over by security to make sure these people had not accidentally left behind their pipe bombs.</p><p begin="00:45:15.62" data-timestamp="2715.62">The Latvian woman stared at me quizzically. I explained very slowly and very loudly what was going on. She gaped at me for a moment. &quot;Ack,&quot; she whispered.</p><p begin="00:45:30.08" data-timestamp="2730.08">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:45:35.55" data-timestamp="2735.55">Eventually, the three of us in row 38 began to read. And an hour later, the plane finally took off again. We, the citizens of row 38, all ordered sodas. The Latvian woman put on a Walkman and began to listen with her eyes closed. The Christian man read his book about the apocalypse. I read <i>The</i> <i>New</i> <i>Yorker</i>. Then the seat belt sign came on and the pilot came back on over the PA system. &quot;I'm afraid we're about to hit some really heavy turbulence,&quot; he said. &quot;Please return to your seats.&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:09.04" data-timestamp="2769.04">A moment later the plane began bouncing around so hard that we had to hold on to our drinks. &quot;Ack! Ack! Ack!&quot; said the Latvian, grabbing for her Sprite. &quot;Everyone take your seat!&quot; the pilot literally barked over the PA system. &quot;We are in for some rough going.&quot; My heart bounced around like a jumping bean. The plane rose and fell and shook, and the pilot came back on and said sternly, like an angry dad, &quot;flight attendants, sit down now!&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:37.12" data-timestamp="2797.12">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:46:42.94" data-timestamp="2802.94">And the plane hit those waves and currents of air on the choppy sea of sky, and we bounced and moaned and gasped. &quot;Whoa,&quot; we all said, as one, like we were on a roller coaster ride. We're going down, I thought. I know a basic tenet of the Christian faith is that death is really just a major change of address--</p><p begin="00:47:04.94" data-timestamp="2824.94">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:47:08.96" data-timestamp="2828.96">--but I had to close my eyes to squinch back tears of terror and loss. Oh my god, I thought. Oh my god. I'll never see Sam again. This will kill me a second time. The plane bucked and shook without stopping, and the Christian read calmly, stoically, rather pleased with himself it seemed to my tiny, hysterical self. The Latvian closed her eyes and turned up her Walkman. I could hear it playing softly.</p><p begin="00:47:37.82" data-timestamp="2857.82">And I, praying for another miracle, thought about the one I had just seen. It took place at my church, where one of our newer members, a man named Ken, is dying of AIDS, disintegrating before our very eyes. He came in a year ago with a Jewish woman who comes every week to be with us, although she does not believe in Jesus. Shortly after the man with AIDS started coming, his partner died of the disease. A few weeks later, he said that right then and there, in the hole in his heart that Brandon's death left, Jesus slid in and had been there ever since. This man has a totally lopsided face, ravaged, emaciated. And when he smiles, he's radiant. He looks like God's crazy nephew Phil.</p><p begin="00:48:22.94" data-timestamp="2902.94">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:48:27.79" data-timestamp="2907.79">He said that he would gladly pay any price for what he has now, which is Jesus and us. There's a woman in the choir named Renola, a beautiful black woman who is smart and jovial and sweet and as devout as can be but who has also been a little standoffish towards Ken, if you ask me. She's always looked at him with confusion, when she looks at him at all, in his goofy, ravaged joy. Or she looks at him sideways. She was raised by Baptists in the Midwest who must have thought that his way of life, that he, was an abomination.</p><p begin="00:49:02.26" data-timestamp="2942.26">Maybe it was hard for her to break through this. Maybe on the most visceral level, she was a little afraid of catching the disease. I'm not sure. But anyway, Kenny has come to church almost every week for the last year and won almost everyone else over. He missed a couple of Sundays a while ago because he was too weak to come. And then a month ago, he came back, weighing almost no pounds, his face even more lopsided, as if he's had a stroke. But during the Prayers of the People, he talked joyously of his life and his decline, of how safe and happy he feels these days.</p><p begin="00:49:37.01" data-timestamp="2977.01">So on this one particular Sunday, for the first hymn, the so-called morning hymn, we sang &quot;Jacob's Ladder,&quot; which goes &quot;every rung goes higher, higher.&quot; But Kenny couldn't stand. But he sang away, sitting down, with a hymnal in his lap. And then when it came time for the second hymn, the fellowship hymn, we sang &quot;His Eye is on the Sparrow.&quot; I noticed that Ken still couldn't stand up to sing. The pianist was playing and the whole congregation had risen. And only Ken remained seated, holding the hymnal in his lap.</p><p begin="00:50:17.23" data-timestamp="3017.23">And Renola watched him rather skeptically for a moment. And then her face began to melt and contort like his. And she went to his side and bent down to lift him up, lifted up this white rag doll, this scarecrow. She held him next to her and he was draped over and against her like a child. And they sang. And it pierced me.</p><p begin="00:50:41.76" data-timestamp="3041.76">I can't imagine anything else but music that could have brought about this alchemy. How is it that you have a chord here and then another chord there and then your heart breaks open? I don't know the answer. Maybe it's that music is about as physical as it gets. Your essential rhythm is your heartbeat. Your essential sound, the breath. We're walking temples of noise. And when you add the human heart to this mix, it somehow lets us meet on a bridge we couldn't get to any other way.</p><p begin="00:51:13.07" data-timestamp="3073.07">Back on board, little by little, the plane grew steadier. And the pilot came back on to say that everything was OK. I was so excited that we were not going to crash and that I might actually get to see Sam again that I started feeling mingle-y. I suddenly wanted to be new best friends with a Christian man. But just when I opened my mouth, the pilot came back on the PA system and asked if there was a doctor on board. The woman behind us who turned out to be a nurse got up and went back to investigate. The Christian man prayed. I tried to rubberneck but I couldn't see a thing.</p><p begin="00:51:52.23" data-timestamp="3112.23">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:51:55.11" data-timestamp="3115.11">I went back to thinking about Ken in my church, and how on that Sunday Ken, of whom Renola had been so skeptical, was trying to sing. But then he and Renola both began to cry. Tears were pouring down their faces, and their noses were running like rivers. But as she held him up, she suddenly lay her black, weeping face against his feverish white face, put hers right up against his, and let all those spooky fluids mingle with her. He looked like a child in her arms who was singing because tiny children just sing all the time because they haven't made all the separations between speech and music. So he sang and she held him up. And she sang and he held her up.</p><p begin="00:52:42.80" data-timestamp="3162.8">When the nurse sitting beside us returned to row 39, it was with the news that a woman in the back was having a heart attack. A heart attack. I ask you. But there were doctors on hand and the nurse thought the woman was going to be OK. &quot;Good lord,&quot; said the Christian man. We looked at each other and sighed. We shook our heads and continued to look at each other. &quot;God,&quot; I said, &quot;I just hope the snakes don't get out of the cargo hold.&quot;</p><p begin="00:53:11.35" data-timestamp="3191.35">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:53:18.90" data-timestamp="3198.9">The prim, apocalyptic Christian man smiled. Then he laughed out loud. The Latvian woman started laughing then, although she still had her Walkman on. And while I hate to look like I'm enjoying my own jokes too much, I started laughing too. The three of us sat there in hysterics. And when we were done, the man reached over and patted the back of my hand, smiling gently. The Latvian woman leaned in close to me, into my Soviet air space, and--</p><p begin="00:53:48.35" data-timestamp="3228.35">[LAUGHTER]</p><p begin="00:53:53.51" data-timestamp="3233.51">--she beamed. I leaned forward so that our foreheads touched for just a second. I thought, I do not know if what happened at church was an honest-to-God little miracle. And I don't know if there has been another one here, the smallest possible sort. But now I felt like I was sitting with my cousins on a plane eight miles up, a plane that was going to make it home. And it made me so happy that I suddenly thought, this is plenty of miracle for me to rest in now.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Renola Garrison</h4><p begin="00:54:28.48" data-timestamp="3268.48">[SINGING &quot;HIS EYE IS ON THE SPARROW&quot;]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:55:48.26" data-timestamp="3348.26">Anne Lamott is the author of <i>Bird by</i> <i>Bird,</i> <i>Operating</i> <i>Instructions,</i> and most recently, <i>Crooked</i> <i>Little</i> <i>Heart.</i> Singing from Renola Garrison and the pianist is Anne Jefferson, both of them from Anne's church.</p></div>  </div>

No, it is not enough.</p><p begin="00:00:27.84" data-timestamp="27.84">But what is enough? Some guys come to Civil War reenactments and bring sodas and coolers and Band-Aids. My unit, I would prefer to have just plain old Caucasian males.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:25.65" data-timestamp="85.65">This tape is from a documentary by Jessica Yu called <i>Men of</i> <i>Reenaction.</i> And she found out that when men stage Civil War reenactments, sure, there is some tension between the Union and the Confederate forces. But the real battle is the one within the ranks over who is properly authentic and who is not. And like any conflict that's big enough and important enough to people, this conflict has spawned its own vocabulary. The guys who do these reenactments call themselves either hard-cores or farbs. You can guess what the hard-cores are. Those are the guys who really want to be authentic. Farbs, well, farb is short for far be it from me, as in &quot;far be it from me to judge what that person is doing right over there.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Reenactor 7</h4><p begin="00:02:11.95" data-timestamp="131.95">A farb? A farb is anyone who would wear tennis shoes or would wear modern eye glasses or would wear cotton instead of wool. When I see someone in line and he's got modern glasses, that takes away from my event. It might not affect his event, but it takes away from mine.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Reenactor 8</h4><p begin="00:02:44.04" data-timestamp="164.04">I'm in this for fun. The question here is why do Americans devote so much emotional energy to restaging the past?</p><p begin="00:03:30.91" data-timestamp="210.91">Well, from WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. Each week, of course, we choose a theme, and invite various writers, performers, documentary producers to take a whack at that theme. Our program today, Simulated Worlds. We visit wax museums, simulated coal mines, fake ethnic restaurants, an ersatz Medieval castle, and other recreated worlds that thrive all across our great land.</p><p begin="00:04:05.62" data-timestamp="245.62">The Italian writer Umberto Eco wrote an essay a few years ago in which he argued that this urge to create miniature simulated worlds is a particularly American impulse, a significant American aesthetic and one that is not talked about very often. Eco traveled the United States from Disneyland to Las Vegas to re-creations of old New York in museums. After a visit to the LBJ Library in Austin, Texas, where he saw a full, life-size re-creation of the Oval Office using the same materials as the original, Eco wrote, &quot;Is this the taste of America? Certainly it is not the taste of Frank Lloyd Wright, of the Seagram Building, of the skyscrapers of Mies van der Rohe. But the American imagination demands the real thing, and to attain it must fabricate the absolute fake.&quot;</p><p begin="00:04:53.01" data-timestamp="293.01">On today's program, Act One, a quick national tour. Act Two, writer Jack Hitt on simulated dinosaur worlds. Act Three, we get Medieval on you. Well, we take a Medieval scholar from the University of Chicago-- a guy with an actual British accent, so you can tell he's for real-- with us to Medieval Times, a suburban castle cum restaurant cum jousting arena. Act Four, how <i>Morning</i> <i>Edition</i> fakes reality every day on the radio and why we fall for it. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Travels in Hyperreality</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:36.18" data-timestamp="336.18">Act One, Travels in Hyperreality.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wax Museum Guide</h4><p begin="00:05:40.63" data-timestamp="340.63">Now, back to Jesus. The following description is alleged to be derived from an ancient manuscript sent by Publius Lentulus, president of Judea, to the Senate of Rome. It reads, &quot;There lives at this time in Judea a man of singular virtue whose name is Jesus.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:58.32" data-timestamp="358.32">Where are we? A wax museum in San Francisco in front of 13 life-size wax statues recreating Leonardo da Vinci's painting, <i>The</i> <i>Last</i> <i>Supper</i>. In this act of our program, rather than use the Michelin guide to tour America, we're using Umberto Eco's essay, &quot;Travels in Hyperreality.&quot; And in researching that essay, Eco visited no fewer than seven-- that's right, seven-- wax versions of <i>The</i> <i>Last</i> <i>Supper</i> between San Francisco and Los Angeles.</p><p begin="00:06:25.32" data-timestamp="385.32">Eco was fascinated with American wax museums, partly because he said that unlike wax museum in other countries, he says, &quot;American wax museums try to reconstruct entire worlds with a kind of maniacal, chilling attention to detail.&quot; This museum, for example, runs 85 different soundtracks in its different rooms. There are scenes of wax figures bathing waist deep in real pools of water. There's a full-scale reconstruction of King Tut's tomb. There are three-dimensional life-size wax versions of a dozen of the world's most famous paintings. A typical exhibit in the World Religion section of the museum, Moses receiving the Ten Commandments on a rocky slope, eerie red light with flashes of white lightning.</p><p begin="00:07:07.10" data-timestamp="427.1">[THUNDER]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Wax Museum Recording</h4><p begin="00:07:12.99" data-timestamp="432.99">I am the Lord your God who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. You shall have no other god before me.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:24.64" data-timestamp="444.64">Eco had a name for these over-the-top production values. He called it &quot;reconstructive neurosis.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rodney Fong</h4><p begin="00:07:34.94" data-timestamp="454.94">My name is Rodney Fong. I'm actually the grandson of the gentleman who opened this museum in 1963. That's my grandfather Thomas Fong.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:42.56" data-timestamp="462.56">Rodney's family owns the largest wax museum in North America, which is in Los Angeles, and the second largest, which is this museum at Fisherman's Wharf. His family owned three wax museums visited by Umberto Eco when Umberto Eco wrote his essay. Rodney himself is an easygoing, friendly sort who grew up working in the museum's shop after school, now 30 years old and the general manager of the place.</p><p begin="00:08:04.04" data-timestamp="484.04">And you might think that growing up in a wax museum would be kind of a fun thing for a kid. But he said that was not his experience.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rodney Fong</h4><p begin="00:08:10.16" data-timestamp="490.16">Actually, I was terrified to go into the museum because my father always used the museum as a threat. If we were not good, we'd have to spend a night in the Chamber of Horrors. Later, we were told the whole thing's fixed. And they put us on the black and white team, because they knew he was going to win. Michael points out that the whole idea that these knights would try to kill each other is not historically accurate. Most tournaments were not intended as a fight to the finish. But despite this, he liked Medieval Times. In the car on the way home, he said that it was Medieval in spirit, anyway. It was spectacle, it was circus, it was populace, it was lowbrow in the best possible sense.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Camille</h4><p begin="00:51:58.95" data-timestamp="3118.95">There were hundreds of things in there I could have said were inaccurate, inauthentic, in terms of costume, design, action. But I think that's the wrong way to think about it. If that was an accurate representation of a Medieval tournament, people would be bored stiff. They wouldn't go to it. They wouldn't get anybody to it, because they lasted for six days. And they were like conventions of aristocrats. It wasn't the kind of experience that is going to be entertaining to people. So if they were being truly authentic, it would be very boring for us.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:52:30.66" data-timestamp="3150.66">When we driving out to Medieval Times earlier, Michael had said that the thing that appealed to him most about the Middle Ages was this other-ness, the fact that it did not seem like our world at all. And in the car home, I suggested to him that Medieval Times did not create that feeling at all. It was mostly familiar images from movies and storybooks. But he disagreed.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Camille</h4><p begin="00:52:48.19" data-timestamp="3168.19">It was weird enough in all the mixtures of strange things in it. I mean, the odd mixture of the modern building and the castle's structure and the long-haired hunky knights that looked like centerfolds from <i>Playgirl</i> with the ways that the things were mixed together. To me, in a strange way, it was Medieval. Because obviously, the Middle Ages is incredibly hybrid and confused. And again, that's what attracts me about it. It's certainly not the age of order and systematic piety that everyone thinks.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:22.97" data-timestamp="3202.97">But the strangeness that you're describing is not the strangeness of the Middle Ages. It's the strangeness of America.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Camille</h4><p begin="00:53:28.65" data-timestamp="3208.65">Exactly.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:30.20" data-timestamp="3210.2">Exactly, Michael said. In fact, as far as he's concerned, America is a very Medieval country, far more Medieval than Western Europe.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michael Camille</h4><p begin="00:53:37.30" data-timestamp="3217.3">In the Middle Ages, it was a pioneer culture. They were just beginning to create things. There was a sense of newness moving forward, evangelical, full of weird and wonderful mixtures, ultra-religious, and yet at the same time, ultra-decadent. I think that's one of the reasons why we're so fascinated in America with the Middle Ages. Because we're living it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:56.40" data-timestamp="3236.4">In the commemorative photo they took of us at Medieval Times, the distinguished scholar from the University of Chicago is grinning happily, a paper crown on his head. The little mock frame they put the photo inside says in typeface at the bottom, &quot;Your Knight to Remember.&quot; That's knight, K-N-I-G-H-T.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act4">
  <h3>Act Four: Simulating Worlds On The Radio</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:48.13" data-timestamp="3288.13">Act Four, simulated worlds on the radio. I worked for National Public Radio's network headquarters in Washington starting when I was 19 years old, a long time ago. And I would like to talk for a minute about how reality is simulated, is constructed on programs like <i>Morning</i> <i>Edition</i> and <i>All</i> <i>Things</i> <i>Considered</i>, programs I love, programs I worked on.</p><p begin="00:55:13.62" data-timestamp="3313.62">Consider <i>Morning</i> <i>Edition</i>. Bob Edwards, the host of the show, says hello and he mentions a few stories coming up today. And then he says, &quot;First, this news from Carl Kasell.&quot; Why does he do that? Well, presumably, Carl is closer to reality. He's closer to the truth, he's closer to the thing being simulated in this simulated world on the radio. And then Carl starts in with the first story. Carl says, today in Russia, somebody did whatever.</p><p begin="00:55:42.23" data-timestamp="3342.23">And then he hands off to a reporter. And again, why does he hand off to the reporter? Well, presumably, the reporter is closer to reality, to the truth, to the thing being simulated in this simulated world. And then the reporter comes on. And the reporter says, OK, today the Russian president said whatever. And then the reporter goes on, at some point during their story, to some piece of tape that they're going to play us. And that piece of tape is maybe 12 seconds, maybe 14 seconds, 17 seconds long. And it's like one long sentence, or maybe two sentences of somebody, some Russian, saying something about something, right?</p><p begin="00:56:17.37" data-timestamp="3377.37">And at that point, we're there. We've gone as far as we can go. We are actually at the real. We're hearing a tape recording made out in the world. This is the real. We have arrived. And it's interesting that the word for that piece of tape in radio journalism is the &quot;actuality.&quot; Like, it's this precious piece of the actual world on the radio. Like you have this whole apparatus, and then finally you get to what's real. And what's funny about it is how puny it usually is. Like a sentence or two, you know? That's how much reality you get. That's how much actuality you get.</p></div>  </div>

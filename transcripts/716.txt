Basically, visitors who pay maybe $1 and walk through the house.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:00:37.89" data-timestamp="37.89"> You know, my great aunts, they treated the place like it was still the family home. We used to fill our pockets full of pebbles from the walkways up there, and go up on the second floor and third floor of the place and crawl out windows onto these parapets that are up around the top of Monticello and drop pebbles on the tourists that were walking around the house. And--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:06.44" data-timestamp="66.44"> Very asocial behavior.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:01:08.10" data-timestamp="68.1"> Yeah. And you're talking to one of-- probably the only person you'll ever talk to in your life that's actually jumped on Thomas Jefferson's bed. I mean, we would play on the bed. We'd go through that little doorway and up the stairs into the closet, which is above the bed there, and sit up there in the closet. And when tourists came through the room, we'd stick our faces out of the window up there and say boo and scare them. We'd hide out there. And so--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:38.65" data-timestamp="98.65"> There was no adult supervision at all.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:01:40.61" data-timestamp="100.61"> No, no adult supervision at all.</p><p begin="00:01:49.68" data-timestamp="109.68">We went through all the bedrooms and stuff up there. And then, of course, we'd play down in the basement rooms. And then of course what they call the dependencies, which were the slave rooms that run under the two wings that run out from Monticello itself.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:07.28" data-timestamp="127.28"> It would be decades before the slave quarters were part of the Monticello tour, Lucian says. He and his brother would explore and run around.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:02:18.63" data-timestamp="138.63"> And play hide and seek or whatever little boys did.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:24.27" data-timestamp="144.27"> It's amazing to me the degree to which it felt like a family home. Did it feel that way to you as a kid?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:02:30.34" data-timestamp="150.34"> You know, I have to tell you that all of this stuff in my family was just taken for granted.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:37.81" data-timestamp="157.81"> These days, of course, nothing about the legacies of the Founding Fathers is taken for granted. Right after he began his reelection campaign, he gave a speech at Mount Rushmore to make clear that he does not want to remove any monuments or rename any buildings or military bases.</p><p begin="00:03:11.21" data-timestamp="191.21">But this great, great, great-- whatever it is-- direct descendant of Thomas Jefferson, Lucian Truscott, he's got a special perspective on this. He led the charge to convince all the other white descendants of Thomas Jefferson to allow the Black descendants of Thomas Jefferson into their official family association.</p><p begin="00:03:27.04" data-timestamp="207.04">The Black family members, of course, trace back to Jefferson and Sally Hemings, one of the people he enslaved. Lucian thinks that everybody needs to get real about the slave-owning past of some of the Founding Fathers.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:38.66" data-timestamp="218.66"> Like, how do you feel about people taking down monuments of Thomas Jefferson?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lucian Truscott</h4><p begin="00:03:42.03" data-timestamp="222.03"> I think they ought to be taken down. I think that the great monument to Thomas Jefferson is Monticello. And I think it's an appropriate monument, because it actually puts Jefferson's life in context. It puts his slave-owning as part of his life and in context. If you visit Monticello today, you get to see where the slaves lived. You'll be shown where Sally Hemings's brother John did woodwork in Jefferson's bedroom, and study, and so forth.</p><p begin="00:04:14.34" data-timestamp="254.34">Slaves built that place. I never heard that slaves built that place when I was growing up. But it's talked about today. The history of Monticello at this point is about half Jefferson history, half slave history. I think that's appropriate. As a memorial, it tells the story of the whole man, not just this sort of god-like Thomas Jefferson we've all been raised to venerate.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:49.25" data-timestamp="289.25"> What are we supposed to do with the shameful parts of America's past? This is being talked about so much today. And back in the early days of our radio show, in the late '90s, one of our contributors, Sarah Vowell, did a story that took that on in this very vivid and complicated way.</p><p begin="00:05:04.05" data-timestamp="304.05">She set out on the road with her sister Amy to visit the site of a historic tragedy, one that involved part of their own family 160 years before that. And we wanted to replay it this holiday weekend because the questions that it addresses, the things that it is obsessed with, are all so much more talked about today than 22 years ago when she did that story.</p><p begin="00:05:25.26" data-timestamp="325.26">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. We take a road trip into history today. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:38.97" data-timestamp="338.97"> So Sarah Vowell and her sister Amy were born in eastern Oklahoma, in what was called Indian Territory before statehood. That's where our story ends, and that's also where it begins. They're enrolled citizens of the Cherokee Nation, like their parents, which, as you'll hear, is important to this story.</p><p begin="00:05:54.05" data-timestamp="354.05">Here's the opening line of Sarah's story from 1998.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Vowell</h4><p begin="00:05:57.47" data-timestamp="357.47"> Being at least a little Cherokee in northeastern Oklahoma is about as rare and remarkable as being a Michael Jordan fan in Chicago. I mean, who isn't?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:07.76" data-timestamp="367.76"> I'm guessing I don't need to explain that antique 1998 sports reference to many of you, but I do have one note before we play the rest of the story. And it's about the word "Injun." Sarah asked me to point out to anyone hearing this today that her grandfather was born in Indian Territory before statehood. And he used the word "Injun" as a term of affection. It was never derogatory. OK, so picking up, Sarah's part Cherokee.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Vowell</h4><p begin="00:06:31.22" data-timestamp="391.22"> It goes without saying that my twin sister Amy is, too, except that I have dark eyes and dark hair, and she's a blue-eyed blonde. And so our grandfather nicknamed me Injun and her Swede.</p><p begin="00:06:43.73" data-timestamp="403.73">Here's Amy's take on all this. I must point out that while my sister and I don't really look alike, we sound almost exactly alike. A hint for listening to this story, I'm usually the grouchy one. Here's Amy.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Amy Vowell</h4><p begin="00:06:57.71" data-timestamp="417.71"> I mean, those roles were assigned to us, you know, Indian and Swede, because of the way we looked. But it was also more like the things we were told about ourselves, and she was the one who was given the Cherokee language book. And I was the one who was always told how much alike I was to our Swedish grandmother. And I think I was probably 6 or 7 or something before I realized that I was Cherokee, too.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Vowell</h4><p begin="00:07:39.14" data-timestamp="459.14"> We're a little French, and Scottish, and English, and Seminole, too. Typical American mutts. But the Cherokee and Swedish sides of the family were the only genealogies anyone knew anything about. Here's what we knew about ourselves.</p><p begin="00:07:54.35" data-timestamp="474.35">Ellis Island, Trail of Tears. And I think, to a kid, Trail of Tears, the Cherokees' forced march from the East to Oklahoma, where we were born, seemed enormously more interesting, just as a name. Even the smallest children know what tears mean. And I think in my earliest understanding of where I came from, I pictured myself descended from a long line of weepers with bloodshot eyes.</p><p begin="00:08:21.41" data-timestamp="501.41">The Trail of Tears. Between 1838 and '39, the US army wrenched 16,000 people from their homes, rounded them up in stockades, and marched them across the country. 4,000 died.</p><p begin="00:08:37.47" data-timestamp="517.47">Every summer when we were children, our parents would drive us to a place about half an hour from where we lived called Tsa-La-Gi, which is the Cherokee word for "Cherokee." Tsa-La-Gi is the tribe's cultural center. There's a recreated village, a museum.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.56" data-timestamp="0.56">OK, this is one of those things that you probably have never heard, and then as soon as somebody tells you, you're like, right, of course. You know how murder figures into so much of American pop culture? On crime shows, and thrillers, and video games and all kinds of stuff. Well, if you knew somebody who actually got murdered, it turns out you might not be into that stuff so much.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:00:22.02" data-timestamp="22.02">I can't watch <i>Law</i> <i>&amp;</i> <i>Order</i>. Not going to watch <i>Law &amp;</i> <i>Order</i> <i></i> or play <i>Clue,</i> or go to a murder mystery dinner theater.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:32.96" data-timestamp="32.96">Rachel Howard's dad was killed when she was 10. And as an adult she's met a lot of people whose family members were killed. Though she says that some of them love shows like <i>CSI</i> and <i>Murder</i> <i>She</i> <i>Wrote</i>, any kind of murder show, officially, organizations like the group Parents of Murdered Children, take a stand against murder as entertainment.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:00:51.23" data-timestamp="51.23">You know, at the Parents of Murdered Children Conference, they have certain presentations really down to give you a little punch in the gut. And one of them is that they have a whole one on this murder mystery dinners. And the way that they always do it is they say, let's just pretend that you were going to have a rape mystery dinner and you were going to show up and the rule of the game was going to be that someone's been raped, and we're all going to find the rapist. That wouldn't go over. Nobody would do it. Everybody would feel that that was deeply distasteful.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:26.83" data-timestamp="86.83">Yeah, and creepy.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:01:28.74" data-timestamp="88.74">Yeah, creepy. Why would you want to put yourself in that role?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:34.87" data-timestamp="94.87">At these conferences for Parents of Murdered Children she says, everybody has a different way of dealing with their loss. Some meet with psychologists, some with crime investigators, some with psychics. But among the families who have a murder that is unsolved, it's common for them to be on a mission to find the killers and get justice. And this is where Rachel is different. A few years ago she wrote a memoir about her family and her dad's murder. The murder was never solved. The book is called <i>The</i> <i>Lost</i> <i>Night</i>, and she was invited to talk about it on a radio show called <i>The</i> <i>Victim's</i> <i>Voice</i>. And the woman who hosts this show, her daughter had been killed, and she was one of those parents who had made it her crusade to track down the killer at any cost.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:02:15.14" data-timestamp="135.14">And then she was telling me the whole story about her daughter's murder and how it had been for her to never give up hope. And I have to have you on my show because it'll get the word out there. You never know what's going to come up. Who knows something. Maybe a reward will come through and I have lots of contacts in the local detectives office. And if you want me to talk to someone about your dad's case.</p><p begin="00:02:42.10" data-timestamp="162.1">And so we had this very awkward conversation. It was quite a long phone call and at every turn I was kind of saying, I'm not really interested in doing that. I don't really want to do that. I talked to the detectives and if something comes up, great. I'll be happy to know about it. But you know, this isn't really what I'm about. And she just seemed baffled.</p><p begin="00:03:13.15" data-timestamp="193.15">I tried to say that I had reached a point where it didn't really matter to me anymore, looking for who killed him. And that what I was doing now, he would rather see me not thinking about it every day and just moving on.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:40.46" data-timestamp="220.46">Rachel did have years where she felt she should be trying to figure out who killed her dad. That if she didn't let it take over her life she was being selfish, it meant she didn't love him enough. And in writing her book she started to work the case. She talked to the police, tracked down leads, made freedom of information requests for documents, tried to convince detectives to release the case file to her. And after poking around like that for a while she asked a crime reporter to look at what she had. And he suggested that she give up.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:04:06.79" data-timestamp="246.79">I remember sitting in the living room that day and having that conversation and feeling liberated.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:13.42" data-timestamp="253.42">Why liberated?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:04:15.09" data-timestamp="255.09">Because this guy was telling me, look, I've looked at a lot of these cases and it doesn't sound like you have a whole lot here. And my first reaction was, good. I don't have to do this anymore. That's it. This guy just told me it's hopeless. Great. I'm free. I've done everything possible.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:39.63" data-timestamp="279.63">And do you worry about how heartless that might sound?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:04:45.02" data-timestamp="285.02">I mean, why does it sound heartless, though? Because it has nothing to do with how much I love my father. You know, why should that sound heartless? If you want to talk me about my father, I love him. I'll tell you all kinds of things about him. But why should it sound heartless to not have to keep looking for who killed him?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:14.90" data-timestamp="314.9">It took her years to get to this point, where she felt so resolved about all this. In the <i>Atlantic</i> <i>Monthly</i> years ago, a writer named Eric Schlosser wrote, &quot;Americans are fascinated by murder and murderers, but not by the families of people who are killed. One might expect that the families of murder victims would be showered with sympathy and support, embraced by their communities. But in reality, they're far more likely to feel isolated, fearful, and ashamed, overwhelmed by grief and guilt.&quot;</p><p begin="00:05:40.04" data-timestamp="340.04">Rachel says that she first read that when she was in college and she felt for the first time that there were other people like her, going through what she went through. That this wasn't like losing a parent any other way.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Howard</h4><p begin="00:05:49.70" data-timestamp="349.7">You know, everyone loses parents. You know, someone tells you, oh, my father died five years ago. Oh, I'm so sorry. That must have been very difficult. But you know that that's what we all have to go through so that person will work through it. It's not that way with murder. There's no guarantee that that person's going to work through it. They could be ruined by it forever.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:22.13" data-timestamp="382.13">This brings us to today's radio show, people doing their damnedest not to get ruined forever by a murder in the family. From WBEZ Chicago it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Today, How to Rest in Peace. Our show today in two acts. In act one, a son who knows that there's something wrong with him, something he has to fix when it comes to his mother's murder, and his feelings about it. In act two, a different son immerses himself in the facts of his mother's death, the cold, hard reality of it, starting long before she actually dies. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Dry Eyes And Videotape</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:04.28" data-timestamp="424.28">Act one. Well, we begin with this story in which a man tries to solve his problems using, among other things, videotape. Our reporter for this story is Brett Martin.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brett Martin</h4><p begin="00:07:14.85" data-timestamp="434.85">One day in March of 2005, a documentary film crew showed up at the house of a woman named Sumter Adolf in Westchester County, New York.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jason Minter</h4><p begin="00:07:22.44" data-timestamp="442.44">Well, should we-- I don't know. Should we sit down and talk? What's best in this situation?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sumter Adolf</h4><p begin="00:07:26.60" data-timestamp="446.6">You're the director.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jason Minter</h4><p begin="00:07:27.73" data-timestamp="447.73">Well, we really wanted to talk to you about sort of your experiences in the house. I don't know.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brett Martin</h4><p begin="00:07:32.94" data-timestamp="452.94">Mrs. Adolf had lived in the house for 21 years. It's an ordinary enough suburban home at the end of a long driveway with grey aluminum siding, patches of woods on either side, and an open field out back. She and her family love the house, but at some point they began noticing strange occurrences. They'd hear moaning and sighing. Sometimes the dishwasher would start by itself.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sumter Adolf</h4><p begin="00:07:54.93" data-timestamp="474.93">I wouldn't even say I was cognizant of things until my daughter-- I'm going to say five or six years ago-- woke up screaming in the night.

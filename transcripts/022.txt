  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.11" data-timestamp="1.11"> When I picked up the phone, it was my mom. And it had been about a month since we talked, which is not unusual and certainly my fault. Anyway, my mom said that she had been invited to speak with a group of women at the local Hadassah. My mom's a therapist in the Jewish suburbs outside Baltimore.</p><p begin="00:00:24.18" data-timestamp="24.18">And these Hadassah women had this group that met regularly. All of them were women in their late 40s through early 60s. And when the group first started meeting, they apparently discussed all sorts of things. It was wide ranging. But as time progressed, they realized that there was only one topic that they wanted to talk about and felt like they needed to talk about all the time, that they felt traumatized enough to have to talk about. And that was their relationships with their adult children.</p><p begin="00:01:04.80" data-timestamp="64.8">And at some point, this became the only thing that the group talked about, its official reason for being. And they had invited my mother to lead a discussion about how to get along with your adult children. So my mom is a big preparer. When she gets invited to talk on various psychological topics, she looks up articles, calls experts, talks to people. And as part of her preparation, my mom decided to call her own three adult children to see what advice they would give to the group about communicating with your children, about having a good relationship with your adult children.</p><p begin="00:01:46.91" data-timestamp="106.91">So my Mom had already talked to my older sister Randi out in California by the time she called me here in Chicago. And she explained to Randi, &quot;It's a thing about communicating with your adult children and how to be close to your adult children.&quot; And she asked my sister what advice she would give to the group.</p><p begin="00:02:06.95" data-timestamp="126.95">Randi's advice was direct and to the point. She said, &quot;Tell them to get a different leader.&quot;</p><p begin="00:02:25.12" data-timestamp="145.12">Adult children, supposedly adult children, and their supposedly adult parents in this addition of <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass, back for another week documenting everyday stories in these United States.</p><p begin="00:02:41.85" data-timestamp="161.85">Today, Act One, Me and My Mom.</p><p begin="00:02:45.84" data-timestamp="165.84">Act Two, Sandra Tsing Loh and her father.</p><p begin="00:02:49.86" data-timestamp="169.86">Act Three, Sandra Tsing Loh, Her Father, and His Mail-Order Chinese Wives. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

The kids, my sisters and I, we have one version of what happened when we were children. And that version tends to be kind of dark, actually. Not completely, but there's a lot of darkness in it. And our parents have a completely different story.</p><p begin="00:04:12.11" data-timestamp="252.11">And you know, I don't even think we need to come to a consensus. I don't think families need to agree about this stuff. I'm not even sure if they can agree. So today's program will not be an attempt to find this common story, but it will be an attempt to define that gap as it exists in a few families. Here, here. I will put her on the phone right now.&quot; Creeping into my father's voice is a tremulous tone that's sickeningly familiar. How many times had I heard it during my childhood as I was pushed towards the piano kicking and screaming? How many times?</p><p begin="00:39:31.31" data-timestamp="2371.31">But that was 20 years ago. I gulped terror back down. I live in my own apartment now, full of director's chairs, potted ficuses, and Matisse posters. I will be fine. My father has moved on to a totally new [? pushy ?] who picks up the phone, clears his throat, then bursts out triumphantly [SINGING IN CHINESE]</p><p begin="00:40:20.96" data-timestamp="2420.96">&quot;I have left you, Dr. Loh, and taken the Toyota. So there.&quot; Five weeks later, Liu just packs her suitcase, makes some sandwiches and takes off in the family Toyota. She leaves her note on the same Formica family dining room table at which she'd first won my father's heart. My father is in shock. Then again, he's philosophical.</p><p begin="00:40:44.27" data-timestamp="2444.27">&quot;That Liu, she was bad, that one. [SPITS] She says I do not like to give her gifts. She says I do not like to go out at night, and it is true. I do not. But I say go. See your friends in Chinatown. It is OK with me. I like it better when she leaves the house sometimes. It is more quiet. But Liu does not want to take the bus. She wants to drive the car. But you know me. I am your crazy old Chinese father. I do not like to pay for her auto insurance.&quot; And then he actually says, &quot;As with many Asians, Liu is a very bad driver.&quot;</p><p begin="00:41:27.61" data-timestamp="2487.61">&quot;Ha,&quot; is Kaitlin's only comment. &quot;Isn't it interesting how he seems to repel even his own kind?&quot;</p><p begin="00:41:44.68" data-timestamp="2504.68">Summer turns to fall in Southern California, causing the palm trees to sway a bit. The divorce is soon final, Liu's settlement including $10,000, the microwave, and the Toyota. Never one to dwell, my father has picked out a new bride, Zhu Ping, 37, homemaker from the Guangzhou province. I groan.</p><p begin="00:42:05.79" data-timestamp="2525.79">&quot;But no, Zhu Ping is very good,&quot; my father insists. He has had several phone conversations with her. &quot;And she comes very highly recommended, not, I have to say, like Liu. She was bad, that one. [SPITS] Zhu Ping is very sensible and hardworking. She has had a tough life. Boy. She worked in a coal mine in Manchuria until she was 25 years old. The winters there were very, very bitter. She had to make her own shoes and clothing. Then she worked on a farming collective where she raised cattle and grew many different kinds of crops by herself.&quot;</p><p begin="00:42:48.57" data-timestamp="2568.57">&quot;I'm sure she's going to fit in really well in Los Angeles,&quot; I say.</p><p begin="00:42:58.41" data-timestamp="2578.41">Zhu Ping, indeed, is a difference sort. The news, to my astonishment, comes from Kaitlin. &quot;I received--&quot; Her voice trails off, the very words seeming to elude her. &quot;A birthday card from Papa and Zhu Ping.&quot; My sister continues in a kind of trance of matter-of-factness, as if describing some curious archaeological artifact. &quot;On the cover, there is a clown holding balloons. It's from Hallmark. Inside, in gold lettering, cursive, it reads, 'Happy birthday, love Zhu Ping and your Daddy.'&quot;</p><p begin="00:43:42.06" data-timestamp="2622.06">&quot;Your what?&quot;</p><p begin="00:43:43.93" data-timestamp="2623.93">&quot;I think Zhu Ping put him up to this. The envelope is not addressed in his handwriting. Nonetheless,&quot; Kaitlin thinks it over, concurs with herself, &quot;yes, yes. I believe this is the first birthday card I've ever received from him in my life. The first. It's totally bizarre.&quot;</p><p begin="00:44:05.59" data-timestamp="2645.59">A week later, Kaitlin receives birthday gifts in the mail. A sweater, hand-knit by Zhu Ping, a box of moon cakes, a bunch of orchids. She is flipping out. &quot;Oh no,&quot; she worries, &quot;now I really have to call and thank her. I mean, the poor woman probably has no friends in America. Who knows what he's having her do? We may be her only link to society.&quot;</p><p begin="00:44:29.43" data-timestamp="2669.43">Kaitlin finally does call, managing to catch Zhu Ping when my father is out on the beach doing his exercises. Although Zhu Ping's English is very broken, she somehow convinces Kaitlin to fly down for a visit. It will be Kaitlin's first trip home since our mother's passing and my first meeting of either of my father's two Chinese wives.</p><p begin="00:44:54.69" data-timestamp="2694.69">I pull up the familiar driveway in my Geo. Neither Kaitlin nor I say anything. We peer out the windows. The yard doesn't look too bad. There are new sprinklers and a kind of intricate irrigation system made of a network of ingeniously placed rain gutters. Soil has been turned, and thoughtfully. Cypruses have been trimmed. Enormous bundles of weeds flank the driveway, as though for some momentous occasion.</p><p begin="00:45:19.99" data-timestamp="2719.99">We ring the doorbell. Neither of us have had keys to the house in years. The door opens. A short, somewhat plump Chinese woman in round glasses and a perfect bowl haircut beams at us. She's wearing a bright yellow &quot;I Hate Housework&quot; apron that my mother was once given as a gag gift and I think never wore. &quot;Kat-lin! Sand-wa!&quot; she exclaims in what seems like authentic joy, embracing us. She is laughing and almost crying with emotion.</p><p begin="00:45:51.11" data-timestamp="2751.11">In spite of myself, giggles begin to well up from inside me, as if from a spring. I can't help it. I feel warm and euphoric. Authentic joy is contagious. Who cares who this woman is? No one has been this happy to see me in ages.</p><p begin="00:46:07.92" data-timestamp="2767.92">&quot;Welcome home,&quot; Zhu Ping says, with careful emphasis. She turns to Caitlin, a shadow falling over her face. &quot;I am so glad you finally come home to see you daddy,&quot; she says in a low, sorrowful voice. She looks over her shoulder. &quot;He old now.&quot; Then, as if exhausted by that effort, Zhu Ping collapses into giggles. I sneak a glance over at Kaitlin, whose expression seems to be straining somewhere between joy and nausea.</p><p begin="00:46:40.88" data-timestamp="2800.88">I jump nervously in. &quot;It's so nice to finally meet you. How do you like Los Angeles? I've heard so much about your cooking.&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:50.10" data-timestamp="2810.1">My father goes off to put some music on his new CD player. &quot;That bad Liu made me buy it,&quot; he exclaims. &quot;But it is nice.&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:59.43" data-timestamp="2819.43">Zhu Ping bustles into the kitchen. &quot;Dinner ready in five minute,&quot; she declares.</p><p begin="00:47:06.69" data-timestamp="2826.69">Kaitlin waits a beat, then pulls me aside into the bathroom and slams the door. &quot;This is so weird,&quot; she hisses. We have not stood together in this bathroom for some 15 years. It seems somehow different. I notice that the wallpaper is faded, the towels are new. But no, it's something else. On one wall is my mother's framed reproduction of the brown da Vinci etching called <i>Praying</i> <i>Hands</i> which she had always kept in her sewing room. Right next to it, in shocking juxtaposition, is a green, red, and yellow Bank of Canton calendar, featuring a zaftig Asian female in a bikini.</p><p begin="00:47:47.24" data-timestamp="2867.24">&quot;I can't go through with this,&quot; Kaitlin continues in stage whisper. &quot;It's too weird. There are so many memories here. And not good ones.&quot; And like debris from a hurricane, the words tumble out. &quot;I go by the kitchen and all I can see is me standing before the oven clock at age five with tears in my eyes. He is yelling, 'What time is it? The little hand is most of the way to 4 and the big hand is on the 8. It was 3:18 22 minutes ago, so what time is it now? What's 18 plus 22? Come on. You can do it in your head. Come on. Come on.' I go buy the dining room and I see him hurling my Nancy Drew books across the floor. They slam against the wall and I huddle against Mom. Screaming. 'Why do you waste your time on this when your algebra homework isn't finished? You good for nothing! You're nothing! Nothing! You'll never amount to anything!' I go by the bedroom--&quot;</p><p begin="00:48:41.19" data-timestamp="2921.19">&quot;Please.&quot; I have this sickening feeling like I'm going to cry, that I'm just going to lose it. I want to just sit down in the middle of the floor and roll myself into a ball. But I can't. Kaitlin's rage is like something uncontainable, a dreadful, natural force, and I am the gate keeper. I feel if I open the door, it will rush out and destroy the house and everything in it. &quot;Please,&quot; is what I end up whispering. &quot;Please, let's just eat. We'll be done in an hour. Then we can go home, I promise. You won't have to do this again for another 10 years. Or maybe ever.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:32.65" data-timestamp="2972.65">At dinner, endless plates of food twirl their way out of the kitchen, Zhu Ping practically dancing underneath. Spinach, teriyaki-ish chicken, shrimp, some kind of egg thing with peas, dumplings packed with little pillows of pork. And amazingly, there is no want of conversational material. Photos from Shanghai are being pulled out of envelopes and passed around of her family, his family.</p><p begin="00:49:57.77" data-timestamp="2997.77">I do recognize three or four Chinese relatives, a cousin, an aunt, a grand uncle. Their names are impossible for me to remember. We had met them in China during our last trip as a family. I was 15. It was right before our mother started to get sick. Shanghai is a distant, confused memory for me of ringing bicycle bells and laundry lines hanging from buildings.</p><p begin="00:50:21.41" data-timestamp="3021.41">What I do remember is how curious my father's family had seemed about Kaitlin and me, his odd American experiment, ooh-ing over our height and touching our auburn hair. There were many smiles but no intelligible conversation, at least to our ears. We probably won't see any of these people again before we die.</p><p begin="00:50:42.26" data-timestamp="3042.26">But Zhu Ping will have none of it. Hearty Manchurian builder that she is, she is determined to use the crude two by fours of her broken English to forge a rickety rope bridge between us. &quot;You, Sand-wa, you. You play the piano, no? Mozart, he very nice. You will show me. And you, Kat-lin, you. You are a teacher, no? That is good, Kat-lin, good. Kat-lin, you are very, very good.&quot;</p><p begin="00:51:13.83" data-timestamp="3073.83">My father puts his spoon down. He is chewing slowly, a frown growing. &quot;This meat,&quot; he shakes his head, &quot;is very, very greasy.&quot; He turns to Zhu Ping and the lines of both sides of his mouth deepen. His eyes cloud. He says something to her in Chinese with a certain sharp cadence that makes my spine stiffen. Zhu Ping's face goes blank for a moment. Her eyes grow big. My stomach turns to ice.</p><p begin="00:51:44.66" data-timestamp="3104.66">How will she respond? By throwing her napkin down? Bursting into tears? Running from the room? Will she knock the table over, plates sliding after each other, sauces spilling, crockery breaking? Will we hear the car engine turn over as she drives off into the night, leaving us here frightened and panicked?</p><p begin="00:52:04.72" data-timestamp="3124.72">It is none of these things. Zhu Ping's head tilts back. Her eyes crinkle. And laughter pours out of her, peal after peal after peal. It is a big laugh, an enormous laugh, the laugh of a woman who has birthed calves and hoed crops and seen harsh winters decimate countrysides. Pointing to our father, Zhu Ping turns to us with large, glittering eyes and says, words which sound incredible to our ears. &quot;You Papa, he so funny.&quot;</p><p begin="00:52:42.45" data-timestamp="3162.45">My jaw drops. No one has ever laughed out loud at this table ever. We laughed behind closed doors, in our bedrooms, in the bathroom, never before my father. We laughed sometimes with my mother on those glorious days when he would be off on a trip.</p><p begin="00:52:59.11" data-timestamp="3179.11">But Kaitlin is not laughing. She is trembling. Her face is turning red. &quot;Why were you always so angry?&quot; she cries out in a strangled voice. It's a question she's waited 30 years to ask. &quot;Why were you so angry?&quot;</p><p begin="00:53:18.35" data-timestamp="3198.35">There is a shocked silence. My father looks weary and embarrassed. He smiles wanly and shrugs his thin shoulders. &quot;No, really,&quot; Kaitlin insists, &quot;all those years with Mama. Why?&quot;</p><p begin="00:53:35.32" data-timestamp="3215.32">&quot;I don't know,&quot; my father murmurs. &quot;People get angry.&quot; And I know in that moment that he doesn't have an answer. He literally doesn't. It's as if rage was this chemical that reacted on him for 20 years. Who knows why, but like some kind of spirit, it has left him now. The rage is spent. He is old now. He is old.</p><p begin="00:54:17.73" data-timestamp="3257.73">Dusk is fallen and long shadows fall across the worn parquet floor of the dining room. After a moment of silence, my father asks Zhu Ping to sing a song. The house frau from Guangzhou opens her mouth and with an odd dignity, sings simply and slowly.</p><p begin="00:54:37.16" data-timestamp="3277.16">My father translates. &quot;From the four corners of the Earth, my lover comes to me playing the lute. Like the wind over the water.&quot; He recites the words without embarrassment, almost without emotion. And why shouldn't he? The song has nothing to do with him personally. It is from some old Chinese fable. It has to do with missing someone, something, someplace, that perhaps one can't even define anymore.</p><p begin="00:55:12.92" data-timestamp="3312.92">As Zhu Ping sings, everyone longs for home. But what home? Zhu Ping, for her bitter winters? My father, for the Shanghai he left 40 years ago? And what about Kaitlin and I? We are even sitting in our own childhood home, and we long for it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sandra Tsing Loh</h4><p begin="00:55:49.85" data-timestamp="3349.85">Papa, why don't you just take us out with a song?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Eugene Loh</h4><p begin="00:55:53.42" data-timestamp="3353.42">This song, usually, I sing with my wife. Since today, Sandra didn't invite my wife, I have to let her stay in the kitchen at home. This song describes a young woman longing for her lover. It sounds like this.

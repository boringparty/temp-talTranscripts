  <h3>Act One: Christmas Lights and Fender Benders</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.48" data-timestamp="0.48"> Randy Amos was police chief in Albertville, Alabama, during years when lots of immigrants moved to town. And people in town sometimes did not know what to make of them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Randy Amos</h4><p begin="00:00:08.70" data-timestamp="8.7"> I remember I would get calls from people saying, you've got to do something about these Mexicans next door. I'd say, well, what are these Mexicans doing? And they said, well, they're devil worshipers. And I said, what do you mean they're devil worshippers? Well, they're over here killing goats.</p><p begin="00:00:23.88" data-timestamp="23.88">And of course, they would hear the cry of a goat when the throat was being cut, which is a bad sound. I agree. But they eat the goat. That was not a satanic ritual that they were going through.</p><p begin="00:00:37.56" data-timestamp="37.56">And so we'd go back to the caller and explain to them, look, these people are not worshipping Satan. I'm sorry that you had to hear the goat cry. But it's not violating any laws or ordinances that we have in existence here in Albertville, Alabama.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:58.52" data-timestamp="58.52"> Well, from WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. This is our second episode about Albertville, Alabama. You don't need to have heard last week's show to understand this one. But just in case you missed it, here's our premise, very quickly.</p><p begin="00:01:11.10" data-timestamp="71.1">The current attorney general, Jeff Sessions, wants to limit immigration to our country. His views have been instrumental in shaping administration policy. And as we explained in last week's show, his perspective comes from what he saw as a senator from Alabama when undocumented immigrants moved into poultry plants and other jobs around the state into towns like Albertville.</p><p begin="00:01:29.84" data-timestamp="89.84">In just 20 years, Albertville went from being 98% white to more than 1/4 Latino. Basically, there were 15,000 people and then another 6,000 arrived, mostly for jobs in the town's chicken processing plants. There's no official estimate of this, but the people who know the Latino community best say that more than half the Latino adults are living here illegally-- maybe a lot more than half.</p><p begin="00:01:53.49" data-timestamp="113.49">In the past, Jeff Sessions has mostly spoken about the effects of those immigrants on jobs and wages-- stuff that we talked about in last week's show. Last week, we stayed inside the chicken plants. But 6,000 new residents, non-white residents, in this little white town-- that also upended things outside the plants, to the point where Albertville became the statewide symbol in Alabama of everything that is wrong with immigration.</p><p begin="00:02:17.09" data-timestamp="137.09">At one point, one of the big national heavy hitters on the immigration issue, Kris Kobach, flew in and declared the city a disaster zone. He wrote, quote, "I have been all over the country fighting this fight, and I have seen the damage done by illegal immigration in cities of every size. But Albertville is in a class by itself." He described a town of mobile home ghettos and a city budget, quote, "thoroughly drained by illegal immigration."</p><p begin="00:02:45.23" data-timestamp="165.23">And the story of what really happened is so different from that. It's a mix of small local stuff in neighborhoods and in traffic-- traffic is actually a big deal-- and the local stuff sort of colliding with big national political forces that blew into town like a hurricane and reshaped how Albertville saw itself for a while-- but only for a while. And now, with the distance of a few years, it is actually possible to figure out the truth of what those 6,000 newcomers did to Albertville-- what they did to crime rates and schools and property values.</p><p begin="00:03:15.20" data-timestamp="195.2">It's possible to say what having them in town really cost taxpayers. And we're going to get to all that today. My coworker, Miki Meek, and I have interviewed over 100 people for this. Miki is co-hosting these two hours with me. Stay with us.</p><p begin="00:03:37.84" data-timestamp="217.84"></p><p begin="00:03:38.32" data-timestamp="218.32"></p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act Two: The March</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:38.72" data-timestamp="218.72"> Act one-- Fender Benders and Christmas Lights. So in the early years, back in the '90's, as people slowly realized that newcomers have arrived and there are lots of them, the problem with the newcomers isn't the stuff that you usually hear about when people talk about immigration. It's not jobs or wages or the cost to taxpayers. It's other stuff, neighbor to neighbor stuff, cars and yards and dead goats.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miki Meek</h4><p begin="00:04:01.80" data-timestamp="241.8"> The first immigrants to move to town were single Mexican men living together in trailer parks. They weren't in the neighborhoods yet. So unless you worked at a poultry plant, your first meaningful encounter with one of the newcomers was probably in your car. The number of hit-and-runs more than doubled in Albertville once immigrants moved to town. And everyone we met seemed to have a story of someone in their family getting into a wreck or fender bender. Again, here's Randy Amos, the police chief in Albertville back in the '90's, and Benny Womack, his successor.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Randy Amos</h4><p begin="00:04:31.28" data-timestamp="271.28"> First of all, they didn't drive very well and not familiar with the laws over here. It put an extra load on our officers answering those calls.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Benny Womack</h4><p begin="00:04:43.43" data-timestamp="283.43"> They would run a stop sign, run a red light, DUI. They didn't have a driver's license, and they wouldn't have insurance.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Randy Amos</h4><p begin="00:04:51.90" data-timestamp="291.9"> And it was almost a common practice. If they had a crash, and their vehicle was not disabled, they would run.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Benny Womack</h4><p begin="00:04:58.81" data-timestamp="298.81"> You know, you can understand that. Because they were in here-- in the US-- illegally. They didn't want to be in contact with law enforcement.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miki Meek</h4><p begin="00:05:08.77" data-timestamp="308.77"> I talked to a bunch of the guys who arrived in the early waves of Mexican workers about this. They said, yeah, it's true. Most of them didn't know what they were doing on the road. Here's Claudio, who was in our first episode. He's speaking through an interpreter.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Claudio</h4><p begin="00:05:21.35" data-timestamp="321.35"> [SPEAKING SPANISH]</p><p begin="00:05:22.96" data-timestamp="322.96"></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Interpreter</h4><p begin="00:05:23.89" data-timestamp="323.89"> He's like, I didn't know what that light was. I didn't know what that line meant.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Claudio</h4><p begin="00:05:27.63" data-timestamp="327.63"> [SPEAKING SPANISH]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Interpreter</h4><p begin="00:05:30.28" data-timestamp="330.28"> No, they didn't know. They would just, like, drive around, like, without doing absolutely anything, totally like idiots.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Claudio</h4><p begin="00:05:35.88" data-timestamp="335.88"> [SPEAKING SPANISH]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Interpreter</h4><p begin="00:05:37.26" data-timestamp="337.26"> You don't know what a stop was.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Claudio</h4><p begin="00:05:39.27" data-timestamp="339.27"> [SPEAKING SPANISH]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Interpreter</h4><p begin="00:05:40.31" data-timestamp="340.31"> You don't know what speed limit was.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Miki Meek</h4><p begin="00:05:41.70" data-timestamp="341.7"> Was that hard to figure out-- to learn all the rules?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Claudio</h4><p begin="00:05:44.19" data-timestamp="344.19"> Si. Si.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miki Meek</h4><p begin="00:05:46.50" data-timestamp="346.5"> They came from really rural places, where some of the guys told me they got around on a donkey or a horse. They didn't drive cars.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:53.26" data-timestamp="353.26"> Of the dozens of people we talked to, one of the people with the most interesting and complicated perspectives on the conflict between the Latino newcomers and the white people in town is Jeannie Courington. She was a city council member, a small business owner-- the kind of a town booster who opened the town's recycling center and then worked there for free for years just to get it off the ground.</p><p begin="00:06:13.57" data-timestamp="373.57">And she was one of the locals who stood between the two communities as a go-between, explaining one side to the other. And when things eventually got pretty heated between the two sides, she was one of the main voices in Albertville urging tolerance and understanding, though she started with some of the same feelings that other people had. Back in the '90's, when Mexican workers were first arriving in Albertville, she owned and ran the town's travel agency on Main Street, which, in those pre-internet days, people would come in to book flights home to Mexico. And she is very frank when she talks about what that was like for her.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jeannie Courington</h4><p begin="00:06:44.01" data-timestamp="404.01"> I mean, I remember when they started coming in, because it was so-- it kind of made you nervous. They usually would come in multiples. They didn't come alone. I guess they were as afraid of us as we were afraid of them.</p><p begin="00:06:58.03" data-timestamp="418.03">And there was just three women in the office. So it was kind of unnerving. Most of them kind of looked a little rough around the edges. [LAUGHS]</p><p begin="00:07:10.31" data-timestamp="430.31">It would be like if I was in a room with any minority. I'm just not used to-- that's just not my everyday, you know-- you just always are a little concerned in everything. Because it's the unknown. And the only thing you hear is the bad things. So it kind of unnerves you.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Miki Meek</h4><p begin="00:07:30.87" data-timestamp="450.87"> But she got used to being in a room with a minority.

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Spring 2008</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:05:22.04" data-timestamp="322.04"> The thing that got me interested in all this was something called a NINA loan. Back when the housing crisis was still a housing bubble, a guy on the phone told me that a NINA loan stands for &quot;no income, no asset,&quot; as in, someone will lend you a bunch of money without first checking to see if you have any income or any assets.</p><p begin="00:05:38.19" data-timestamp="338.19">And it was an official loan product, like you could walk into a mortgage broker's office, and they would say, well, we can give you a 30-year fixed rate, or we can put you in a NINA. He said there were lots of loans like this, where the bank didn't actually check your income, which I found confusing. And it turns out even the people who got them found them confusing.</p><p begin="00:05:55.38" data-timestamp="355.38">For example, a guy I met named Clarence Nathan, he worked three part-time, not very steady jobs, and made a total of $45,000 a year, roughly. He got himself into trouble and needed money, so he took out a loan against his house, a big one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:06:08.47" data-timestamp="368.47"> Call it 540 for round figures.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:06:11.07" data-timestamp="371.07"> You basically borrowed $540,000 from the bank, and they didn't check your income?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:06:15.92" data-timestamp="375.92"> Right. It's a no income verification loan. They don't call me up and say, how much money? They don't do that. It's almost like you pass a guy in the street and say, will you loan me $540,000? He said, well, what do you do? I ain't got a job. OK. It seems as if it's that casual. Even though there are a lot of papers that get filled out and stuff flies all over with the faxes and the emails and all like that, essentially, that's the process.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:06:55.40" data-timestamp="415.4"> Would you have loaned you the money?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Clarence Nathan</h4><p begin="00:06:57.21" data-timestamp="417.21"> I wouldn't have loaned me the money, and nobody that I know would have loaned me money. I think it came across as that you and everybody out there was out there preying on poor people.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Glen Pizzolorusso</h4><p begin="00:48:26.62" data-timestamp="2906.62"> Right. There was no malice, and I never set out to hurt anyone. I just did what I saw everyone else doing.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:48:40.60" data-timestamp="2920.6"> Talking to Glen now, he seems like a guy in the early stages of a major life change. He's going back to college. He loves school now. He used to always hate it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:48:49.52" data-timestamp="2929.52"> In fact, he does a lot of things he never used to do, like listen to the news and read books. He spends a lot of time with his kids now, no time at all with B-list celebrities. He wants to get his degree in theology and law and then go into politics. He says he really wants to do some good in the world.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:49:03.63" data-timestamp="2943.63"> But he points out, his big life change, at first, anyway, it was not his choice.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Glen Pizzolorusso</h4><p begin="00:49:08.42" data-timestamp="2948.42"> I have been humbled. I've been forced to be humbled. I used to look at mortgage applications, and the people, their income was $2,500 a month, $3,000 a month. And I used to think, how can people live on that? And I would welcome it now. I would be able to live on it so comfortably. I'm driving a car that has no payment on it. It's a piece of junk. And I used to think that it mattered. But it doesn't.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:49:52.36" data-timestamp="2992.36"> I'm picturing an alternative Glen, the Glen from the world where there was no bubble bursting, where the Glen who's still making $100,000 a month, who still has that lifestyle. And I am picturing meeting that Glen today, and I feel like I like this Glen a lot more.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Glen Pizzolorusso</h4><p begin="00:50:10.86" data-timestamp="3010.86"> Without a doubt. Well, because, how do I explain this, other than that Glen was about Glen. And this Glen is about what I can bring to-- trying not to sound cliche-- to society, what I can bring to my family, what I can do to make sure that we don't keep creating that Glen?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:50:50.00" data-timestamp="3050"> I feel like you're really emotional talking about this now. Why?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Glen Pizzolorusso</h4><p begin="00:50:57.76" data-timestamp="3057.76"> I don't know why. I think part of it is shame that I let the money take me over. I should've helped people when I was making that money. I should've done things that I could sit here and be proud of with it. And I didn't. I didn't do anything that I can really be proud of with it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:51:48.09" data-timestamp="3108.09"> So another character in our story that we wanted to check in on is the giant pool of money itself, all that money global investors have that they're looking to invest somewhere.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:51:57.67" data-timestamp="3117.67"> When we last met the giant pool of money before the financial collapse, it was big, $70 trillion. And it was growing fast. It had doubled in six years.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:52:07.30" data-timestamp="3127.3"> So we checked back in. We called the International Monetary Fund and found out a few interesting things. One, that 70 trillion number, that was wrong. The IMF had underestimated how much money there is and how fast it had grown. Back in 2007, that number really should have been almost $80 trillion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:26.48" data-timestamp="3146.48"> And today, two years later, we talked to the very same person, Ceyla Pazarbasioglu.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:52:31.75" data-timestamp="3151.75"> Very nicely done.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:32.55" data-timestamp="3152.55"> Thank you. After nearly two years of recession, the collapse of housing prices all over the world, the giant pool of money has, according to her, grown. The IMF thinks it is now about $84 trillion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:52:46.18" data-timestamp="3166.18"> Now, we were shocked that it grew at all.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:48.42" data-timestamp="3168.42"> Of course.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:52:49.12" data-timestamp="3169.12"> But here's the context. The bursting of the housing bubble did do some damage to the giant pool of money. The IMF thinks investors in subprime securities and the like lost at least $3 trillion. That's like losing all the money spent in a year in Russia and Canada combined.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:05.38" data-timestamp="3185.38"> So given all that, how did the giant pool of money get bigger? Well, two major things happened. One, investors pulled money out of stock markets all around the world and added it to the giant pool of money. Two, government stepped in. The Federal Reserve and central banks all over the world actually created trillions of new dollars and euros and yen. It's more new money than ever before, and much of that has made its way into the giant pool.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:53:32.28" data-timestamp="3212.28"> In other words, the giant pool of money would probably have shrunk by a good amount, except the world's governments and the world's central banks have been pumping out trillions of dollars to keep the world economy from complete collapse.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:44.99" data-timestamp="3224.99"> But while the size of the giant pool of money has gotten bigger, its attitude has gotten a lot smaller. Remember that army of investment managers?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:53:53.33" data-timestamp="3233.33"> The nervous guys?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:54.14" data-timestamp="3234.14"> Yeah. Well, two years ago, there wasn't anything in the world they wouldn't throw money at. They'd take any risk for a bit of return. Now, they're terrified. They want to invest in solid, safe, boring, low-interest government bonds. The safer, the better. One of the hottest items out there right now, one-month treasury bills, paying effectively 0% interest.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:54:15.95" data-timestamp="3255.95"> Meanwhile, as you've probably noticed, credit is tight everywhere. People with great credit scores and a lot of money in the bank have a hard time getting approved for home loans. Businesses can't convince anyone to lend them money to build new factories. Cities can't borrow money to build schools and hospitals. That's a big part of why we've been stuck in this recession.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:54:35.76" data-timestamp="3275.76"> The world's governments have tried to step in to supplement the giant pool of money, but the annual budgets of all the world's governments combined is less than $15 trillion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Adam Davidson</h4><p begin="00:54:45.68" data-timestamp="3285.68"> That's less than one fifth the size of the world's investments. It turns out, there's just not enough government in the world to replace the lending the giant pool of money used to do.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:54:56.82" data-timestamp="3296.82"> And that is the reason nobody can say with certainty when the economy will get better again. Because we're waiting for investors everywhere to feel safe to invest again. We're all waiting on the giant pool of money.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:55:09.00" data-timestamp="3309"> Alex Blumberg and Adam Davidson. It was after they did the first giant pool of money broadcast that our show and NPR News decided that lots of economics reporting like this might be a good idea as we headed into tough economic times. And we started this project, Planet Money. If you liked today's program, check out their thrice-weekly podcast-- you're going to be getting this three times a week-- and their blog at www.npr.org/money.</p></div>  </div>

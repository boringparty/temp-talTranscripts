  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.62" data-timestamp="0.62">From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. Today on our program, the story of two Americans who went to South Africa. One's white, one's black. And they went there because there's something about South Africa for them and for many Americans, something familiar and unfamiliar at the same time in a way that makes it mesmerizing.</p><p begin="00:00:22.01" data-timestamp="22.01">Robert Kennedy described this congruence of nations as well as anybody. In 1966, he was visiting South Africa. And he began a speech at the University of Cape Town this way--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robert Kennedy</h4><p begin="00:00:31.64" data-timestamp="31.64">I come here because of my deep interest and affection for a land settled by the Dutch in the mid-17th century, then taken over by the British, and at last independent. A land in which the native inhabitants were at first subdued, but relations with whom remain a problem to this day. A land which was once the importer of slaves and now must struggle to wipe out the last traces of that former bondage. I refer, of course, to the United States of America.</p><p begin="00:01:15.42" data-timestamp="75.42">[APPLAUSE]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:20.17" data-timestamp="80.17">That stunned pause before the audience begins applauding, I think that's so beautiful. More than England or France or Israel, more than Canada or Japan, when we think of South Africa, it is a more interesting mirror of the United States than nearly any country. I think because we glimpse a distant echo of the most frightening parts of our own country and the most inspiring parts.</p><p begin="00:01:55.40" data-timestamp="115.4">In the 1950s and '60s, black South Africans looked to the US for inspiration. Lewis Nkosi lived in Sophiatown. He remembers reading American literature like Langston Hughes' <i>Simple</i> <i>Speaks</i> <i>His</i> <i>Mind.</i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lewis Nkosi</h4><p begin="00:02:06.69" data-timestamp="126.69">We used to laugh so much about what he was saying, because it sounded to us like this was Johannesburg. And listening to the music, the films like <i>Stormy</i> <i>Weather</i> were just speaking for us.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:30.21" data-timestamp="150.21">&quot;Harlem was like our neighborhood,&quot; Lewis Nkosi says. Black South Africans in Sophiatown listened to Dizzy Gillespie and Charlie Parker and Miles Davis, tried to dress and talk like Lena Horne and Cab Calloway. In Sophiatown, gang members modeled themselves after Hollywood movies, dressed in zoot suits, picked up phrases from tough guy films like <i>Street</i> <i>with</i> <i>No</i> <i>Name.</i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lewis Nkosi</h4><p begin="00:02:49.06" data-timestamp="169.06">We wanted to sound like Americans, to relate to American culture. And we wanted also to fantasize about being black Americans rather than black South Africans, because the intensification of suffering in South Africa was just too great. America, it was a country where you have Thurgood Marshall working to eliminate a lot of the civil rights abuses and getting schools integrated and so on. So you know, this was a movement in the opposite direction to where we were moving.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Robert Kennedy</h4><p begin="00:03:43.01" data-timestamp="223.01">Nations, like men, often march to the beat of different drummers. And the precise solutions of the United States can neither be dictated nor transplanted to others. What is important, however, is that all nations must march toward increasing freedom, toward justice for all. You must do this not because it is economically advantageous, although it is, not because the laws of God commanded, although they do, we must do it for the single and fundamental reason that it is the right thing to do.</p><p begin="00:04:23.79" data-timestamp="263.79">[APPLAUSE]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lewis Nkosi</h4><p begin="00:04:26.88" data-timestamp="266.88">And looking to America as a land of hope, this is a cliche now. But we really did believe that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:41.87" data-timestamp="281.87">Now, of course, the situation is reversed. Once Nelson Mandela got out of prison, and especially after he entered the president's office, it's been black Americans who look to South Africa for inspiration. A few years ago, I did a series of stories for National Public Radio with a South African reporter, comparing race relations in the US with race relations in South Africa. And one of the most striking differences between the way that blacks in the two countries saw things was blacks in South Africa, they said they had hope.</p><p begin="00:05:16.40" data-timestamp="316.4">Of course, it isn't just black Americans who look to South Africa to see a place where things are changing, where things are getting better in small ways. Today, we've devoted our entire program to the story of two guys who look to South Africa that way. Their names are Josh Seftel and Rich Robinson. Both of them grew up in the normal morass of post-civil rights race relations in this country, went to schools that were integrated but where blacks and whites usually stayed separate, rarely mixed.</p><p begin="00:05:42.01" data-timestamp="342.01">So they headed to the new South Africa for a few weeks. And it turns out they did not agree on what they saw there. What we bring you today is partly a story of the emerging multiracial society over there and partly a story about the one here at home to contemplate this 4th of July weekend. Rich starts our story.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Act One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Rich Robinson</h4><p begin="00:06:01.27" data-timestamp="361.27">It was my idea to go to South Africa. I'd met this South African guy at work. He told me that South Africa is the most beautiful country in the world and that everything you see on TV about it is wrong. I asked Josh to go with me.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Seftel</h4><p begin="00:06:13.83" data-timestamp="373.83">I had another reason for wanting to visit South Africa. I had just gotten a letter from some South African guy named Benzion Seftel asking if we might be related. My family's very small. Besides my parents and two sisters, there's only a few Seftels in all of North America. I guess I was simultaneously excited and scared. I mean, it's not every day that the size of your family suddenly doubles.</p><p begin="00:06:37.19" data-timestamp="397.19">But when they're white South Africans, all these questions pop into your head, the first and most obvious question being, &quot;So do they hate black people?&quot; I mean, what do you do when your family doubles in size but the new half is a bunch of white supremacists? I decided not to share any of these worries with Rich.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Rich Robinson</h4><p begin="00:07:02.72" data-timestamp="422.72">Josh and I met 10 years ago in college. We ended up living in the same house, and we've lived together off and on since then. Josh is the son of a Jewish doctor in the suburbs. I grew up in the city. My parents are public high school teachers. He's white, I'm black, but none of this has ever been an issue. Before this trip, I don't think we ever had one serious talk about race.</p><p begin="00:07:22.90" data-timestamp="442.9">We both wanted to see the new South Africa, to see how much the country had changed since Nelson Mandela took power two years ago. We decided to begin our search in a neighborhood called Yeoville. The free magazine they give you on the plane had an article about it. Lots of glossy pictures of blacks and whites laughing together in the streets, drinking together in bars and clubs, eating together in restaurants.</p><p begin="00:07:43.23" data-timestamp="463.23">[CLUB MUSIC]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Seftel</h4><p begin="00:07:48.65" data-timestamp="468.65">Yeoville's a party. The street is lined with outdoor cafes, people are wearing Nike T-shirts, New York Yankees hats, all kinds of American stuff. The scene is just like the magazine pictures. Blacks and whites talk together and drive by us in BMWs and Mercedes. When Rich and I go into a convenience store to get a drink, there's a tall black man wearing a Public Enemy T-shirt in the corner. He's playing Pac-Man.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Yeoville Man</h4><p begin="00:08:11.69" data-timestamp="491.69">I tell you, things are going properly. We are just mixing up. Like, I'm talking to you guys. I think you are colored, yeah? You are colored?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Rich Robinson</h4><p begin="00:08:20.33" data-timestamp="500.33">We call it &quot;black&quot; in America.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Yeoville Man</h4><p begin="00:08:21.58" data-timestamp="501.58">Is it? OK, that's black. And you're &quot;white,&quot; right?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Josh Seftel</h4><p begin="00:08:24.01" data-timestamp="504.01">I'm white.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Yeoville Man</h4><p begin="00:08:24.52" data-timestamp="504.52">Yeah, sure, sure, sure. That's OK. I mean, that is positive. You can look like my woman. She has [? got a ?] white too, man.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Josh Seftel</h4><p begin="00:08:33.02" data-timestamp="513.02">Standing over by the pay phone, his girlfriend was plump and rosy in an African print jumpsuit and a red cashmere Kangol beret.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Yeoville Man</h4><p begin="00:08:40.13" data-timestamp="520.13">She's not from around here, but still, I've got here my woman, my white woman. Lovely. I love her to the maximum, I'll tell you that.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.27" data-timestamp="0.27"> Kate Aurthur became the rat columnist for <i>New</i> <i>York</i> magazine because she believed, in some way, that rats were at the heart of life in the Big City. Every New Yorker, she says, has a story about a rat. The singer and actor, Ru Paul, found an alley near City Hall with, he said, a thousand rats in it. If he'd fallen off his bicycle, he swore they'd a killed him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kate Aurthur</h4><p begin="00:00:19.23" data-timestamp="19.23">I think he was exaggerating. But I did go to that alley at night and it was, by far, the most rats I've ever seen in one place.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:29.18" data-timestamp="29.18">I'm picturing like a carpet of rats.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kate Aurthur</h4><p begin="00:00:33.01" data-timestamp="33.01">I was with a friend of mine who was driving, and we stopped at the end of the alley and turned out her lights. There's so much garbage, first of all, so they were just climbing all over the place. So it was very disturbing, because they were actually at our eye level. And I mean, it was terrifying. We're in a big truck and it didn't seem safe enough, like to be in an Isuzu Trooper. It seemed like they had the advantage. I mean, she was screaming so loudly that I became afraid of her. It was terrible. I was like drive, drive! It was terrible.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:21.61" data-timestamp="81.61">Well from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. Today on our program, stories of nature creeping into the most man-made environments possible. Nature sneaking in places where it's very existence is a rebuke to the notion that we, as a species, have it under control. When vermin infiltrate your house, or my house, there's this feeling of it's us versus them. It's a contest with nature where it suddenly feels like nature might actually win.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 1</h4><p begin="00:01:53.54" data-timestamp="113.54">I'm from Brooklyn. I mean, I'm from the streets of Brooklyn. I was born, raised, and probably will die.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:58.20" data-timestamp="118.2">This is from this great documentary by Mark Lewis called-- simply enough-- Rats. <i></i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 1</h4><p begin="00:02:03.51" data-timestamp="123.51">And I mean, I know what a mentality is, a street mentality is. I know when somebody takes on that type of a role. And I when I looked into the eyes of the rats, I mean, I could see that they had an intelligence just like in the street where it's not just one kid, you're facing up to 20 kids. They all have the single-minded presence of mind to do what they have to do to you.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:28.56" data-timestamp="148.56">Usually, when we talk about man versus nature, and we build these urban environments and we quash nature and we control nature, there's a part of us that believes that we should be rooting for nature. That nature means beautiful greenery and the clear blue sky and the deep blue sea. But when weeds breakthrough our perfectly paved sidewalk, when the cockroaches march in, when the rat arrives, we remember why we wanted to tame nature in the first place.</p><p begin="00:02:55.48" data-timestamp="175.48">Rats showed up at this guy's house, and he tried ceiling walls and he tried poisoning them, he tried chasing them and killing them with a two by four, and still they came, and multiplied. Terrorizing his family, finally forcing them to move.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 1</h4><p begin="00:03:08.54" data-timestamp="188.54">I really do believe that they knew that they won. Because remember, when you do look inside of his eyes, you can see he's got a soul. He's got something inside of him, and he's looking right back at you. He wants you just as bad as you want him. It's you or him. I know when I was there I wished the rats dead. If I could of, I would have killed each one by hand myself personally. It brought my family terror, horror, economic grief. So yeah, I definitely held a grudge against them. They scarred me and they scarred my family, and will always be with us.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:48.15" data-timestamp="228.15">Well today on our program, five stories of nature seeping into man-made environments, stripping the veneer of civilization off of us in the process. Act one, Interpretation of Dreams. In which we hear how an entire nation has coped with the sheer trauma of what it means to knock down trees, rip into the earth, and all the other things you have to do to nature when you build a modern, industrial city. Act two, One Brave Man Stands Up For What's Right. In which one of America's best known politicians goes mono a mono on behalf of human beings against the untamed wilderness.</p><p begin="00:04:21.72" data-timestamp="261.72">Act three, Church of Latter Day Snakes. Scott Carrier tells the true story of one family experiment at bringing the outdoors indoors. Act four, God Bless the Hamster Who Has His Own. In which an average high school student explains why it is bad luck for any animal to become her pet. Act five, Waiting For the End of the World. The writer Iggy Scam hits the road to test a theory about the men who fish. Maybe you've seen these guys right in the middle of the Big City. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Interpretation Of Dreams</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:58.47" data-timestamp="298.47">Act one, Interpretation of Dreams. Building everything that we think of as modern life, cities and suburbs both, means trammeling nature. And that bothers some people, they want to keep nature at hand. Even if they're living in the heart of the city. Even if the part of nature they're trying to preserve is something most of us can't see and never notice. David Rakoff brings us this case example.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:05:21.16" data-timestamp="321.16"> Grasteinn, literally gray rock, is a famous boulder in Reykjavik, Iceland. It sits cracked in two, fat and satisfied on its own square of sod. On two occasions in the last 30 years, plans were underway to expand the road and detonate the rock. Both times, locals came forward and protested the destruction, claiming that the rock was inhabited by &quot;hidden people.&quot; Invisible beings from another dimension who, along with elves and trolls, are a mainstay of Icelandic folklore.</p><p begin="00:05:51.18" data-timestamp="351.18">And both times, despite a complete lack of concrete evidence, indeed despite a complete lack of even a history of tales attached to that rock, the Icelandic Public Roads Administration heeded these protests and spared the boulder.</p><p begin="00:06:09.77" data-timestamp="369.77">Prior to my arrival, Victor Ingolfsson, chief of the publishing unit for the Public Roads Administration-- their PR guy-- had sent me an article he had written that explained the decision. It is titled, &quot;The Public Roads Administration and the Belief in Elves.&quot; It turns out to be quite beautifully written. In addition to his day job, Victor is a novelist. His third book has been on the national bestseller list for months. But its tone is an interesting mixture of exasperation and respect for the feelings of others.</p><p begin="00:06:41.13" data-timestamp="401.13">He writes, &quot;A lot of time goes into answering the same old questions. It is hope that this text will rectify the situation and it can be looked upon as the author's interpretation of the PRA's view on the issue. It will not answer the question of whether the PRA's employees do or do not believe in elves and hidden people because opinion differs greatly, and it tends to be a rather personal matter. However, you may assume that the author severely doubts the existence of such phenomena.&quot;</p><p begin="00:07:10.47" data-timestamp="430.47">In person, I ask him about this.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:07:14.37" data-timestamp="434.37">This belief in these hidden people is primarily a quaint folk tradition, not really believed by many people.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Viktor Ingolfsson</h4><p begin="00:07:24.75" data-timestamp="444.75">Yeah that's right.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:07:27.05" data-timestamp="447.05">I wonder why you guys make any concessions?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Viktor Ingolfsson</h4><p begin="00:07:32.06" data-timestamp="452.06">Of this? Because the people who do believe, they are pretty serious about it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:07:39.00" data-timestamp="459">But certainly we have people who believe in a lot of things that nobody else believes in, in the larger community, and we would never even think--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Viktor Ingolfsson</h4><p begin="00:07:47.03" data-timestamp="467.03">But this is a small community. So basically, everyone knows everyone, almost. So you really have to listen to everyone because you are probably going to meet them at a party after awhile. You know, when you scream at someone in traffic in New York, you know you're probably not going to meet them again, so you do it. But not so much here I think.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Arni Bjornsson</h4><p begin="00:08:21.36" data-timestamp="501.36">I heard stories as a child, yes. But I don't think that people took it seriously.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:08:28.89" data-timestamp="508.89">Arni Bjornsson is a cultural anthropologist and head of the ethnological department of the National Museum. He is a sweet-looking, beetle-browed grandfatherly type. A man in his 70s, who, like Viktor, also seems wistfully regretful that the pretext that gets someone like me onto an airplane and over to Iceland is to scrutinize this daffy, essentially non-representative aspect of their society.</p><p begin="00:08:53.26" data-timestamp="533.26">Imagine you were outside of a jazz club, a place devoted to a quintessentially American art form, and a reporter from another country comes up and microphone in hand, starts quizzing you on why so many high-rises here don't have 13th floors.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Rakoff</h4><p begin="00:09:07.82" data-timestamp="547.82">Now, but when you were a child, did you believe in them?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Arni Bjornsson</h4><p begin="00:09:11.61" data-timestamp="551.61">I didn't believe in them, but I didn't exclude the possibility.

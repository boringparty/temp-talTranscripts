If the only way to sell the story to Americans was to lop off its conclusion, then so be it.</p><p begin="00:48:59.63" data-timestamp="2939.63">So now there were two <i>Clockwork</i> <i>Oranges</i> in the world with two different endings depending on where you lived. Burgess writes about this whole mishigas and how he felt about it in an introduction to a later edition of the book. I just want to read-- this is probably my favorite part of what he says.</p><p begin="00:49:18.77" data-timestamp="2958.77">"Now, when Stanley Kubrick made his film, though he made it in England, he followed the American version and, so it seemed to his audiences outside America, ended the story somewhat prematurely. Audiences did not exactly clamor for their money back, but they wondered why Kubrick left out the denouement. People wrote to me about this. Indeed, much of my later life has been expended on Xeroxing statements of intention and the frustration of intention while both Kubrick and the New York publisher coolly bask in the rewards of their misdemeanor. Life is, of course, terrible."</p><p begin="00:49:55.00" data-timestamp="2995">It's funny, but it was also endlessly frustrating to Burgess. He wrote that he didn't think the American edition and thus the movie was a fair depiction of human life. It's as inhuman to be 100% evil as it is to be 100% good. The two need to coexist. He was unequivocal about that.</p><p begin="00:50:13.11" data-timestamp="3013.11">Further, when the film came out, there was a moral panic about it, both in the UK and here in the States. And it wasn't just the violence people were upset about. It was the ending. An editor for <i>The</i> <i>New</i> <i>York</i> <i>Times</i> wrote in the Arts and Leisure section of the paper, "The thesis that man is irretrievably bad and corrupt is the essence of fascism." I can't help but think how all of this might have been different if that last chapter had never been cut.</p><p begin="00:50:40.88" data-timestamp="3040.88">And that, for years, was everything I knew. But then recently, as I was getting ready to tell all of this to you, O, my brothers, I thought I should actually do some research, make sure I got my facts straight. And as with <i>A</i> <i>Clockwork</i> <i>Orange</i> itself, it turns out there's a whole other chapter to this saga, one that I didn't know existed. To start with, that quote from Burgess I read earlier that ends with "Life is, of course, terrible."</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:51:08.39" data-timestamp="3068.39"> That's a very entertaining account of the story. I think it's inaccurate in various ways.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:51:14.07" data-timestamp="3074.07"> This is Andrew Biswell. He's spent more than 25 years researching Burgess in part for his aptly titled book <i>The</i> <i>Real</i> <i>Life</i> <i>of</i> <i>Anthony</i> <i>Burgess.</i> It wasn't always the easiest job.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:51:24.29" data-timestamp="3084.29"> He would embroider, and he would be more concerned with telling a good story than with sticking to factual accuracy. Now, I'd been going through the manuscript of <i>A</i> <i>Clockwork</i> <i>Orange</i> as part of my research into Burgess.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:51:38.48" data-timestamp="3098.48"> The original manuscript, the one Burgess sent around to his editors in England and America.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:51:42.83" data-timestamp="3102.83"> And just turning the pages and noting any annotations on the typescript. And I remember coming to this note in his own handwriting, which says at the end of chapter 20, "Should we end here?"</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:51:58.19" data-timestamp="3118.19"> "Should we end here? An optional "epilogue" follows." "Epilogue" is in quotes. Again, this was at the end of the second-to-last chapter, where Alex turns bad again.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:52:09.67" data-timestamp="3129.67"> And what did you think when you saw it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:52:11.72" data-timestamp="3131.72"> I nearly fell off my chair. I was very surprised, because I'd grown up with the Burgess introductions and commentaries on his book. And up until that point, I'd been inclined to believe them. And this question, "Should we end here?" I was surprised by the level of doubt.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:52:31.28" data-timestamp="3151.28"> Surprised because Burgess publicly was so emphatic that he had been forced to cut the last chapter and that it was the wrong decision. And when Andrew looked into it further, he found that Burgess's editor in America, Eric Swenson, never insisted on scrapping the last chapter. Yes, he thought it was Pollyanna-ish and, quote, "unconvincing," but getting rid of it wasn't a condition of publication.</p><p begin="00:52:53.24" data-timestamp="3173.24">Not only that, this guy Swenson said Burgess agreed with his opinion and that Burgess told him he'd only added the 21st chapter because the British publisher wanted a happy ending. Also Burgess wrote his own screenplay for <i>A</i> <i>Clockwork</i> <i>Orange</i> that ended at the same place Kubrick's screenplay did, no redemption. And then years later, Burgess wrote a musical, yes, a musical version of the story, which reverted back to the longer redemptive ending and took it even further.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:53:22.04" data-timestamp="3202.04"> Alex goes off with his girlfriend, and they're going to get married.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:53:25.01" data-timestamp="3205.01"> Oh!</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:53:25.49" data-timestamp="3205.49"> That's right. Yeah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:53:26.39" data-timestamp="3206.39"> Is she a character, or is she offstage somewhere?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:53:29.08" data-timestamp="3209.08"> No, no. She appears and speaks. She's called Marty.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:53:31.79" data-timestamp="3211.79"> [LAUGHS]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:53:32.81" data-timestamp="3212.81"> And then the play has a prologue in the Garden of Eden, where Alex and Marty play Adam and Eve. It's very confusing. The whole thing is messy. It's strange that he tries to pin this on other people, whereas the reality is that it's like the good angel and the evil angel are dictating sort of different endings to him.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:53:55.74" data-timestamp="3235.74"> So in the end, which ending do you think that Burgess thought of as the better ending?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:54:00.71" data-timestamp="3240.71"> By the time you get to the 1980s and he's making his stage adaptation, he's coming down in favor of chapter 21 as the correct or the authorized ending.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:54:11.84" data-timestamp="3251.84"> And what does that say, do you think, about his worldview, like about what he believed about the true nature of human beings?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Andrew Biswell</h4><p begin="00:54:22.31" data-timestamp="3262.31"> Well, the big thing that had changed in his life was that he had a son by his second marriage and a very wayward son. He was, I suppose, worried that this person should do well in the world. Yeah. I suppose Burgess in the '80s, he's much more of a protective father figure.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sean Cole</h4><p begin="00:54:42.92" data-timestamp="3282.92"> Which, if that's the reason, makes so much sense. When you have a kid, especially one you're worried won't turn out well, you have to believe people can change like Alex finally changed, dreaming of his own son. It's like they literally ended up on the same page, Burgess and Alex. One of them happened to have typed out that page while the other danced across it in a jockstrap and suspenders. They both grew up.</p><p begin="00:55:10.71" data-timestamp="3310.71">Funnily, Andrew Biswell says he prefers the shorter ending. Just thinks it makes for a tougher book, although he goes back and forth, he says. Depends on what day you ask him.</p><p begin="00:55:19.08" data-timestamp="3319.08">Me, I come down where Burgess ultimately did. I like believing that we can grow into better versions of ourselves. And besides all that, you get to see Alex walk off into the sunset. On the last page he says, "Farewell from your little droog." Should we end here?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:55:48.21" data-timestamp="3348.21"> Sean Cole is one of the producers of our show.</p><p begin="00:56:01.86" data-timestamp="3361.86">[MUSIC - "TRY A LITTLE KINDNESS" BY GLEN CAMPBELL]</p><p begin="00:56:02.37" data-timestamp="3362.37"></p></div>  </div>

  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.15" data-timestamp="0.15">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> distributed by Public Radio International.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lance Corporal</h4><p begin="00:00:04.20" data-timestamp="4.2">My [UNINTELLIGIBLE] sergeant put me on a deployment roster to go to the Middle East. And I was told to go to a pre-deployment brief.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:13.88" data-timestamp="13.88">This guy's a Lance Corporal from the Marines' Eight Communication Battalion at Camp Lejeune, North Carolina. At his pre-deployment briefing to go overseas for whatever is going to happen next in Iraq, there were maybe 300 Marines.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lance Corporal</h4><p begin="00:00:24.95" data-timestamp="24.95">You know, folding chairs. There was a projection screen being used to show us the plan of attack.</p><p begin="00:00:33.25" data-timestamp="33.25">OK, this is where we're going to be landing. And then we're going to travel here. You'll be wearing this type of gear. They also taught us about Saddam Hussein's military. You know, how much he has. And after that, this other speaker came out speaking about the Arab culture. He was a sergeant and how the Marine Corps works per se, this guy is not specializing in Arab culture. You know, he was probably asked to do a course on Arab culture two weeks prior to the situation. He did some research, per se, and gave the class.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:19.43" data-timestamp="79.43">In the class, the sergeant explained what Arabs eat. That it gets to 140 degrees in the desert. The different ethnic groups.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lance Corporal</h4><p begin="00:01:25.60" data-timestamp="85.6">Then he said to us, when you go over there, make sure you do not shake an Arab with your left hand. And we're all wondering, why? So he says and the reason is because over in Iraq, over in the Middle East, they wipe their asses with their left hand because they don't have any toilet paper. And so I'm standing there and I could see people's faces. And they were like in disgust. They're like, &quot;Oh man. Wow. That's just nasty.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:06.37" data-timestamp="126.37">This Marine told us that he thought the only reason the sergeant said that was to make the soldiers think that Arabs are disgusting, to make them hate. Who knows? Honestly, who knows? We checked into it and it's true. Lots of people in Iraq and Pakistan, and lots of other countries near there, do not use toilet paper. People wash their hands. But that sergeant really might have been trying to teach them how to be sensitive to local customs, just didn't do the very best job of it.</p><p begin="00:02:36.29" data-timestamp="156.29">Once there's a war going on, everyone is suspicious of the information they're getting. Nothing is trustworthy. And when a war is over, it's still hard to tell what the truth was. For instance, the number of Iraqi deaths in the Persian Gulf War back in 1991, do you know? No one knows. Just after the war, the US Defense Intelligence Agency made a very rough estimate of 100,000. The British government says 30,000. The US House Armed Services Committee estimated 9,000. The PBS show <i>Frontline</i> estimated maybe 27,000.</p><p begin="00:03:10.95" data-timestamp="190.95">Well today on our program, we bring you three stories, each one from a different war. Each one trying to get you the real true story behind that war.</p><p begin="00:03:20.66" data-timestamp="200.66">Act One of our show, Jarheads. In that act, a marine reminds us just how scary it was to fight against Saddam Hussein the last time around if you weren't just watching on TV.</p><p begin="00:03:31.96" data-timestamp="211.96">Act Two, what's the truth good for anyway. An act which asked the question, if Israelis looked back at their war of independence and decided that maybe they shared some blame for Palestinians having their country, would it change anything?</p><p begin="00:03:46.92" data-timestamp="226.92">Act Three, Jar Jar Heads. In that act, one brave and funny man doesn't simply try to rewrite the history of a war, he tries to rewrite the movie of the history of the war. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Jarhead</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:06.52" data-timestamp="246.52">Act One.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:04:07.76" data-timestamp="247.76">I'm Anthony Swofford and I served in the US Marine Corps from December of 1988 through December of 1992. And when I left the Marine Corps I was a corporal.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:20.17" data-timestamp="260.17">And Anthony Swofford is here because he agreed to read an excerpt from his memoir, <i>Jarhead:</i> <i></i> <i>A</i> <i>Marine's</i> <i>Chronicle</i> <i>of the</i> <i>Gulf</i> <i>War</i> <i></i> <i>and</i> <i>Other</i> <i>Battles.</i></p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:27.36" data-timestamp="267.36">And Anthony, before we start, I want to do a quick rundown of some of the words that are going to appear here and there during the readings so nobody gets lost. Give me just quick definitions to these. Ruck.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:04:39.13" data-timestamp="279.13">Ruck is a pack, like a knapsack.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:41.96" data-timestamp="281.96">Dope.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:04:43.34" data-timestamp="283.34">Well, dope is any sort of information that helps you at all in the field, such as the distance you are from a target, or the wind speed.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:54.70" data-timestamp="294.7">Or intelligence?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:04:55.74" data-timestamp="295.74">Or any kind of intelligence, yes.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:57.99" data-timestamp="297.99">Freqs. That's F-R-E-Q-S.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:05:01.57" data-timestamp="301.57">Those are the radio frequencies that are being used on the battlefield.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:05.70" data-timestamp="305.7">A grand.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:05:06.62" data-timestamp="306.62">A grand is one kilometer.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:10.88" data-timestamp="310.88">STA. That's S-T-A. It appears all through this story.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:05:15.54" data-timestamp="315.54">STA is the acronym for the platoon I served in. It stands for Surveillance and Target Acquisition.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:23.75" data-timestamp="323.75">I hope this next one isn't too naive. Click. This is one that I feel like I've seen in a million war movies and they always say, go three clicks over, or five clicks over. And I've always wondered, how far is a click?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:05:35.64" data-timestamp="335.64">Yeah, a click is one kilometer. A thousand meters.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:42.23" data-timestamp="342.23">Well, with that preparation, here then is Anthony Swofford, reading from his memoir of the Persian Gulf War.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Anthony Swofford</h4><p begin="00:05:48.50" data-timestamp="348.5">The oven heat of the Arabian Desert grips my throat. In the distance, the wind blows sand from the tops of dunes, beige waves that billow through the mirage. Just beyond the tarmac, artillery batteries point their guns east and north. Fighter jets patrol the sky.</p><p begin="00:06:06.63" data-timestamp="366.63">Our days consist of sand and water and sweat and piss. We look north toward what we're told is a menacing military, 400,000 or more of war torn and war savvy men. Some of the Iraqi soldiers who fought during the eight year war with Iran got their first taste of combat when we were 10 years old.</p><p begin="00:06:26.74" data-timestamp="386.74">While fighting Iran, the Iraqis became experts at fortifying their border with mines and obstacles, such as the 30 kilometer long lake they created to defend the city of Basra. We're forced to wonder what the Iraqis are preparing for us at the Saudi-Kuwaiti border.</p><p begin="00:06:43.17" data-timestamp="403.17">In 1983 and 1984, they use mustard and nerve gas against the Iranians. And since then, they've dropped nerve gas on the Iraqi Kurds. We believe they'll do the same to us. We wait for the Iraqi army. This is our labor, we wait.</p><p begin="00:07:05.78" data-timestamp="425.78">Knowing the reporters will arrive soon, we shave for the first time in a week. Our chief scout sniper gathers the platoon in a school circle under the plastic infrared cover. He has already recited a list of unacceptable topics. He's ordered us to act like top Marines, patriots, the best of the battalion.</p><p begin="00:07:25.46" data-timestamp="445.46">We're prohibited from divulging to the press data concerning the capabilities of our sniper rifles or optics and the length and intensity of our training. Basically, don't get specific, he says. Say you can shoot from far away. Say you're highly trained. That there are no better shooters in the world than marine snipers. Say you're excited to be here and you believe we'll annihilate the Iraqis. Take off your shirts and show your muscles.</p><p begin="00:07:54.77" data-timestamp="474.77">The reporters arrive at 09, 9 o'clock.

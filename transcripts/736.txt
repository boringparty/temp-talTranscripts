  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:11.78" data-timestamp="11.78"> We all know how it is today. For most of the year, medical experts and government officials told us all to wear face masks. Half of us don't do it. Now that we finally have a vaccine, a third are hesitant to take it or say they definitely will not take it. But it wasn't always like this. Americans used to do what they were asked by medical authorities, mostly. Like when the 1918 flu pandemic hit the United States, killing similar numbers of Americans that COVID is killing today. Let's look at one American city that did a pretty good job minimizing deaths back then. Howard Markel writes about the history of American epidemics.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:00:47.76" data-timestamp="47.76"> So a great example, the example I love to use, is St. Louis, Missouri. And they had a very adept commissioner of health named Max Starkloff.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:59.75" data-timestamp="59.75"> Commissioner of health is the local official, usually a doctor, who tells everybody what to do when there's an epidemic or other health emergency. For instance, Max Starkloff closed schools. He ended public gatherings of all sorts. He ordered people who were sick to be quarantined. Dr. Starkloff was a good political operator.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:01:16.01" data-timestamp="76.01"> But he was also very good at communicating to the public and made twice-daily statements that were reported in the newspapers. And you have to remember, back then, most newspapers had six or more editions per day. So they updated them, much like CNN would update its stories.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:33.14" data-timestamp="93.14"> What was his messaging? Do you know?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:01:35.03" data-timestamp="95.03"> Yeah, it was like, be a good St. Louisian, I remember. You know, and don't spread the flu. It is a socially mediated disease, and we've got to work together.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:45.57" data-timestamp="105.57"> Howard says Dr. Starkloff had all kinds of messages, telling people to wash their hands, don't cough on others. And because medicine was so primitive back then, they hadn't actually isolated the flu virus. So they had no way to confirm if you had it. They couldn't test you. So the only way they could keep track of the disease was that people who were sneezing and sick told authorities. So Starkloff would tell the public--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:02:08.10" data-timestamp="128.1"> These are the symptoms that you need to call us about. You need to communicate this to us. That's where the word "communicable disease" comes from, by the way. Not so much that it's infectious, even though it is, but that one source is communicating it to a higher source and to a public health department.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:27.49" data-timestamp="147.49"> Oh, wow.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:02:28.35" data-timestamp="148.35"> Yeah. So that works because if you can get people on board with that, they're self-isolating, they're self-reporting, they're self-quarantining and so on. So that's good.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:38.60" data-timestamp="158.6"> To be clear, not everybody was on board. There's pushback from the public in every American epidemic, Howard says. German immigrants rioted rather than get vaccinated in the 1880s. During the 1918 flu, 2,000 people showed up to protest a mandatory face mask law in California. But mostly, Howard Markel says, people complied. He says they trusted scientists more back then, even though scientists knew so much less than they know today.</p><p begin="00:03:03.98" data-timestamp="183.98">And also, remember, 1918, the country was at war. It was World War I. So there's a lot of patriotic "let's all pull together" feeling in the country. But maybe the biggest reason that people listened to public health authorities back in 1918-- self-interest. Avoiding public gatherings, quarantines, that was about all you could do to protect yourself and your loved ones, given the state of medicine back then.</p><p begin="00:03:27.97" data-timestamp="207.97">Most everybody knew somebody who'd died of an infectious disease, Howard says. Two of five babies died before their fifth birthday, many of them from infectious diseases. And Howard says epidemics were coming through every year or so, diseases we had no treatment for.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:03:42.04" data-timestamp="222.04"> Yeah, whether there was a diphtheria or a whooping cough epidemic, or occasional smallpox epidemics, or measles epidemics, you know, they were frequent enough that you were quite used to epidemics, particularly if you were a parent.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:58.21" data-timestamp="238.21"> And used to the tactics to deal with them, used to quarantining and isolating and all that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:04:02.53" data-timestamp="242.53"> Right, because you didn't have anything else.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:05.23" data-timestamp="245.23"> This is before the age of antibiotics. That doesn't happen till the 1940s. Antivirals don't come till way after that. I think all of us living today, when so many diseases are treatable, I think we forget what a different world that was. Like for instance, if you got tuberculosis in New York City in the 1940s, they would send you to a TB ward to keep you from infecting anybody else.</p><p begin="00:04:28.49" data-timestamp="268.49">I had a neighbor who was born in 1927, and she told me as a kid, when she got the disease, she was sent to one of those wards to live on the Upper West Side. The building's actually still there. She said they told her when she arrived, "OK, we don't know how to cure this, but we do know how to make you comfortable. Some of you live, and some of you die. Good luck."</p><p begin="00:04:53.35" data-timestamp="293.35">Today, of course, things are so different. Howard Markel says there's never been an epidemic in America like the one we're going through right now.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:05:00.19" data-timestamp="300.19"> None. None have come close to being as politicized, as red versus blue, on even the most mundane of scientific matters than this pandemic.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:13.16" data-timestamp="313.16"> Yeah, yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howard Markel</h4><p begin="00:05:15.07" data-timestamp="315.07"> Not even close. It feels as if it's so baked in to the ideologies of so many Americans right now, it feels almost hopeless.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:29.93" data-timestamp="329.93"> Well, today on our program, we have stories of public health experts trying to protect the public, telling us all to do things that will save our lives in this moment when unprecedented numbers of us are not obeying them.</p><p begin="00:05:40.76" data-timestamp="340.76">We have two stories today. The first is about what it's like to do these jobs while getting so much pushback. The second, an expert on how to get through to Republican voters tries to convince them to take the vaccine, which is not easy.</p><p begin="00:05:56.32" data-timestamp="356.32">WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Hazardous to Your Health Official</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:10.90" data-timestamp="370.9"> Act 1, Hazardous to Your Health Official.</p><p begin="00:06:14.71" data-timestamp="374.71">So the way the system works in our country is that there are thousands of local public health officials in cities and counties across the country. In normal times, they test our drinking water. They make sure restaurants are sanitary. They enforce smoking bans, control mosquito populations in some places, run education campaigns, like nutrition programs for new mothers. The specifics vary a little from state to state, but you get the idea. They oversee health conditions.</p><p begin="00:06:36.73" data-timestamp="396.73">And when there's a health emergency, like the outbreak of an infectious disease, they get special powers, broad powers to quarantine, close down businesses, do what's necessary to keep it from spreading. And they're obligated to take action. This past year, like I've said, the public that they're protecting has made all that a lot harder to do.</p><p begin="00:06:54.79" data-timestamp="414.79">Keep in mind, these are not elected officials. They are public servants. They're government employees. Anna Maria Barry-Jester, a reporter with Kaiser Health News, has a story about two of these public health officials in Santa Cruz County in California, where she lives.</p><p begin="00:07:08.32" data-timestamp="428.32">A heads-up before we start-- some things in this story may not be right for children to hear. Here's Anna.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Anna Maria Barry-Jester</h4><p begin="00:07:15.18" data-timestamp="435.18"> I've spent the last year talking to dozens of public health officials all over the country. I'd call to ask about COVID testing, contact tracing, or some other issue I was reporting on. And they'd often make an offhand comment about getting threatening calls in the middle of the night, or emails that had detailed photos of their house with messages like "Look out, we're coming for you."</p><p begin="00:07:36.09" data-timestamp="456.09">Most of these public health people didn't want to go into detail about it on the record. It was too scary. They worried that it would only make them bigger targets. But these two officials in Santa Cruz County stood out, because they not only agreed to talk, they were unusually candid.</p><p begin="00:07:51.24" data-timestamp="471.24">The first official I'm going to tell you about is Dr. Gail Newel.

  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.69" data-timestamp="0.69">When Madison Nguyen was elected to the San Jose City Council, she was just 30 and was a really big deal. There is a huge Vietnamese community in San Jose, but no Vietnamese had ever made it to the city council.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:00:11.98" data-timestamp="11.98">The Vietnamese-American community was just beside themselves with having the very first Vietnamese-American elected official there.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:18.93" data-timestamp="18.93">My Thuan Tran is the reporter who covers California's Vietnamese communities for the <i>Los</i> <i>Angeles</i> <i>Times.</i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:00:24.53" data-timestamp="24.53">They really saw her as the beloved daughter, as a golden child. There was a lot of celebrations. And she won and her face was all over different magazines. She was interviewed by all the Vietnamese press. People were following her every move. They wanted to know what she was up to.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:40.80" data-timestamp="40.8">But within two years, mainly because of one issue. One issue. Lots of people were furious with Madison Nguyen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:00:48.53" data-timestamp="48.53">People started protesting in front of City Hall every single Tuesday. And they would hold banners that had a slash across her face. They wrote Madison Nguyen is a traitor. There was one protest that drew thousands of people, not only from San Jose but from the neighboring cities in the Bay Area, and also people who came from as far as Orange County or Houston.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:10.77" data-timestamp="70.77">Also there was a hunger striker?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:01:12.40" data-timestamp="72.4">Yes, there was. He was camped out in a tent for 29 days in front of City Hall and he was flanked by a lot of his supporters.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:23.62" data-timestamp="83.62">And the main issue that turned so many people against Madison Nguyen? That eventually led to a special recall election for the golden child?</p><p begin="00:01:31.39" data-timestamp="91.39">Well, there's a area of San Jose just a couple blocks long on Story Road, with lots of Vietnamese stores, and restaurants, and businesses. And one of the first things that Madison Nguyen did on the City Council was convince her colleagues they should do to economic development there, and designate that area with a special name.</p><p begin="00:01:47.31" data-timestamp="107.31">Several possible names were discussed. Most Vietnamese Americans who cared enough to come out to those meetings preferred Little Saigon, but Madison Nguyen chose the name Saigon Business District. That's it. That's the whole controversy. Little Saigon versus Saigon Business District.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:02:06.95" data-timestamp="126.95">I think the issue, it's a very subtle one. And I guess for a lot of non-Vietnamese, it was very difficult for them to understand, because they felt what's the difference? It's just Little Saigon versus Saigon Business District, but for many Vietnamese refugees, it just invoked everything that they had lived through in their lives. Of Vietnam and coming over here.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:30.38" data-timestamp="150.38">Specifically, the name Little Saigon had taken on huge significance. That's what some other Vietnamese exile communities like this big one that is down in Orange County had called themselves, partly as a political statement.</p><p begin="00:02:41.86" data-timestamp="161.86">These communities, you got to remember, were full of people who had fought the Communists during the Vietnam War and then fled the Communists after the war. The name Little Saigon was seen as an act of defiance against people who had taken over the city of Saigon and renamed it. Renamed it, actually, after the founder of the Vietnamese Communist Party-- Ho Chi Minh City.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:03:00.70" data-timestamp="180.7">The name that she did not choose, the name Little Saigon had just become such a symbol for them. It became a symbol for them of why they fled Vietnam, of why they fought for South Vietnam, of what hardships and pain that their family went through. And when she didn't choose that name, they felt like she wasn't listening to their stories, or to their history. Anything that wasn't Little Saigon, it's just seen as an insult.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Madison Nguyen</h4><p begin="00:03:30.88" data-timestamp="210.88">Me, on a personal level, I like the name Vietnam Town. Because we have Chinatown, we have Japantown, but obviously I guess what I liked didn't really matter.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:41.58" data-timestamp="221.58">This is Councilwoman Madison Nguyen. The San Jose politician at the center of all this controversy. She says that although a vocal portion of her Vietnamese constituents definitely preferred Little Saigon, Vietnamese-Americans only make up a third of her district. Non-Vietnamese people and businesses in the district wanted other names. And she thought it would be better to avoid a name that was so political. So she chose what she thought was a compromise.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Madison Nguyen</h4><p begin="00:04:04.59" data-timestamp="244.59">And so I thought, OK, well both names have the name Saigon in it, and this is a business district designation. Why don't we just called it Saigon Business District and hopefully that will make everyone happy.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:15.30" data-timestamp="255.3">I want to play you a recording from this six and a half hour City Council meeting about this. And it's many people standing up, saying that they don't want this name. And here's one man and-- there's a point in the tape where he says if Madison wins, what he means is if you get your way and the community is not called Little Saigon. Here let me play this for you.</p><p begin="00:04:34.58" data-timestamp="274.58">[AUDIO PLAYBACK]</p><p begin="00:04:34.90" data-timestamp="274.9">-Before 1975, I was Ranger Commander in Vietnam. I was in jail nine year. Tonight I come here to request only one vote from you for Little Saigon. I strongly support you from the beginning. But if Madison win, we stay away from her, because she's pro-Communist. Thank you.</p><p begin="00:05:08.66" data-timestamp="308.66">[END AUDIO PLAYBACK]</p><p begin="00:05:09.60" data-timestamp="309.6">What's your reaction to that kind of thing?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Madison Nguyen</h4><p begin="00:05:13.41" data-timestamp="313.41">It was torture to have all these people, who are pretty much at the age of my parents or grandparents, coming up and looking you, staring you in the face, in your eyes and said we no longer trust you. You're not one of us. We regret that we ever supported you. To hear that over, and over, and over, and over again. I mean it's painful.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:38.50" data-timestamp="338.5">Then there's the fact that she was being called a Communist, widely.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Madison Nguyen</h4><p begin="00:05:42.02" data-timestamp="342.02">The first time I heard it, I was very hurt. I felt that-- I came here as a boat child back in the late 1970s. I escaped Communism in Vietnam and I came here. We pretty much risked our lives to come to this great country. And to have this particular group of people in the Vietnamese community label me as a Communist sympathizer, I thought was really beyond absurd.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:14.77" data-timestamp="374.77">Madison is convinced that if she had been a non-Vietnamese City Councilperson suggesting the exact same name, Saigon Business District, it never would have gone this far. No protest. Nobody would have been labeled a Communist.</p><p begin="00:06:26.11" data-timestamp="386.11">People felt betrayed, she says, because she was one of them. They expected her to do what they wanted. And they couldn't believe it when she didn't. To them, she was a turn coat. What strikes deeper than that?</p><p begin="00:06:37.47" data-timestamp="397.47">Well today on our show, we have two other stories of turncoats. It's This American Life from WBEZ in Chicago distributed by Public Radio International.</p><p begin="00:06:44.64" data-timestamp="404.64">We'll get to those in a bit. In Madison Nguyen's case, things are ugly. Things happened pretty fast.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Code Red</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:06:50.37" data-timestamp="410.37">If you listen to Vietnamese radio during that time period, a lot of people would come out and say they believe she was a Communist.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:58.99" data-timestamp="418.99">Again, reporter My Thuan Tran.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:07:01.34" data-timestamp="421.34">I just felt that it was a really interesting case because it kind of showed the challenges of being a Vietnamese-American politician. That you kind of had to navigate among all these lingering emotions from this war that had ended 34 years ago.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:16.50" data-timestamp="436.5">Yeah, you wrote, &quot;The rules of politics are different for a Vietnamese-American politician. Even business owners, reporters, and pop singers carefully tiptoe around inferences and innuendo that can cast a person as being soft on Communism. A misstep can launch vocal protests and accusations. Reputations can be tarnished. People must bow to the pressure.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>My Thuan Tran</h4><p begin="00:07:36.82" data-timestamp="456.82">Right. I think it's a surprise for many people who are kind of unfamiliar with the Vietnamese-American community how much this name-calling and suspicion still resonates. I think some people feel like it looks like out of the McCarthy era when they view the Vietnamese-American community.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:53.78" data-timestamp="473.78">There are tons of examples of this kind of red-baiting. The first Vietnamese Superintendent of Schools in the Westminster School District in Orange County was removed a week after she was appointed, she says, because an activist lobbied school board members, saying she was a Communist.</p><p begin="00:08:06.72" data-timestamp="486.72">In Saint Paul, Minnesota, when a Catholic bishop visited from Vietnam, he asked that not be photograph with anything political. My older brother told my mother that's what I wanted. And she bought the best ones. Mom smiled when she handed them to me. She was sure I didn't know what was in the bag. But I could tell right away by the Adidas logo. I took the shoe box out of the bag and said thank you.</p><p begin="00:53:37.77" data-timestamp="3217.77">The box was rectangular. Like a coffin. And inside it lay two white shoes with three blue stripes on them. And on the side it said Adidas Rom. I didn't have to open the box to know that. Let's try them on, Mom said, pulling the paper out. See if they fit.</p><p begin="00:53:56.13" data-timestamp="3236.13">She was smiling the whole time. She didn't realize what was happening. They're from Germany, you know?, I told her and squeezed her hand hard. Of course I know, Mom smiled, Adidas is the best make the world. Grandpa was from Germany, too, I tried hinting. Grandpa was from Poland, Mom corrected me. She grew sad for a moment. But it passed right away, <i></i> and she put one of the shoes on my foot and started lacing it up.</p><p begin="00:54:23.47" data-timestamp="3263.47">I didn't say anything. I knew by then it was no use. Mom was clueless. She never been to the Vohlin Memorial Museum. Nobody had ever explained it to her. And for her, shoes were just shoes. And Germany was really Poland. So I let her put them on my feet and I didn't say anything. There was no point in telling her. It would just make her sadder. After I said thank you one more time and gave her a kiss on the cheek, I said I was going out to play.</p><p begin="00:54:51.71" data-timestamp="3291.71">Watch it, eh? Dad kidded from his armchair in the living room. Don't you go wearing down the soles in a single afternoon. I took another look at the pale leather shoes on my feet. And thought back about all the things the old man who strangled the soldier said we should remember. I touched the Adidas stripes again, and remembered my Grandpa in the cardboard photograph.</p><p begin="00:55:12.87" data-timestamp="3312.87">Are the shoes comfortable? Mom asked. Of course they're comfortable, my brother answered instead of me. Those shoes aren't just some cheap local brand. They're the very same shoe that Pele used to wear. I tiptoed slowly towards the door, trying to put as little weight on them as possible. I kept walking that way towards Ben Gurion Park.</p><p begin="00:55:32.99" data-timestamp="3332.99">Outside, the kids from Borouchoff Elementary were forming three soccer teams-- Holland, Argentina, and Brazil. The Holland group was one player short, so they agreed to let me join even though they usually never took anyone who didn't go to Borouchoff. When the game started, I still remembered to be careful not to kick with a tip, so I wouldn't hurt Grandpa. But as it continued, I forgot. Just like the old man at the Vohlin Memorial Museum said people do. And I even scored the tiebreaker with a volley kick.</p><p begin="00:56:02.91" data-timestamp="3362.91">After the game was over, I remembered and looked down at them. They were so comfortable all of a sudden. And springier, too. Much more than they'd seemed when they were still in the box. What a volley that was, eh? I'm reminded Grandpa on our way home. The goalie didn't even know what hit him. Grandpa didn't say anything. But from the bounce in my step, I could tell he was happy, too.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:56:33.03" data-timestamp="3393.03">Matt Malloy, reading the story &quot;Shoes&quot; from Etgar Keret's collection of short fiction that's titled, <i>The</i> <i>Bus</i> <i>Driver</i> <i>Who Wanted</i> <i>to be God,</i> <i>and</i> <i>Other</i> <i>Stories.</i></p><p begin="00:56:47.14" data-timestamp="3407.14">Our program is produced today by Sarah Keonig and me.

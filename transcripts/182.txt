  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:02.23" data-timestamp="2.23">Joe worked at this office where every now and then, the office manager would bring her nine-year-old to work. Good kid, kind of tomboyish.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joe</h4><p begin="00:00:09.27" data-timestamp="9.27">And she would just help out around the office. She would pass mail out. And over the time that I was there, she and I developed this kind of teasing relationship. She would come into my office, and she would drop my mail off and stick her tongue out at me. And I would fake chase her down the hallway or something.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:24.86" data-timestamp="24.86">That's sweet.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joe</h4><p begin="00:00:25.82" data-timestamp="25.82">Yeah, yeah. She was an incredibly sweet kid. And so there's this day when it's early in the morning. I've arrived at the office. And I go into the bathroom. And when I come out of the bathroom, I have my glasses in my shirt pocket, rather than on my head.</p><p begin="00:00:48.56" data-timestamp="48.56">And I look down this hallway and I see this small person walking towards me. And I then get down and start to crab walk towards her. So I go down on my haunches and put my hands up as if they're claws and waddle towards her.</p><p begin="00:01:07.56" data-timestamp="67.56">And as I'm waddling towards her, I say, in this kind of creepy voice, &quot;Oh, no, I can't believe you're here today.&quot; And then, at that moment, as I say &quot;today,&quot; she comes into focus. And I realize, in fact, it's not at all the young girl who I thought it was. But it's in fact, one of our interns, a business intern who is a midget.</p><p begin="00:01:40.72" data-timestamp="100.72">And so she comes into focus. And I see her. And I'm horrified. And I go bolt upright. And I stand up, and I say, &quot;Oh my god. I'm terribly sorry. I thought you were somebody else.&quot;</p><p begin="00:01:51.93" data-timestamp="111.93">And I think to myself, &quot;Who could she possibly think that somebody else is?&quot; And I wondered at the time, should I have tried to explain it to her? And it seems to me like one of those situations where it only gets worse the more you try to explain it. The only thing I could do was, in fact, apologize and then end all contact with her forever, right there.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:20.13" data-timestamp="140.13">Joe says the woman was utterly gracious. She introduces herself. She tries to put him at ease.</p><p begin="00:02:25.62" data-timestamp="145.62">But Joe says, not only did he cringe when this happened, he cringes every single time he tells this story. People cringe when they hear this story. And why? Seriously, why? What is it about certain stories that get this physical reaction out of us?</p><p begin="00:02:42.09" data-timestamp="162.09">You know what I mean? What are the physical reactions we ever actually give a story? There's laughing. There's crying. There's cringing? It's in the top three?</p><p begin="00:02:52.42" data-timestamp="172.42">Well, this week on our radio show, <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> we have looked into this matter. And I have to say, if there is scientific literature specifically on the physiology or psychology of cringing, we and many helpful doctors and researchers were unable to find it. But from examining a wide array of cringe stories, we have made some tentative conclusions about what it is that makes us cringe.</p><p begin="00:03:13.55" data-timestamp="193.55">Consider please, Howie's story. It was his sixth grade graduation dance. There had been one teacher, Mrs. S, who had been really cruel to him all year long. He was chubby, and she used to examine his papers for food stains, which then she would hold up and show the entire class. She'd mock him in front of class up to the day of the graduation dance.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howie</h4><p begin="00:03:34.99" data-timestamp="214.99">As we're in line, she's like, &quot;OK, you're all going to high school. I want you to be good.&quot; And then she kind of stops. And she says, &quot;Well, I don't know if we're all going to high school.&quot; She goes, &quot;I don't know. What do you think, kids? You think Howard should pass?&quot;</p><p begin="00:03:44.70" data-timestamp="224.7">And then all the sudden, my heart dropped into my stomach. &quot;What do you mean, ma'am?&quot; &quot;Yeah, yeah, let him pass. Let him pass.&quot; &quot;I don't know. I don't know if we should let Howard pass. It's up to you kids. It's up to you. Should Howard pass?&quot; &quot;Yes, yes.&quot; &quot;Should he pass?&quot; &quot;Yes, yes.&quot; &quot;I don't know.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:02.37" data-timestamp="242.37">That was humiliating, but that is not the part of the story that makes him cringe. The cringe part happens when, dressed in his best <i>Saturday</i> <i>Night</i> <i>Fever</i> tight pants and polyester shirt, he takes matters into his own hands. He takes action. At the end of the dance, relieved that he is, in fact, graduating, he finds her in the teachers' lounge.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howie</h4><p begin="00:04:20.71" data-timestamp="260.71">So I walk in. I'm feeling good. I'm smiling. I'm strutting around in my John Travolta clothes. I grab my jacket. And picture this really sweaty, fat little kid, clothes plastered to his body.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:32.88" data-timestamp="272.88">Mrs. S, who'd been so cruel, is standing with another Mrs. S, the French teacher.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Howie</h4><p begin="00:04:37.97" data-timestamp="277.97">I basically bowed to the French teacher. And I said, &quot;Enchantee, Madame S.&quot; And then I looked at the other Mrs. S., and I went, &quot;Thank you so much, Mrs. S.&quot; And I took a deep bow. I actually waved my arm underneath my waist, bowing deep to my knees, you know, getting up, flipping my jacket with one finger over my shoulder and going a Jackson 5 spin around, and strutted out, thinking I was the coolest.</p><p begin="00:05:05.06" data-timestamp="305.06">I remember that when I bowed, and I said &quot;Enchantee,&quot; and all this, I remember their faces. And they were disgusted. There wasn't a smile. There wasn't some kind of, &quot;Oh, thank you, Howard.&quot; It was basically silence, this stern, down-at-the-mouth like, uugh.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:26.71" data-timestamp="326.71">The things that she did to him were just humiliating, he says. It was the fact that he decided to thank her for it. That's what makes him cringe.</p><p begin="00:05:34.20" data-timestamp="334.2">And that seems to be true of a lot of cringe stories. The people in them go out of their way to embarrass themselves. In Joe's case, to get back to the guy crab walking in the office, if he had just come out of the men's room with his fly open, for example, that would've been embarrassing.</p><p begin="00:05:48.54" data-timestamp="348.54">But he went further. He made a special effort. A part of him was proud that he was the one adult in the office cool enough to goof around with a little kid. And pride, my friend, goeth before a cringe.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Joe</h4><p begin="00:06:01.46" data-timestamp="361.46">You know, you're in your office. And then suddenly, you're just like, &quot;You know, I'm in a pretty good mood today. And I'm ready to goof around a little. I'm going to crouch down by these mailboxes, and I'm going to walk like a crab towards her down the hallway. This is a stuffy place full of stuffy people, and I'm still a pretty fun guy.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:19.49" data-timestamp="379.49">You cringe in that moment of revelation, when you suddenly see that maybe you are not the fun guy, when you see yourself as others see you. And it is not pretty. One of the doctors that we talked to about cringes for this week's show pointed out that a cringe is basically the human body cowering in fear for an instant. And he said that one of the most fearsome, stressful things that we can encounter as people is the thought that we are not who we think we are, the thought that the world sees us differently than we see ourselves, and not in a good way.</p><p begin="00:06:53.17" data-timestamp="413.17">Well, our program today, as always from WBEZ Chicago, distributed by Public Radio International. Today on our show, an investigation into stories that make us cringe. Act One, What We Cringe About When We Cringe About Love. Nancy Updike explains the peculiar characteristics of a cringe love affair. Act Two, <i>M*A*S*H</i> Notes, in which I tell a story from my past that I actually spent two decades trying to forget about a week that I had back in 1979 on the set of a television show. Also in that story, why I hope to never run into Alan Alda on the street.</p><p begin="00:07:28.99" data-timestamp="448.99">Act Three, Ariel Sharon, Shimon Peres, David Ben-Gurion and Me, the story of one teenager's cringeworthy dream, to become prime minister of a nation in which he does not even reside. Act Four, Cringe and Purge. We have a story from Bruce Jay Friedman about a man who tries to rid himself of a decades-old cringe, if that is really possible. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: What We Cringe About When We Cringe About Love</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:55.78" data-timestamp="475.78">Act One, What We Cringe About When We Cringe About Love.

It really wasn't clear what was going to happen to it, if it was going to be left to spoil, if it would get delivered.</p><p begin="00:02:19.30" data-timestamp="139.3">Then the State Department said that life-saving food and medical aid could get permission to continue, but lots of it stayed stuck. You guys, we're fine. Don't worry.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:50.78" data-timestamp="290.78"> Then she leans over to my producer, Ike, who's on the floor.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Navyn Salem</h4><p begin="00:04:53.91" data-timestamp="293.91"> Ike, sit on the chair. Just pretend. Just, I didn't want to insult them.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:59.57" data-timestamp="299.57"> Navyn describes her workday since January during this period of foreign aid limbo as an ongoing series of brand new, possibly business-ending, problems to solve, one after another. This happened twice.</p><p begin="00:05:20.60" data-timestamp="320.6">In February, when almost all the 10,000 employees at USAID were let go, the contracting officer that Navyn dealt with was one of them. Because you said they're rejected with no reason? OK. It's OK.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:20.74" data-timestamp="380.74"> And that's $7 million for stuff you've already made.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Navyn Salem</h4><p begin="00:06:23.29" data-timestamp="383.29"> I have a binding contract with USAID. I can't resume if we can't get along here.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:02.56" data-timestamp="422.56"> The production line's conveyor belts are right outside her office, and she says she would sometimes look at them and think, every hour they were shut, that's 415 kids she couldn't feed.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Navyn Salem</h4><p begin="00:07:12.08" data-timestamp="432.08"> That's the calculation that's going through my head. That's the urgency that I feel. And I'm trying to light things on fire to let people know.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:22.24" data-timestamp="442.24"> How she's handled this is a good illustration of what you have to do when normal systems to solve problems no longer exist. She's done a ton of press and says it's actually helped. They know which lab reagents need funding quickly to keep testing people.</p><p begin="00:49:02.13" data-timestamp="2942.13">She says 20 years of American aid has made Eswatini's health system stronger in ways that will persist. It trained nurses and other medical staff, developed more effective ways to distribute antiretroviral drugs, just generally raised standards for care. She sees the health system as fairly sturdy at this point, able to care for its own people.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Velephi Okello</h4><p begin="00:49:22.38" data-timestamp="2962.38"> I always say to people, our health system is like a-- let me say a Toyota. [CHUCKLES] We are using a model that is basic, basic health service delivery model. It's moving and maybe not so comfortable, but it gets you there.</p><p begin="00:49:42.54" data-timestamp="2982.54">So when PEPFAR came, we always say that they took us from that basic Toyota into some kind of Mercedes Benz or Rolls-Royce, which now made sure that our quality of services is great. And we are moving faster in achieving the targets that we had aimed to achieve.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Diane Wu</h4><p begin="00:50:02.98" data-timestamp="3002.98"> The Mercedes is gone now, but she thinks they'll still get to where they need to go.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chico Harlan</h4><p begin="00:50:08.05" data-timestamp="3008.05"> Dr. Okello, of course, works for the government. A UNAIDS assessment that came out recently-- not so rosy. It said there is a risk of running out of antiretroviral drugs and other supplies in three to six months in Eswatini.</p><p begin="00:50:21.70" data-timestamp="3021.7">And while Eswatini does pay for its own antiretrovirals, it takes a lot more than drugs to combat HIV. It's a chronic illness where so much of the work involves constant monitoring for new cases and maintaining a network of clinics and community health workers. So people have the drugs they need and are taking them every day. In Eswatini, lots of that work was still paid for by foreign money, mostly American.</p><p begin="00:50:45.94" data-timestamp="3045.94">Some PEPFAR funding may survive. They're debating that right now on Capitol Hill. But even if nothing is restored, Eswatini is in a better position than most PEPFAR countries in Africa to take over that work. It has a bunch of advantages. It's small. It has a fair amount of money. And it's done a good job to mobilize and control HIV as a national priority for decades.</p><p begin="00:51:06.98" data-timestamp="3066.98">One of the people who originally created PEPFAR told us, out of the 50 PEPFAR countries worldwide, maybe a dozen are ready to move off the program's money fairly quickly. And Eswatini is one of them. He also told us, PEPFAR was never intended to last this long. The conversations about how to wind down had already started in the Bush years, but nobody carried them through. Once a big bureaucracy took shape around this money in the US and abroad, there wasn't much momentum to uproot everything. That is, of course, until now.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:51:48.65" data-timestamp="3108.65"> Chico Harlan is a reporter at <i>The</i> <i>Washington</i> <i>Post.</i> He also did a great print version of this story. Diane Wu is one of the producers of our show. By the way, since they visited, the Luke Commission has opened up its gates again, at a much smaller scale. To pay for that, they're charging for their services for the first time.</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Act Three: Two Daughters</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:52:09.72" data-timestamp="3129.72"> So how bad is this going to be, the US pulling back from all this humanitarian aid? Well, in March, a USAID employee made a memo public on his way out the door, where the agency estimated that there would be 200,000 more children paralyzed with polio each year, 18 million extra cases of malaria each year. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has said that nobody's died as a result of the freeze on USAID. That seems not to be true. Reporters have found examples-- real people.</p><p begin="00:52:39.84" data-timestamp="3159.84">But how many people are likely to die? There are different estimates. The most thorough one we found was done by the Center for Global Development. My coworker, David Kestenbaum, talked with Charles Kenny, who worked on some of the modeling. Kenny said that some of the first deaths likely to show up would be from the thing we've been talking about, cuts to HIV programs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:52:59.72" data-timestamp="3179.72"> We have a pretty good knowledge of how many people and how quickly die-- how quickly they die, if you take them off antiretroviral medications.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:10.58" data-timestamp="3190.58"> So they went through the actual contracts that got cut in the government database and found that about 18% of the money for these programs had been eliminated. And they ran the numbers.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:53:19.87" data-timestamp="3199.87"> The modeled estimate is that 200,000 or so people will die due to the cuts to awards this year.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:28.78" data-timestamp="3208.78"> He says it is possible that individual countries' governments will step in, at least to some extent. But because of the abruptness of the cuts, the way the United States just froze funding when President Trump came in, without giving countries time to prepare and take over those health services, he says, there's no way that's going to happen fast enough.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:53:46.69" data-timestamp="3226.69"> While I think it is probably fair to say that 200,000 estimate isn't going to be true in the long-term, in the short-term, I think it's probably horribly close to true.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:58.41" data-timestamp="3238.41"> And that's just from HIV programs. There were cuts to malaria prevention, tuberculosis, childhood vaccines. Adding it all up, Kenny says it could be over a half million deaths each year.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:54:09.08" data-timestamp="3249.08"> It's really depressing because you can see the trends in HIV deaths and malaria deaths, and they were going down. They were dramatically down. And that is an amazing effort. And the US is leading it. And that does make all of this more tragic.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:54:29.21" data-timestamp="3269.21"> How does it feel to run the numbers?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:54:33.98" data-timestamp="3273.98"> It's easier to think of them as just numbers, eh? [CRYING]</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:54:41.18" data-timestamp="3281.18"> Are you crying now? Sorry.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:54:44.48" data-timestamp="3284.48"> Yes, I am.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>David Kestenbaum</h4><p begin="00:54:46.75" data-timestamp="3286.75"> [BREATHES DEEPLY] I'm sorry.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Kenny</h4><p begin="00:54:49.80" data-timestamp="3289.8"> No, no. It is a completely legitimate question.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:58.11" data-timestamp="3298.11"> Whether or not you think the United States should stay in the business of doing things like providing HIV drugs around the world, the way it suddenly stopped in January--without warning, without giving countries time to take over those services-- has had such a profound impact that it's hard not to wonder: Did anybody consider the cost in human lives of doing it this way? If they did, why is this the path they chose? Or did they not care enough to even ask the question? We asked for an interview with anybody at the State Department or the White House who could explain why it was done this way, and they turned us down.</p><p begin="00:55:16.17" data-timestamp="3316.17">When a few producers on our staff called around, in country after country, we kept finding people who told us about major disruptions in service. One researcher, Susan Hillis, has been asking people to record voice memos about what they're experiencing. She got this one from a man named Jay, somebody who took antiretroviral drugs every day. He's a student, a tour guide. He recorded this at home one night.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jay</h4><p begin="00:55:39.93" data-timestamp="3339.93"> Hi, I'm Jay. I'm turning 24 this June, and I live in Mombasa. I have been taking the medication my whole life. I felt like it was really hard for me when the funding was stopped, and I went to the hospital and I couldn't find medicine. So I would go, be told come next week, come next week, and I wouldn't find medicine.</p><p begin="00:56:18.72" data-timestamp="3378.72">And I started getting this boils on my hand and legs and all over the body.

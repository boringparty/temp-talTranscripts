  <h3>Prologue: Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.62" data-timestamp="0.62"> From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. There's this kind of therapy for trauma, victims of sexual assault, soldiers with PTSD, where instead of taking years and talking, and talking, and talking on some couch to a therapist with no end in sight, you basically knock it out, all the treatment in just 10 or 12 sessions.</p><p begin="00:00:20.75" data-timestamp="20.75">Two weeks-- you can do it in two weeks. And it's effective. Studies have shown that. This therapy's been around since the '80s, but I think lots of people who might find it useful don't even know it's an option for them. Like, for instance, Jaime Lowe, who's a writer and reporter and a good candidate for this treatment.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:00:39.37" data-timestamp="39.37"> I was sexually assaulted when I was 13, almost 30 years ago. And for most of those 30 years, I didn't really talk about it. Not because I didn't want to or need to, but because I didn't know how. And no one asked.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:54.77" data-timestamp="54.77"> Jaime Lowe decided to do a story where she would go through this therapy herself and record all of the sessions, which never happens. Like most therapy, usually this treatment is in private, not on the radio.</p><p begin="00:01:08.48" data-timestamp="68.48">And hearing how it works, it really is kind of remarkable. My mom was a therapist. I've been in therapy. This is so different from what I have ever heard. And that's going to be our show today. We're going to hear it step by step, this sometimes life-changing process, and how that can happen so quickly.</p><p begin="00:01:27.56" data-timestamp="87.56">A quick warning to listeners before we start about content. This story does mention the sexual assault of a teenager. Here's Jaime Lowe.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Week One</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:01:37.04" data-timestamp="97.04">I thought because I didn't talk about the assault or even think about it much, everything was as resolved as it could be. But then after Donald Trump, and "grab them by the pussy," and Harvey Weinstein, and all of them, it's not that specific memories of the assault would pop up, I just felt immobilized, anxious, protective of my body. And I realized my sexual assault wasn't resolved at all.</p><p begin="00:02:04.01" data-timestamp="124.01">Around this time, I heard about a therapy for sexual assault survivors, called Cognitive Processing Therapy, or CPT. A researcher described it to me as short-term inexpensive, practical, like learning a skill. The typical way to deal with trauma in therapy is to talk about the incident over and over, until it's less radioactive.</p><p begin="00:02:27.99" data-timestamp="147.99">CPT does the same thing, but in a systematized way. It's all laid out in advance, and the same for everyone-- what you'll do in the first session, and the second, and the third, and so on. Each session is based on learning a skill and practicing that skill on a worksheet. You go over each element of the trauma, piece by piece, and try to see it differently. The goal is to change the story you've been telling yourself about what happened.</p><p begin="00:02:57.97" data-timestamp="177.97">I got in touch with Dr. Debra Kaysen, a psychologist with a specialty in trauma therapy at the University of Washington School of Medicine. She and I started talking and e-mailing about CPT. It all seemed so different than the therapy I was used to-- my weekly talk sessions-- and I realized I wanted to try it.</p><p begin="00:03:18.52" data-timestamp="198.52">I've done a lot of therapy. After the assault, I had two manic episodes and was diagnosed bipolar. I've always been very open about mental illness. I wrote a book about being bipolar a couple of years ago. But even though that book is partly about my adolescence, I barely mention the assault. I've never dealt with it, I never wanted to-- in the book, in treatment.</p><p begin="00:03:43.70" data-timestamp="223.7">But now I was feeling the trauma more. I thought CPT might help me. I also thought, what if this could help lots of people? I asked Dr. Kaysen if I could record the sessions and play them on the radio. She checked in with colleagues-- a psychologist with an expertise in journalism, a journalism ethics professor-- to make sure we'd be able to have a therapeutic relationship while I was simultaneously doing a story about the therapy.</p><p begin="00:04:12.52" data-timestamp="252.52">After a lot of back and forth and establishing some basic ground rules, we set up our CPT boot camp-- 10 hour-long sessions over two weeks in Seattle. Usually CPT is one session a week for 12 weeks, but we decided to condense it. And early studies show this approach is effective. Last October, I flew out to Seattle.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:04:35.11" data-timestamp="275.11"> Session one, October 22nd.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:04:38.68" data-timestamp="278.68"> Excellent.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:04:39.32" data-timestamp="279.32"> Yeah. OK.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:04:40.81" data-timestamp="280.81"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:04:40.96" data-timestamp="280.96"> I'm sitting with Dr. Kaysen in a generic room at the university clinic. I fail at setting up the recording equipment a few times, before getting it semi-right. I'm not a radio person.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:04:52.37" data-timestamp="292.37"> So let me tell you a little bit about what we're going to do today. OK?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:04:54.99" data-timestamp="294.99"> OK.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:04:55.34" data-timestamp="295.34"> So usually, today is a little more heavy on the psycho ed. It's going to be a little different than a lot of our other sessions. I'm going to be talking a lot.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:05:03.88" data-timestamp="303.88"> OK.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:05:04.29" data-timestamp="304.29"> OK? Which takes a little bit of the pressure off of you, maybe.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:05:07.31" data-timestamp="307.31"> OK.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:05:08.10" data-timestamp="308.1"> Dr. Kaysen has a kindergarten teacher presence that makes me feel ready and OK. She starts the first session with me the way she would with anyone, by talking about sexual assault and PTSD. 50% of women who are sexually assaulted develop PTSD. That could mean symptoms like depression, anxiety, flashbacks-- some of the things I've actually been feeling when I hear the news.</p><p begin="00:05:35.34" data-timestamp="335.34">Dr. Kaysen hands me a photocopied worksheet. Everything we're going to do, this entire therapy, is structured around these worksheets. I'll do them at my Airbnb. And when Dr. Kaysen and I meet, we'll go over them. Each set of worksheets will cover a new skill, and I'll master that before moving on to the next skill, which Dr. Kaysen will introduce at the end of each session.</p><p begin="00:05:58.25" data-timestamp="358.25">The idea is, by the time we're done, I'll be able to do this on my own with any issue in my life. Dr. Kaysen explains my first assignment. Most of the worksheets have all sorts of boxes to fill in with answers. They look like badly designed forms you'd get at the DMV, but this first one is just a sheet of paper.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:06:17.80" data-timestamp="377.8"> What I want you to do is write at least one page on why you think that the sexual assault occurred. I do not want you to use this as an opportunity to write specific details about the event, OK? This is more writing about what you think caused the event.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:06:37.33" data-timestamp="397.33"> The phrase "caused the event" makes me uneasy, because as much as I intellectually believe that nothing specific really caused the event, there are things I still question. It's embarrassing that, after 30 years, I still might think it's my fault, but I do.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:06:55.52" data-timestamp="415.52"> I can tell you that the cause is going to be very hard for me.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:06:58.57" data-timestamp="418.57"> Yeah. Yeah, we talked earlier about what are the stories we tell ourselves.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:07:02.19" data-timestamp="422.19"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:07:03.38" data-timestamp="423.38"> It was this.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:07:04.60" data-timestamp="424.6"> Mm-hmm.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:07:05.49" data-timestamp="425.49"> Right? The event happened because I took a shortcut. Right? The event happened because I had a drink. The event happened because I was wearing a short skirt. And sometimes people know that that's not why it happened, but sometimes those thoughts still haunt them. OK. There aren't any right or wrong answers. I want you to just vomit the ideas on the page.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Jaime Lowe</h4><p begin="00:07:28.93" data-timestamp="448.93"> That's my specialty.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Debra Kaysen</h4><p begin="00:07:30.02" data-timestamp="450.02"> Awesome.

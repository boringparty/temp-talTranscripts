  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.26" data-timestamp="0.26">From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Brigid</h4><p begin="00:00:04.69" data-timestamp="4.69">Start from the top? I needed a driver. I couldn't drive. The problem was I wanted to have my same life, my same nice little suburban, get in the car and drive everywhere life.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:24.31" data-timestamp="24.31">Brigid was going blind in mid-life with a kid. She had stuff to do. So she took out an ad in the paper and hired somebody to drive her around, a guy named Harry. Nice guy. The first day, he picks her up in a van, takes her to her daughter's school at the end of the school day. They wait In line with the other parents.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Brigid</h4><p begin="00:00:40.47" data-timestamp="40.47">And Harry jumped out and started with his whisk broom. Opened up the van, opened up on both sides and whisk brooming and tidying it up.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:55.66" data-timestamp="55.66">On errands, Harry would park in the handicapped space. Or if that wasn't available, he'd park on the grass to save Brigid a few steps. She asked him to take her to the grocery store.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Brigid</h4><p begin="00:01:05.34" data-timestamp="65.34">Anyway, so then we get out and always he'd be wanting to help me up by the elbows. He wanted to help. And he says to me as we went into the grocery, here, let me get the basket for you. Let me carry your-- and I said, no, no no. I'm fine. And oh, he says, please. Oh, let me. I'm here to be of service for you. And he kind of shadowed me throughout the grocery store. And it was just very, again, a lot of that roar-roar fight back. And then this awful feeling of that he's just trying to help me. What I he really wanted was to help somebody out.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:51.95" data-timestamp="111.95">When you do something for somebody else, it always seems so simple. You just want to help. But in fact, you're forming a relationship with that person. And it's just as complicated as any relationship you can have. And like any other relationship, if you're not attentive to what the person actually wants, it's easy to blunder, which is why doing good for others is so difficult. In certain religions, the question of how to do good is a kind of obsession. Among my people, your Jews, it was traditionally a very big deal. In fact, one way to understand all Jewish law is as a set of instructions for doing good in the world. A set of instructions, I should say, so complicated, spanning so many centuries and thinkers, that in the 12th century, a philosopher named Maimonides actually made a crib sheet summarizing it all by listing the eight different ways that a person can do good for others.</p><p begin="00:02:37.65" data-timestamp="157.65">He listed in order of preference, from least desirable to the most. The lowest level of doing for others is giving grudgingly. Then there's giving less than you should but giving it cheerfully. Then giving after being asked. Then giving before being asked. And it continues on up through the most meritorious way to give, which is, tellingly, enabling the person you're helping to become self-sufficient so they don't need the help anymore.</p><p begin="00:03:05.19" data-timestamp="185.19">Today on our program, stories of people trying to do good and why they often fail and why they occasionally succeed. Our program today in just two acts.</p><p begin="00:03:15.32" data-timestamp="195.32">Act One, You Can't Go Home Again, the story of two people trying to restore a small town to its former splendor and how the biggest obstacle turned out to be the people whose lives they wanted to improve.</p><p begin="00:03:26.87" data-timestamp="206.87">Act Two, Humanitarians. I chat with writer Philip Gourevitch about his encounters with international do-gooders, about Rwanda and Kosovo, about genocidal murders, and about Rick in <i>Casablanca.</i> Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: You Can't Go Home Again</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:48.00" data-timestamp="228">Act One. You Can't Go Home Again. You drive through cotton fields to get here, down old Highway 61 through Southeast Missouri. The nearest big town is Sikeston, population 18,000, 20 minutes away. When you drive into Canalou, Missouri, there's a sign that says, &quot;population 319.&quot; And then just beyond it, another sign saying, &quot;pavement ends.&quot; Then it's mostly gravel roads and trailer homes. The businesses on Main Street aren't just closed, they're gone, torn down decades ago. The movie theater, the restaurants, the grocery stores. People used to come here from all the surrounding towns. And when Kenny Wharton and his wife Jackie talk about what it was like in Canalou when they were kids, it's like they're describing a dream, a town in an old, black and white Hollywood film.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kenny Wharton</h4><p begin="00:04:30.96" data-timestamp="270.96">This was the place on a Saturday. You had a hard time finding a place to park if you had a car. And we had everything in this little town. And people would come to town to visit. Old men would be playing Checkers up and down the streets. And the women would be visiting and kids would be playing. We played until 2:00 in the morning, we'd play.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jackie Wharton</h4><p begin="00:04:56.32" data-timestamp="296.32">And us younger kids would be asleep in the back seat of the car because we would give out.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:06.91" data-timestamp="306.91">When I pulled into Canalou to talk to the Whartons, a four year old who lives next door to them, in a muddy shirt, mud on his face, no shoes in the cold, was playing in the drainage ditch. These ditches line both sides of every street in town because there's no sewage system here. Most people live in trailers not regular houses, and some people empty their septic tanks straight into the ditches where kids play.</p><p begin="00:05:27.51" data-timestamp="327.51">The day I arrived, it had rained. The ground was soft, muddy everywhere. An adult who had let this four year old touch her face a few weeks back had gotten a rash on her face that even the doctors up in St. Louis couldn't identify or cure. This is the town that Kenny and Jackie couldn't wait to get back to.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kenny Wharton</h4><p begin="00:05:43.89" data-timestamp="343.89">Anyway, this town was a good little town to grow up in. But we got married and we went away, went to the big  city of St. Louis. And we've been gone, I guess we were gone about 40 years.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jackie Wharton</h4><p begin="00:05:56.62" data-timestamp="356.62">And this has been a dream, to come back and build a home here.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:03.12" data-timestamp="363.12">In St. Louis, Kenny had been a supervisor at McDonnell Douglas. The Whartons lived in a house in the suburbs, raised two kids, active with neighborhood associations and the Board of Ed. And they thought, once they moved back to Canalou, they would try to bring back some of the spirit that they remembered growing up here in the '50s and '60s. Maybe start a little league, build a public park with swings and trees, put up a gym where kids could play ball, the kinds of straightforward, innocent improvements it's hard to imagine that anybody would oppose anywhere.</p><p begin="00:06:31.37" data-timestamp="391.37">This is the story of why they failed, of why people did turn their backs on the Whartons, why three years of using every skill they have, devoting energy, devoting hope, only proved to them that Canalou did not want to be improved, and that something had changed in this small town that would take a lot more than two do-gooders to reverse.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Drake</h4><p begin="00:06:51.29" data-timestamp="411.29">I don't know if we can climb over the rubble, or we should walk around.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Drake</h4><p begin="00:06:54.04" data-timestamp="414.04">I can. I can. I know how.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Drake</h4><p begin="00:06:57.32" data-timestamp="417.32">No, baby. You fall, see all this glass and this rock? Fall and hurt yourself.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Drake</h4><p begin="00:07:02.90" data-timestamp="422.9">I didn't do that 'afore. I didn't do that. It's OK.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:09.31" data-timestamp="429.31">Directly across the street from the Whartons is a trailer where Susan Drake lives with her five-year-old daughter Rachel, her eight-month-old son David, and her boyfriend Brad. Next to their trailer, one house down, is a lot that used to be an auto repair shop. The shop burned down 10 years ago, and nobody has cleaned up the site. It's a mess of broken glass, twisted, rusted out tin from the roof, concrete blocks from the walls, a stack of old tires. In short, an irresistible place for any small child, including Rachel and her friends. Susan and Brad and Rachel take me on a tour.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Brad</h4><p begin="00:07:38.87" data-timestamp="458.87">You've got an open refrigerator standing here. Well, the door's standing wide open. There. Right there.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Drake</h4><p begin="00:07:46.48" data-timestamp="466.48">You can't get in here.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Susan Drake</h4><p begin="00:07:48.27" data-timestamp="468.27">Well, the shelves are in there. But if you really wanted in there, you could get in there. But that's dangerous baby. When you get inside there and that door shuts, you can't open it from the inside. You're stuck in there and you could die, suffocate.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:59.28" data-timestamp="479.28">Rachael considers a diplomatic response.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rachel Drake</h4><p begin="00:08:01.24" data-timestamp="481.24">Well, we went in the cars, though. The other thing that one just has to look at is how completely calculating those who conduct wars these days are.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:59.77" data-timestamp="3299.77">Well, this is the thing I was just going to say, is one of the things that comes through in the book is that the Hutu militia in the camps were very canny about exploiting Western attitudes towards them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Philip Gourevitch</h4><p begin="00:55:13.28" data-timestamp="3313.28">Absolutely.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:02.02" data-timestamp="2.02">Every night, when I was a kid, my family would sit down to dinner together. And every night, as best as I can remember, when the meal was done, my mom would take the ice from her glass, tip it back into her mouth, and chew on it loudly. And every night, all of us would ask her, &quot;Please don't crunch the ice. Please never, ever, ever do that again.&quot; And every night, she would ignore this request.</p><p begin="00:00:28.99" data-timestamp="28.99">And in retrospect, I have to say, I got to hand it to my mom. She is not somebody to be pushed around. This is just who she is. She is willful. She gets her way. It's something to be admired.</p><p begin="00:00:44.26" data-timestamp="44.26">And of course, this story happened at the dinner table. The dinner table is the stage for so many family dramas. Today on our program, we bring you three stories about families at mealtime. From WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> Life. I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:00:59.57" data-timestamp="59.57">Today's program, You Gonna Eat That? Three families, three stories, in three acts. Act one, Breakfast, a story of eating and not eating, and what you learn when you view the world through the prism of a single meal. Act two, Lunch, we hear a father's lament about sippy cups, chewing with your mouth closed, and why it is not good to apply tape to the cat. Act three, Dinner. We have a story from Dave Eggers about what family dinner's like when the parents are suddenly gone, and the kids are left to run things themselves. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Breakfast</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:38.66" data-timestamp="98.66">Act One, Breakfast. It was produced by Jay Allison.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman 1</h4><p begin="00:02:03.91" data-timestamp="123.91">This morning for breakfast, I had a toasted everything bagel with eggs and cheese and a cup of Earl Gray tea with--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:02:12.76" data-timestamp="132.76">A reporter where I used to work taught me to ask people what they had for breakfast at the beginning of an interview. He said it was a good way to set your recording levels.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:02:21.97" data-timestamp="141.97">In 1996, Seattle police officers faced a tense situation when they were called out--</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:02:27.32" data-timestamp="147.32">That's me on the radio in Seattle. I was a reporter for a public station. They didn't pay me or anything. I was just a volunteer. But I was still a reporter. I had a microphone and a tape recorder, and I went to press conferences.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:02:41.43" data-timestamp="161.43">--department, they flooded it with tear gas.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:02:43.23" data-timestamp="163.23">I wasn't very good at it. I couldn't stand being told what to say, and I was too cowardly to figure out the truth for myself.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:02:50.15" data-timestamp="170.15">--came out firing his gun, and officers retaliated, shooting him nine times.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:02:55.70" data-timestamp="175.7">A lot of times, the most revealing response I got from people was what they had for breakfast. I'm Annie Cheney for KUOW, 94.9, public radio.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:03:09.31" data-timestamp="189.31">So I decided to follow my instincts and tell a story about breakfast. I met her when I was 15. She was 36. And sometimes, I think she saved my life.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vivian</h4><p begin="00:03:56.19" data-timestamp="236.19">A cup of black coffee, and guilt.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:04:04.24" data-timestamp="244.24">When she agreed to meet me, I didn't know what to expect. And she almost died.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vivian</h4><p begin="00:04:21.34" data-timestamp="261.34">When I was going down towards 60, I would crawl out of bed, out of the futon. And I thought about the food I'd get. And I was alone in the house.</p><p begin="00:04:38.14" data-timestamp="278.14">And I would crawl to the kitchen. I'm getting dizzy even thinking about now. So I said to him, when you make it, make it so that the back of it slants in, so that my cheekbones-- my face will be as sunken as it can be.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:06:19.22" data-timestamp="379.22">When was this?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vivian</h4><p begin="00:06:21.03" data-timestamp="381.03">Well, I've had it maybe for a year and a half. I don't know. Or less.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:06:25.03" data-timestamp="385.03">Would you still say that today?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vivian</h4><p begin="00:06:28.45" data-timestamp="388.45">Probably.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Annie Cheney</h4><p begin="00:06:30.42" data-timestamp="390.42">Vivian's wrist bones could easily fit in a child's fist. Her recording levels are too hot.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.42" data-timestamp="0.42"> You probably remember when those Danish cartoons of the prophet Mohammed caused so much anger in the Muslim world. Violent protests over Europe and the Middle East, flags were burned, Danish products pulled off shelves. It's blasphemy to depict the prophet at all. Many American newspapers would not reprint the Danish cartoons. And is this true for you? I am not actually sure I've ever seen any kind of picture of the prophet Mohammed, ever.</p><p begin="00:00:29.08" data-timestamp="29.08">Well with that in mind, it's kind of interesting to hear that there is a place in this country where you can see an image of Mohammed right now. There's actually a sculpture of Mohammed on display in Washington, DC. And not in some crackpot salon. This is actually in a US government building. And it's not just any US government building. It's the Supreme Court building. And it's not just any room in the Supreme Court building. It's actually in the room where the justices hear cases. You're a US Supreme Court Justice, you look up and to the right from the bench, and there's Mohammed.</p><p begin="00:01:09.71" data-timestamp="69.71">And hearing this, perhaps right now you're asking yourself the exact same question that Ibrahim Hooper had when he first heard about this.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:01:16.47" data-timestamp="76.47">Why would they have an image of the prophet Mohammed in the Supreme Court?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:20.91" data-timestamp="80.91">Unlike you, however, it is Ibrahim Hooper's job to get to the bottom of this kind of thing. Hooper, I should tell you, seems like an unusually cheerful man to have the job that he has. Every single day he goes off to work in an office with the not so simple mission of explaining the Muslim perspective to non-Muslims.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:01:38.08" data-timestamp="98.08">I'm spokesman or communications director for the Council on American-Islamic Relations.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:44.72" data-timestamp="104.72">And it was in this job that back in 1996, he first heard about this sculpture. And he looked into it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:01:50.56" data-timestamp="110.56">What you see when you go in to the Supreme Court is there's a series of sculptures around the top of the main room. And one of them is of the prophet Mohammed. And he is shown with a sword in one hand and a Koran in the other.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:07.59" data-timestamp="127.59">He's actually one of 18 figures, all of them lawgivers throughout the ages including Moses and Solomon, Hammurabi the King of Babylon, King John of England whose seal is actually on the Magna Carta, Augustus Caesar who was the first emperor of Rome. You have Charlemagne, Confucius, Napoleon, basically all of them lined up in a row like it's a small, small world at Disneyland but for lawyers, OK?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:02:29.35" data-timestamp="149.35">And they tell us it was designed to pay tribute to these people, including the prophet Mohammed. And we don't disagree with that.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:39.11" data-timestamp="159.11">Now it's my understanding this has been there since the '30s.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:02:42.07" data-timestamp="162.07">Yeah, and we almost hated to bring it up to them of this. If they really think it's there to honor them, it's like well what else are these guys going to complain about?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:59.86" data-timestamp="179.86">Ibrahim says that the problem from a Muslim perspective was not just that they depicted the prophet Mohammed in this sculpture.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:03:06.05" data-timestamp="186.05">But in this case in particular, you've got a somewhat stereotypical representation in that it has the sword and the Koran, which is often-- people make the stereotype of Islam was spread with camel riding Muslims going across the desert with a sword in one hand and the Koran in the other. So that in itself, along with the visual representation of the prophet, is a problem for Muslims.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:34.14" data-timestamp="214.14">So, on behalf of his organization and American Muslims, Ibrahim wrote a letter to the Supreme Court. And he met with the Supreme Court staff in what he says was a very amicable meeting. And before long, he got an answer about the sculpture and a letter from then Chief Justice Rehnquist.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:03:49.32" data-timestamp="229.32">They came back saying basically that because it was designed to honor the prophet, that it would not be changed. But that the literature describing-- it's called the North Wall Frieze-- would be changed to reflect some of the criticism.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:08.48" data-timestamp="248.48">&quot;Altering the stone carving,&quot; the Chief Justice wrote, would quote &quot;impair the artistic integrity of the whole.&quot; And as to the fact that Mohammed was shown with a sword, Rehnquist said, quote, &quot;I would point out that swords are used throughout the Court's architecture as a symbol of justice. Nearly a dozen swords appear in the courtroom friezes alone.&quot; And so, with that, the statue stays.</p><p begin="00:04:30.73" data-timestamp="270.73">But the official Supreme Court information sheet about it says, quote, &quot;The figure above is a well-intentioned attempt by the sculptor to honor Mohammed and it bears no resemblance to Mohammed.&quot; And then it says, &quot;Muslims generally have a strong aversion to sculptured or pictured representations of their prophet.&quot; That is, we know you are offended, and we acknowledge that. Now sit down.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:05:00.72" data-timestamp="300.72">A bit disappointing, but at least we did what we needed to do.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:04.88" data-timestamp="304.88">I have to say, the moral of the story is sort of dispiriting. It's one of these moments where people are trying to be inclusive. And even then, they screw it up.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:05:14.84" data-timestamp="314.84">Yeah, and it's kind of a situation where people aren't quite understanding each other's perspective. We would hope that people would understand why we would raise objections about this portrayal. But I have the feeling that people on the other side are scratching their heads and going, you can't please them. We thought we were doing a good thing by putting in an image of the prophet Mohammed and still they don't like it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:43.79" data-timestamp="343.79">And how typical is this kind of thing?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ibrahim Hooper</h4><p begin="00:05:47.34" data-timestamp="347.34">Well it seems to be getting worse as we go along. We had the recent situation where we had the imams praying in an airport in Minnesota. And that seemed to freak people out. And then they were taken off the plane. And from our perspective, who could object to somebody praying? But from other people's perspectives, they say, well they prayed before they crashed the planes into the World Trade Centers.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:27.28" data-timestamp="387.28">Which brings us to today's program, stories of Muslims and non-Muslims shouting across a divide, trying to communicate with each other and not always getting their point across. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Act One of today's show, forget about the problems of presidents and diplomats have being understood by each other. Why would it be so hard for Muslims and non-Muslims to get along in the fourth grade.</p><p begin="00:06:53.70" data-timestamp="413.7">Act Two, America the Ad Campaign. In that act, Shalom Auslander explains the difficulties of selling brand America to the Arab world, which he experienced in his job at a big ad agency. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Which One of These Is Not Like the Others?</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:07.83" data-timestamp="427.83"> Act One, Which One of These is Not Like the Others? This is partly a story about Muslims and non-Muslims not seeing eye to eye. But it's also about how that kind of dispute can affect a family. And effect it in a really big way, a surprisingly large way. Alix Spiegel tells what happened.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:07:28.30" data-timestamp="448.3">This sad story begins with a happy one, a love story set in a location not usually associated with love stories, the West Bank. It was there that Serry met her husband, a man with a wonderful sense of humor who, like Serry, felt strongly about his Islamic faith. Now because they were observant Muslims, the couple didn't date in the traditional American way. Instead, they met exclusively in the presence of family, talked, exchange philosophies. Serry was, is, an American, born and bred. But these restrictions didn't bother her, didn't faze her at all.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:08:02.67" data-timestamp="482.67">I pretty much knew this was the person I wanted to have children with. I knew he was a really decent, to the core kind of guy. And it really didn't take long for us to realize, we were pretty much soul mates.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:08:26.26" data-timestamp="506.26">So there really was no question that they would get married. No question that they would spend the rest of their lives together. The only real question was where to raise their family. On this point, Serry felt pretty strongly. When they were getting to know each other, her husband had shared horror stories about his childhood in the West Bank, stories of situations that Serry, who grew up in a large American city without being exposed to strife of any kind, had no interest in experiencing firsthand.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:08:53.62" data-timestamp="533.62">So I convinced him to come to America. And I said it would be a perfect place to raise our children because they would never have to go through some of the things he went through as a child. Anyway, he was very hopeful. She couldn't take another year of turmoil.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:34:44.10" data-timestamp="2084.1">So over a few weeks' time, we literally packed up, and we pretty much were forced to leave.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:34:53.72" data-timestamp="2093.72">Serry remembers sitting down with her husband to try to figure out whether the family should move east, west, north or south. But her husband had a very different idea.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:35:02.82" data-timestamp="2102.82">When it came time for us to move from where we were, he wanted to leave the country, which I couldn't fathom. And so he wound up leaving us. He wound up just walking out one day.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:35:27.94" data-timestamp="2127.94">Serry naturally was devastated. She loved her husband. She loves her husband. But she didn't want to move back to the West Bank, and that was the place her husband yearned to go. He argued that at least everyone in the West Bank had the same problem, was in the same boat. And for him, that made the difficulties bearable. In America, he said, you face the same troubles, but you face them alone. Serry didn't see things that way at all. She says that because she grew up in this country, she just had a more subtle reading of what had happened to her family.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:35:59.51" data-timestamp="2159.51">In other words-- well I was born and raised in this country, and I'm aware of what makes this country great, and I know that what happened to our family, it doesn't speak to American values. And I feel like this is such a fluke. I have to believe this is not what America is about. I know that. But I don't think that's the same for my husband. I think he came to this country with certain hopes and dreams, and it was pretty much a big letdown.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:36:46.24" data-timestamp="2206.24">Today, Serry lives alone with her five children in a small apartment about an hour away from her old town. Instead of being a full time mom, as she was for over a decade, she has two jobs and goes to school at night. This means that she rarely sees her children. To keep up with their lives, she has built a mailbox for the kitchen counter. She asks her kids to write her letters about their lives, what happened to them during the day, week. And then, late at night after they've gone to sleep, she sits alone in her bed and reads them. She says that when they don't make her cry, they make her laugh.</p><p begin="00:37:31.19" data-timestamp="2251.19">It's been almost a year since her husband walked out, went to live with a cousin in a distant state while the family tries to figure out what to do. Serry says that every night she prays for him to come back to her, but they only talks sporadically. Still, she hasn't changed her feelings about moving to the West Bank or her interpretation of what happened to her children and family.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Serry</h4><p begin="00:37:52.15" data-timestamp="2272.15">I have to believe this was a fluke. I have to. Otherwise, it's really hard to keep moving forward. But I also think it's a sign of our times. It's happening all across America. It's not just happening in our little town here. We hear stories of different things going on in schools, and places of employment, and public places, in restaurants.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:38:26.15" data-timestamp="2306.15">From his post at the Justice Department, Eric Treene, in his own way, has seen the same thing.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Eric Treene</h4><p begin="00:38:31.54" data-timestamp="2311.54">There's been a dramatic rise in hate crimes since 9/11 against Muslims, Arabs, Sikhs, who are often mistaken as being Muslim, and South Asians. And sometimes people with dark skin, such as a Portuguese man, just because they're perceived to be Arab or Muslim.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:38:51.76" data-timestamp="2331.76">In fact, says Treene, since 9/11, one in five of the complaints reviewed by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission involves a case of discrimination against Muslims. That number, one in five, is of course totally out of proportion to the number of Muslims that live in America.</p><p begin="00:39:13.67" data-timestamp="2353.67">As for Chloe, she's now happy in her new school, and so far hasn't encountered any problems. She's even gone back to being Muslim.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chloe</h4><p begin="00:39:21.61" data-timestamp="2361.61">I pray and occasionally will go to the mosque and everything. And we celebrated Ramadan.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:39:31.55" data-timestamp="2371.55">And how did that feel?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chloe</h4><p begin="00:39:33.40" data-timestamp="2373.4">It felt good.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alix Spiegel</h4><p begin="00:39:35.57" data-timestamp="2375.57">But, she says, she never acknowledges her religion to her new friends, or really to anyone at her new school. It just wouldn't be a good idea, she tells me.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Chloe</h4><p begin="00:39:45.23" data-timestamp="2385.23">We're still going through war and everything, and it's just so a bad time.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:40:01.55" data-timestamp="2401.55">Alix Spiegel in Washington, DC. Coming up, now a quick word from our friendly sponsor, who happens to be the world's only superpower. How to explain America in 30 seconds on television to people far, far away. <div class="act" id="act2">
  <h3>Act Two: America, The Ad Campaign</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:41:21.39" data-timestamp="2481.39">It's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> from Ira Glass. Each week on our program we choose a theme, bring you a variety of different kinds of stories on that theme. Today's show, Shouting Across the Divide, stories of Muslims and non-Muslims trying to communicate with each other, and running into some difficulty sometimes doing that. We have arrived at Act Two of our program. Act Two, America the Ad Campaign.</p><p begin="00:41:44.05" data-timestamp="2504.05">Not long after September 11, as the United States started to fight the Taliban in Afghanistan, there came to be a great interest in this country in bolstering America's image abroad among people who did not seem to see us the way that we see ourselves. We wanted to change that. And one man who got swept up in that effort was one of our regular contributors here at our radio show, Shalom Auslander. Here he is.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Shalom Auslander</h4><p begin="00:42:08.01" data-timestamp="2528.01">I worked for an advertising agency in New York City. A major soft drink company needed some commercials for the urban youth market. This meant black kids. They wanted a magic liquid campaign. This meant that someone in the commercial would drink the soda and become magically transformed. The unhappy would become happy. The stupid would become smart. The unpopular would become beloved. I showed them some commercials a week later.</p><p begin="00:42:35.32" data-timestamp="2555.32">One commercial took place at a swimming pool where the kids were unhappy. Then they drank the soda. Then they were happy. A long, uncomfortable silence filled the room. Finally, the chief creative director spoke. &quot;Do black kids swim?&quot; he asked. A long debate followed. I hated my job.</p><p begin="00:43:00.41" data-timestamp="2580.41">Three months after 9/11, a meeting was held in the chief creative director's office. America had an image problem in the Muslim world, and the State Department had asked a number of advertising agencies to help fix it. Our agency was one of them. &quot;It wasn't just a chance to help the country,&quot; said the chief creative director, &quot;it was a chance to help earn some positive PR for the agency by doing something good.&quot; As he spoke the word good, he made quotation marks in the air with his fingers.</p><p begin="00:43:30.56" data-timestamp="2610.56">Nobody wanted to work on it. There were a half dozen senior creative directors below the chief creative director, each of whom was responsible for his or her own accounts. Their job performance, salaries, and raises were evaluated in terms of how much money their accounts made or lost over the course of the year. &quot;I don't have time for this pro bono crap,&quot; said one. &quot;I mean, I'd love to help, but my software client is up my wazoo with a pitch fork.&quot; Others nodded. &quot;Are they doing TV?&quot; asked one. &quot;It's possible,&quot; said the chief creative director, &quot;but it won't run in this country.&quot; Everyone groaned. If the ads didn't air on American television, then they weren't eligible for American advertising awards shows.</p><p begin="00:44:18.31" data-timestamp="2658.31">Most of the creative directors bailed. I was a freelancer at the time, a hired gun. I worked on whatever they told me to work on. The briefing would be later that afternoon. The chief creative director followed me out the door. &quot;Don't let this interfere with the soda account,&quot; he said. &quot;We really have to nail that.&quot;</p><p begin="00:44:44.46" data-timestamp="2684.46">I decided to get some lunch. A message was waiting for me when I returned. The account director on Project Dialogue had phoned. That was the code name for the brand America account, Project Dialogue. I phoned her back. &quot;Do you need an Arab?&quot; she asked. &quot;Hello?&quot; &quot;Do you need an Arab?&quot; &quot;I don't need an Arab.&quot; &quot;OK&quot; &quot;Thanks though.&quot; &quot;If you need one, let me know. We can get you one.&quot; &quot;I'm good.&quot;</p><p begin="00:45:17.00" data-timestamp="2717">I took a bite of my chicken sandwich, and flipped through the newspapers and magazines that had collected on my desk. <i>Advertizing</i> <i>Age</i> had a cover story on why it's so hard to cram the benefits of brand USA into a 30-second spot and what might work. They had interviewed famous creative directors across the nation. &quot;You have to infiltrate their lives,&quot; said one. &quot;You set up events. You show them movies.&quot; He suggested <i>Boogie</i> <i>Nights.</i></p><p begin="00:45:51.59" data-timestamp="2751.59">The Project Dialogue briefing was supposed to begin at 3:00, but trouble on an international cosmetics account pushed it back to 4:00. And news of a possible fast-food pitch pushed it back again until 5:00. Finally, at 5:45, the creative director arrived. He glanced at his watch. &quot;Christ almighty,&quot; he said. &quot;Let's get this over with.&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:14.41" data-timestamp="2774.41">The lights dimmed and the Powerpoint presentation began. As with any other target audience, Muslims had been divided into a few different groups. Likely to agree, the moderate Muslims. Unlikely to agree, the radical Muslims. And the undecideds. A red arrow appeared beside the word &quot;undecideds&quot; and began to flash. &quot;This,&quot; said the strategic planner, &quot;is our sweet spot.&quot;</p><p begin="00:46:40.80" data-timestamp="2800.8">A few slides later, the undecideds were further broken down into three more subgroups. The hero worshipers, the economic hopefuls, and the future seekers, each of which was divided again into another three groups, all nine of which would require their own distinct form of messaging. &quot;What's the message?&quot; someone asked. &quot;We don't know,&quot; answered the strategic planner. &quot;Do they like humor?&quot; someone asked. &quot;We don't know.&quot; &quot;Do they trust commercials?&quot; &quot;We don't know.&quot; &quot;Are there any commercials they do like?&quot; &quot;We don't know?&quot; &quot;Is a direct approach the best?&quot; &quot;No.&quot; &quot;How will they react to an indirect approach?&quot; &quot;Badly.&quot;</p><p begin="00:47:24.88" data-timestamp="2844.88">For a group of people setting out to convince another group of people how much we had in common, we didn't seem to know the first thing about them. And I was no exception. The only footage I had ever seen of Afghanistan was of madrassas. Row after row of children rocking back and forth reciting passages loudly from the Koran, a sight so eerily similar to the childhood I'd spent locked up like a veal in an orthodox Jewish yeshiva that I wanted to bomb the place myself.</p><p begin="00:47:53.36" data-timestamp="2873.36">The strategic planner told us that basic messaging concepts were still being tested in focus groups in Egypt and Jordan. And we wouldn't have the final strategy until the following afternoon. The basic function, though, of all communications would be to convince the Muslim world that we all wanted a better life, and that freedom and democracy were the best ways of achieving those desires. I realized that if you switched freedom and democracy with cool and refreshing, it was pretty much the same strategy as the one I'd been given for selling soda to African American kids. But I thought it might be best to bring this up after the meeting.</p><p begin="00:48:29.22" data-timestamp="2909.22">&quot;So what do you think?&quot; the creative director asked me after the meeting. &quot;I think it's the same strategy as the soda account.&quot; &quot;How is that going?&quot; he asked. &quot;Good,&quot; I said. &quot;I've been thinking. What if we change it from a swimming pool to a picnic.&quot; He thought about that for a moment before frowning and shaking his head. &quot;No good?&quot; I asked. He continued shaking his head. &quot;Black kids don't have picnics?&quot; I asked. &quot;More urban,&quot; he said, &quot;you know, like fire hydrants.&quot; &quot;Fire hydrants?&quot; &quot;Yeah,&quot; he said &quot;you know, when they open the fire hydrants. When it's hot.&quot; &quot;Sure,&quot; I said. &quot;When it's hot.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:13.64" data-timestamp="2953.64">I assumed we would cut before the cops came and started beating the kids with their night sticks. He nodded, growing increasingly pleased with his idea. &quot;I like that,&quot; he said. &quot;A bunch of kids in their bathing suits, jumping around a fire hydrant. We'll get somebody good to direct it. That could be nice, don't you think?&quot; I nodded. &quot;Do black kids have bathing suits?&quot; I asked. &quot;I mean, if they don't swim?&quot; &quot;I like that,&quot; he ignored me. &quot;I like that a lot. &quot;Write a few more like that, will you?&quot; &quot;Sure,&quot; I said. &quot;How's Friday?&quot; &quot;How's tomorrow?&quot; he asked. &quot;Tomorrow,&quot; I said, &quot;no problem.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:58.15" data-timestamp="2998.15">The following morning I was sitting in my office writing commercials about black kids and fire hydrants when there was a knock on my door. I looked up to see a man roughly my age with dark skin, slicked back hair, and a drawing pad. &quot;Shalom?&quot; he asked. &quot;I'm Sabhi.&quot; I rose and shook his hand. &quot;You must be the Arab,&quot; I said. &quot;I'm the Jew.&quot;</p><p begin="00:50:23.98" data-timestamp="3023.98">Sabhi was from Lebanon where he had studied graphic arts at the University of Beirut. I asked him what life was like there, how people felt about America, how they felt about their lives and their future. He told me there was a lot of anger and a lot of frustration. There were economic problems, governmental problems, social problems. I asked him what the cause of the problems might be. He told me it was the Jews.</p><p begin="00:50:48.65" data-timestamp="3048.65">&quot;The Jews?&quot; I asked. &quot;The Israelis,&quot; he said. He explained that according to Islamic legend, the prophet Mohammed said they would never be peace in the Muslim world until three cities were under Muslim rule. They currently had Mecca and Medina, but Jerusalem was still controlled by the Jews. &quot;The Jews?&quot; I asked. &quot;The Israelis,&quot; he said. &quot;And so,&quot; he added, &quot;that's why there can be no peace for Muslims.&quot;</p><p begin="00:51:16.83" data-timestamp="3076.83">An awkward silence followed and an unexpected disappointment overcame me. I realize that as naive as it might have been to think that advertising could solve these century-old problems, a small part of me, and a large part of the US government, hoped that it could. That something, anything, might be said or filmed or edited into a 30-second TV commercial that would undo all that had been so horribly done, and that would avoid all the bad that seemed so certain to come in the future.</p><p begin="00:51:46.13" data-timestamp="3106.13">Unfortunately, I was raised with orthodox religion. And the minute I hear people talking about prophets and holy cities, I lose all hope. That, combined with yesterday's discussion about whether black people swim, and if they do, whether or not they own bathing suits, had caused me to wonder whether we could ever understand the person living right beside us, let alone people on the other side of the world.</p><p begin="00:52:08.44" data-timestamp="3128.44">&quot;Well, you've got Mecca and Medina,&quot; I offered Sabhi. &quot;Two out of three isn't bad.&quot; He kind of laughed. I kind of laughed back. Then he left. The telephone rang. &quot;How did it go?&quot; asked the account director. &quot;Fine,&quot; I said. &quot;Was it helpful?&quot; &quot;Very,&quot; I said. &quot;Should I get you another one?&quot; she asked.</p><p begin="00:52:41.26" data-timestamp="3161.26">The focus groups in Jordan and Egypt had not been very helpful. The moderator had shown a number of different boards to a number of different Arab men ages 18 to 34. The first board read, &quot;Children deserve a happy future. And in order to have a happy future, people must be free.&quot; &quot;Do you agree with this statement?&quot; asked the moderator. Everyone nodded. &quot;But we don't have freedom,&quot; said one, &quot;because of Israel.&quot; Everyone nodded.</p><p begin="00:53:10.88" data-timestamp="3190.88">The moderator held up a second board. &quot;We all deserve happiness,&quot; read the board, &quot;and part of happiness is having the freedom to pursue your business and economic dreams.&quot; Everyone nodded. &quot;We could pursue our economic dreams,&quot; said one, &quot;if it weren't for Israel.&quot; This continued for some time. It continued with women age 18 to 34. It continued with men age 35 to 50. It continued with women age 35 to 50.</p><p begin="00:53:40.03" data-timestamp="3220.03">&quot;Were they all like that?&quot; I asked the strategic planner. He nodded. &quot;What are you going to do?&quot; I asked. &quot;Get off this goddamn assignment,&quot; he said.</p><p begin="00:53:53.09" data-timestamp="3233.09">There was a four o'clock meeting later that afternoon for me to present my new soda commercials. I made my way to the creative director's office a few minutes early and asked if I could speak with him. &quot;I can't work on this America thing,&quot; I told him. &quot;Why not?&quot; he asked. &quot;Did you come up with anything?&quot; &quot;I've got a slogan,&quot; I told him. &quot;Two out of three ain't bad. It's about the holy cities.&quot;</p><p begin="00:54:20.04" data-timestamp="3260.04">On the television behind his desk, Geraldo Rivera was reporting from Tora Bora. &quot;We want Osama bin Laden behind bars,&quot; said Geraldo. &quot;Or six feet under. Or maybe just one foot under. Or maybe just a pile of ash.&quot; They cut back to Laurie Dhue in the studio. &quot;Well said Geraldo,&quot; said Laurie Dhue. The creative director sighed and shook his head. &quot;It's hopeless, isn't it?&quot; he said. &quot;Mankind?&quot; I asked. &quot;Yes,&quot; I said, &quot;it is.&quot;</p><p begin="00:54:51.47" data-timestamp="3291.47">We went into the conference room and I presented my black kids around a fire hydrant commercial. For the next 20 minutes, 15 Caucasian people sat around a long, oak table discussing whether or not fire hydrants were racist. And if they weren't, would black kids have bathing suits, given that they don't swim?</p><p begin="00:55:12.42" data-timestamp="3312.42">I still hated my job, but at least I was off the America thing. Within a few months, everyone was off it. After dozens of focus groups and tens of thousands of dollars, the only conclusion anyone could reach regarding the question of how to speak to Muslims was that nobody had any idea how to speak to Muslims.</p><p begin="00:55:30.90" data-timestamp="3330.9">It wasn't long before the department within the State Department that had been charged with this assignment was dismantled. A few projects survived. Some radio stations were jammed. Some leaflets were dropped. Then Iraq was invaded. Then Shiites began killing Sunnis. Then Sunnis began killing Shiites.</p><p begin="00:55:53.36" data-timestamp="3353.36">Maybe it was naive, but five years ago when all this was happening, someone said, let's talk to Muslims. Lately, more and more people are saying, let's not. Let's let Iran talk to them. Let's let Syria talk to them. Let's let the Muslims talk to themselves.</p><p begin="00:56:16.97" data-timestamp="3376.97">My fire hydrant commercials never ran either. In the end, it was felt that the fire hydrants might be perceived as racist. And they were replaced in the script with swimming pools and diving boards, scripts a moderator then showed to a roomful of African Americans age 16 to 21.</p><p begin="00:56:33.43" data-timestamp="3393.43">&quot;Do you like these commercials?&quot; the moderator asked. Everyone frowned. &quot;Black kids don't swim,&quot; said the African Americans age 16 to 21. Someone suggested the location should be more urban, a city perhaps, maybe a fire hydrant. The moderator nodded. A few weeks later, the project was reassigned to an African American agency. It was thought best that they talked to themselves.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:57:11.15" data-timestamp="3431.15">Shalom Auslander. He's the author of a book of short stories called <i>Beware</i> <i>of</i> <i>God.</i> A new book about his own very strict religious upbringing comes out next year.</p></div>  </div>

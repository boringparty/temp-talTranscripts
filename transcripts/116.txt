  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.69" data-timestamp="0.69">There's certain conversations that those of us who do not live on farms get into with people who do live on farms, where you know, you just know. As the city person, or the suburban homeowner, you know that the farm person you're talking to is just thinking the entire time you're talking to them, &quot;You are so naive.&quot; A lot of these conversations have to do with animals and their deaths.</p><p begin="00:00:24.27" data-timestamp="24.27">Take this example. The woman who runs the website for our radio show, Elizabeth Meister, was at the annual exhibition run by the American Poultry Association in Columbus, Ohio. And here she is. She's standing in a room with 12,000 chickens. And she gets into this conversation with an 11-year-old girl.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:00:40.35" data-timestamp="40.35">I've only been in poultry and waterfowl for a year. I'm more into waterfowl.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:45.42" data-timestamp="45.42">This is Kamiko Overs from Bliss, New York, who was at the convention to show some of her own birds.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:00:50.45" data-timestamp="50.45">So do your friends think it's weird? Do they think it's strange that you show chickens and stuff?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:00:57.41" data-timestamp="57.41">No, because a lot of people show. They'll either be showing pigs, or cows, or rabbits, or something like that. A lot of people in our school show different things.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:15.27" data-timestamp="75.27">Kamiko and Elizabeth talked for a while. And then Elizabeth tried, very, very gently, to ask a question that was on her mind.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:01:23.23" data-timestamp="83.23">Do you-- this is sort of a hard question for me to ask. But do you ever eat them?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:01:29.08" data-timestamp="89.08">Yeah, I do.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:01:29.58" data-timestamp="89.58">You do. And does it make you really sad when-- do you raise these chickens from little chicks?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:01:36.53" data-timestamp="96.53">Yeah, we hatch them out. And then, yep. We get them right from the hen. And we eat them a lot. We do raise a lot for eating.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:01:47.24" data-timestamp="107.24">And so when you're cooking up this chicken that you've raised from being a little chick, do you feel a little bit guilty that you're eating this chicken?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:01:57.11" data-timestamp="117.11">No.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:01:57.58" data-timestamp="117.58">No?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:01:58.06" data-timestamp="118.06">No.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Elizabeth Meister</h4><p begin="00:01:58.53" data-timestamp="118.53">Do you ever eat the chickens that you show?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kamiko Overs</h4><p begin="00:02:01.23" data-timestamp="121.23">If they're no good for show, the losers, yeah.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:10.76" data-timestamp="130.76">Kamiko is untroubled by this. If a bird has bad feathers or a lopsided comb, it might be a nice bird. But it's not going to win competitions. You eat it. That's life.</p><p begin="00:02:22.34" data-timestamp="142.34">Those of us who do not live on farms, we divide animals off into those we love and those we eat. In Kamiko's world, there's not such a hard line separating the two. You can get to know a bird on an up-close and one-to-one basis and still eat it. An animal's transition from buddy to dinner is a great deal less problematic than it is for us city folks.</p><p begin="00:02:52.25" data-timestamp="172.25">Well, from WBEZ Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. And every year, during the period between Thanksgiving and Christmas, the highest poultry consumption time of the year in these United States, we bring you a program about poultry, our annual Poultry Slam, stories of chickens, turkeys, ducks, birds of all kinds, and our relationships with them.</p><p begin="00:03:14.69" data-timestamp="194.69">In past years, we have brought you an opera about the life of Chicken Little. We've brought you the story of a hand puppet duck that would not leave people's lives. And naturally, we've brought you real stories of actually growing up with turkeys, and chickens, and other birds. And in putting together this year's show, a very few particular ideas and questions kept coming up in story after story. Without us ever intending for it to happen this way, a lot of this year's show has turned into a kind of referendum about where you draw that line between friend and food.</p><p begin="00:03:50.67" data-timestamp="230.67">Act One of today's program, Still Life with Chicken. Food writer Jonathan Gold tells what it's like to pan-fry a chicken with a live chicken watching you the entire time. Act Two, Last Meal. When Francois Mitterand knew he was about to die, he decided that the last food to cross his lips would be poultry, a tiny bird that is actually illegal to eat in France, a bird that, by tradition, is eaten with a napkin covering your head. Michael Paterniti set out for France to try the contraband capon himself. He has a full report.</p><p begin="00:04:24.75" data-timestamp="264.75">Act Three, People who Love Chickens and Hate <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> Last year, a woman named Karen Davis started a national letter-writing campaign to try to get <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> to stop the very program you're listening to at this very moment, the annual Poultry Slam. In this portion of our show, she explains what it is that we just do not understand about poultry, and why the whole idea of this poultry show was wrongheaded from the start. Act Four, The Meaning of a Bird. David Rakoff explains how his life was changed in a single evening in a room of 5,000 chickens. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Still Life With Chicken</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:07.91" data-timestamp="307.91">Act One, Still Life with Chicken. It was an accident that Jonathan ended up living with the chicken. He was not living the kind of lifestyle that one usually associates with chicken.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jonathan Gold</h4><p begin="00:05:20.04" data-timestamp="320.04">This was during the period when I considered myself to be a performance artist of a sort, a naked performance artist, to be specific.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:27.18" data-timestamp="327.18">These days, Jonathan Gold is a food writer in Los Angeles. This all happened 15 years ago. He was putting together a performance. He had a PA system which could put out the requisite amount of annoying feedback sound at high decibels. He had the two full bottles of Glade American Beauty air freshener, which he would spray, in their entirety, in the performance space.</p><p begin="00:05:47.75" data-timestamp="347.75">And he had a live chicken, which he bought the day before the performance in one of those Chinese poultry markets in Los Angeles. And it comes the day of the show. An audience gathers in a darkened warehouse in West LA.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jonathan Gold</h4><p begin="00:05:59.39" data-timestamp="359.39">I don't know if you've been to a lot of performance art, but this was really typical of the stuff that was going on in the period. And I showed up, and I was naked. And I was carrying a machete, and I was blindfolded. And I stood in the middle of this pile of supermarket chickens, the broilers that you buy. And the chicken that I bought was tethered to a three-foot rope around me. And I hacked up and down blindly with a machete.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:31.35" data-timestamp="391.35">Toward the chicken, or just in general?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jonathan Gold</h4><p begin="00:06:33.42" data-timestamp="393.42">Well, I was blindfolded, so I didn't know if it was towards the chicken or not. And I had fully intended that, in fact, I would kill the chicken in the midst of this performance. But chickens aren't that stupid. And this chicken wanted no part of the machete, stayed at the end of its rope the entire time, apparently.</p><p begin="00:06:59.39" data-timestamp="419.39">And after 10 minutes, when I was completely exhausted, I fell to a heap, and everybody left. And the performance was over. I don't know if you've stuck around after an art performance, but the few minutes after an art performance are some of the most depressing in the world.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:21.41" data-timestamp="441.41">How so?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jonathan Gold</h4><p begin="00:07:24.04" data-timestamp="444.04">You've done your wad. You've done your bit for art, which has either worked or it hasn't. But you're sitting there. You're covered with chicken effluvia, in my case. It stinks to high hell. Everybody's gone, and you've got to clean up. And you're naked. It's really not a pretty picture.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:52.98" data-timestamp="472.98">So Jonathan cleaned up. And when he was done, he had a chicken. And he didn't feel like he could kill the chicken. Destiny had brought them together. He felt like he could not turn his back.</p><p begin="00:08:03.23" data-timestamp="483.23">He says it was the same as if a kitten shows up on your back door, scratching, and lonely, and needy. So he took the chicken home.

Some live in caves. Some cross borders in disguise. Some slip from one safe house to the next. Sometimes they must go hungry. They pray. But for every person on the front lines, there are dozens backing them up with equipment, logistical support, and Cheez-Its. This aircraft carrier has only 50 or 60 pilots on board who actually drop bombs, who actually take part in combat. Everyone else is here, over 5,000 people, to get them in the air.</p><p begin="00:02:36.52" data-timestamp="156.52">Back home, over the last few months, we've heard about the pilots. It's one thing to turn your life upside down to go shoot bullets at bad guys. It's another to give up everything to go fill candy machines 12 hours a day.</p><p begin="00:02:58.07" data-timestamp="178.07">In the months since the <i>USS</i> <i>Stennis</i> embarked for the Arabian Sea, Crevon has only gone outside twice. If you picture for a moment what an aircraft carrier looks like, the deck of the ship, that big, flat deck, is where jets take off and land, so you can't just walk around up there. She sees daylight maybe once a day. She's 20 years old. It's her first time away from home.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Crevon Scott</h4><p begin="00:03:18.68" data-timestamp="198.68">You know, the hardest part is just missing home, the home thing. They email me every day and stuff.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:41.28" data-timestamp="221.28">Today on our program, everyday life aboard a ship at war, what it's really like. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Alex and Wendy and I went to the <i>Stennis</i> together, and throughout this hour you'll hear from all three of us.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Wendy Dorr</h4><p begin="00:03:58.96" data-timestamp="238.96">We got to the <i>Stennis</i> about six weeks after the ship arrived in the North Arabian Sea.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:03.69" data-timestamp="243.69">The other aircraft carrier on duty there, the <i>Theodore</i> <i>Roosevelt,</i> sends planes during the day, and the <i>Stennis</i> sends them at night. This means that reveille is called over the ship's PA system to wake everybody up for work at 7:00 PM. We expected a bugle, or at least a recording of a bugle, but it turns out to be sort of no frills.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Pa System</h4><p begin="00:04:20.22" data-timestamp="260.22">Reveille, reveille, all hands heave out.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:23.42" data-timestamp="263.42">From the time we get up, we're led everywhere by a group of public affairs officers, who are, contrary to what you think they might be-- well, contrary to what we suspected they'd be anyway-- good-humored and quite helpful. It's four and 1/2 acres. But most of the life of the ship takes place here, in the eight stories of rooms and passageways below the flight deck. There are no windows on these decks, and the boat is so big that in good weather, it doesn't really even sway. And so most of the time, the sensation is less being at sea than being underground in some sort of huge subterranean warren.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Officer 2</h4><p begin="00:05:05.03" data-timestamp="305.03">Can I cut through?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:06.61" data-timestamp="306.61">You're constantly squeezing by people coming from the other direction.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Officer 2</h4><p begin="00:05:09.03" data-timestamp="309.03">Hey you.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sailor 1</h4><p begin="00:05:09.53" data-timestamp="309.53">Are you still awake?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Officer 2</h4><p begin="00:05:10.36" data-timestamp="310.36">I'm still awake.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:11.79" data-timestamp="311.79">After five minutes of winding our way through these narrow corridors, we come to a big, rounded steel door. It's maybe five feet tall, airtight. It's got a big lever in the middle. Someone turns it, and we climb through into one of the largest rooms we've ever seen.</p><p begin="00:05:31.51" data-timestamp="331.51">This is the hangar deck. It's two football fields long, three stories tall. Entering it is like walking through a closet door and finding yourself at the pitcher's mound in the Astrodome. We walk past parked fighter planes and hundreds of people, all scurrying around, pushing hand trucks, talking into walkie-talkies. And then we arrive at the hangar bay door, which is basically a three-story hole in the side of the ship.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Wendy Dorr</h4><p begin="00:05:57.54" data-timestamp="357.54">It's nighttime.

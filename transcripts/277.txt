  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.14" data-timestamp="0.14">My mom was a therapist and she saw hundreds of couples where somebody in the couple had cheated. And then the couple tried to stay together and work it out, and that's how they ended up in couples counseling. And my mom said this thing nearly always happens in this situation. This thing happens where the person who cheated confesses. Tells the spouse as many details as the spouse wants to hear, which usually is a lot. And in fact, you kind of have to tell them everything they want if there's going to be any kind of forgiveness. And so it's just all this painful detail. What happened this particular day when you said you were here and did you say I love you to the other person? Just like the incredibly painful, incredibly hard stuff. And the person who cheated says that they know they were in the wrong, and they want to work it out, and they're sorry. They're so, so sorry.</p><p begin="00:00:46.20" data-timestamp="46.2">And then weeks pass, and everybody tries to get along. And at some point, the person who cheated starts to feel a little better. You know, they confessed. They cleared the air. It's a new beginning. But their spouse is living still, in a world of pain. The spouse doesn't trust them. The betrayal was so huge, the lies were so big, the spouse is still wary. And still, little bursts of anger. They're not over it.</p><p begin="00:01:13.93" data-timestamp="73.93">And here's where it gets complicated. The person who cheated starts to get impatient. They feel like, come on, I confessed. I said I'm sorry. I've done everything I can do to make this right. But you just won't let it go. And the other person can't let it go. A very hard thing for both of them. A very hard thing for any couple to get through. And it almost always happens. The apology wasn't enough. And really, there are so many situations where apologizing doesn't do the trick, where it doesn't satisfy both parties. And not just big situations, these can be the tiniest situations in the world.</p><p begin="00:01:52.09" data-timestamp="112.09">Sarah Vowell, one of our contributing editors on the radio show says that she was running late on her way to meet a friend for breakfast.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Vowell</h4><p begin="00:01:59.56" data-timestamp="119.56">I was on the subway and I realized I didn't know where breakfast was. I was, I don't know, 15 or 20 minutes late, which was just the most agonizing 15 minutes of my life of me running up and down 9th Avenue trying to remember where I was supposed to meet him. You know, I got there and he had wondered where was I was, but he brought the paper and he was fine, so I spent the entire breakfast apologizing. Then when I got home I sent him an email apologizing. Then before I went to bed I sent another apology email.</p><p begin="00:02:30.65" data-timestamp="150.65">Then the next time we had breakfast I was 15 minutes early and then had to talk about how I was early this time. And it's just so stupid. And I realized at that point, the apology sickness becomes a kind of narcissism. Like, all you think about is yourself and you feel like, oh, my actions affected this other person so much I need to just constantly apologize. When really, it wasn't that big of a deal.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:04.16" data-timestamp="184.16">This happens all the time. The apology can go wrong for either person in the transaction. Even the apologizer.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Sarah Vowell</h4><p begin="00:03:10.57" data-timestamp="190.57">I feel like he totally accepted my apology. It's more like I didn't accept my apology. [LAUGHTER]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:22.69" data-timestamp="202.69">There's moments where you apologize to somebody and you're not doing it for selfish reasons, but because you really want them to know you're sorry. And where they really hear what you're saying and your eyes lock and everybody knows that it happened, the apology happened. Those are almost pretty rare, I think.</p><p begin="00:03:37.48" data-timestamp="217.48">Today on our radio show, we have three stories of people groping their way toward I'm sorry, toward this moment of apology. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> distributed by Public Radio International, I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:03:48.19" data-timestamp="228.19">Our program today in three acts. And let me just say right here, it's a politics free program. Let's have a little break. Act one, &quot;Repeat After Me.&quot; In that story, David Sedaris, his sister, and a talking bird. Act two, &quot;Dial S for Sorry.&quot; In which we have a little visit with Mr. Apology and his wife. Act three, &quot;Two Words You Never Want to Hear From Your Doctor.&quot; In that act, a movement to try to get people who do not like to say they're wrong to say they're wrong. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Repeat After Me</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:25.45" data-timestamp="265.45">Act one, &quot;Repeat After Me.&quot;</p><p begin="00:04:27.87" data-timestamp="267.87">This story was recorded in front of a live audience. Here's David Sedaris.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>David Sedaris</h4><p begin="00:04:32.61" data-timestamp="272.61">Although we discussed my upcoming visit to Winston-Salem, my sister and I didn't make exact arrangements until the eve of my arrival when I phoned from a hotel in Salt Lake City.</p><p begin="00:04:43.89" data-timestamp="283.89">&quot;I'll be at work when you arrive,&quot; she said. &quot;So I'm thinking I'll just leave the key under the ower-ot near the ack door.&quot;</p><p begin="00:04:54.99" data-timestamp="294.99">The what?</p><p begin="00:04:57.05" data-timestamp="297.05">&quot;Ower-ot.&quot;</p><p begin="00:04:59.86" data-timestamp="299.86">I thought she maybe had something in her mouth until I realized she was speaking in code.</p><p begin="00:05:06.87" data-timestamp="306.87">What are you on a speaker phone at a methadone clinic? Why can't you just tell me where you put the god damn house key?</p><p begin="00:05:15.34" data-timestamp="315.34">Her voice dropped to a whisper. &quot;I just don't know that I trust these things.&quot;</p><p begin="00:05:20.46" data-timestamp="320.46">Are you on a cellphone?</p><p begin="00:05:21.89" data-timestamp="321.89">&quot;Of course not,&quot; she said. &quot;This is just a regular cordless. But still, you have to be careful.&quot;</p><p begin="00:05:30.09" data-timestamp="330.09">My sister's the type who religiously watches the fear segments of her local eyewitness news broadcasts. Retaining nothing but the headline. She remembers that applesauce can kill you, but forgets that in order to die you have to inject it directly into your bloodstream.</p><p begin="00:05:49.70" data-timestamp="349.7">Announcements that cellphone conversations may be picked up by strangers mixed with the reported rise of both home burglaries and brain tumors, meaning that as far as she's concerned all telecommunication is potentially life threatening.</p><p begin="00:06:05.16" data-timestamp="365.16">OK, I said. But can you tell me which ower-ot?</p><p begin="00:06:10.11" data-timestamp="370.11">&quot;It's ed.&quot; She told me. &quot;Well, edish.&quot;</p><p begin="00:06:18.19" data-timestamp="378.19">I arrived at Lisa's house late the following afternoon, found the key beneath the flower pot, and let myself in through the back door. A lengthy note on the coffee table explained how I might go about operating everything from the television to the waffle iron. Each carefully detailed procedure ending with the line, remember to unplug after use. The note reflected a growing hysteria. Its subtext shrieking, oh my God, he's going to be alone in my house for close to an hour.</p><p begin="00:06:53.24" data-timestamp="413.24">She left her work number, her husband's work number, and the number of the next door neighbor, explaining that she didn't know the woman very well, so I probably shouldn't bother her unless it was an emergency. PPS, she's a Baptist, so don't tell her you're gay.</p><p begin="00:07:13.28" data-timestamp="433.28">The last time I was alone at my sister's place, she was living in a white brick apartment complex occupied by widows and single middle-aged working women. College hadn't quite worked out the way she'd expected. And after two years in Virginia, she'd returned to Raleigh and taken a job at a wine shop. It was a normal enough life for a 21 year old, but being a dropout was now what she had planned for herself. Worse than that, it had not been planned for her.</p><p begin="00:07:41.07" data-timestamp="461.07">As children, we'd been assigned certain roles-- leader, bum, troublemaker, slut. Titles that effectively told us who we were. As the oldest, smartest, and bossiest, it was naturally assumed that Lisa would shoot to the top of her field, earning a master's degree in manipulation, and eventually taking over a medium-sized country. We'd always known her as an authority figure, and while we took a certain joy in watching her fall, it was disorienting to see her with so little confidence.</p><p begin="00:08:19.19" data-timestamp="499.19">Suddenly she was relying on other people's opinions, following their advice, and withering at the slightest criticism. Do you really think so? Really? She was putty.</p><p begin="00:08:31.26" data-timestamp="511.26">My sister needed patience and understanding, but more often than not, I found myself wanting to shake her. If the oldest wasn't who she was supposed to be, then what did it mean for the rest of us?</p><p begin="00:08:42.66" data-timestamp="522.66">Lisa has been marked most likely to succeed and so it confused her to be ringing up gallon jugs of hardy burgundy. I had been branded as lazy and irresponsible, and so it felt right when I, too, dropped out of college.</p><p begin="00:08:59.96" data-timestamp="539.96">I went to live with Lisa in her white brick complex. And when I eventually left her with a broken stereo and an unpaid $80 phone bill, the general consensus was, well, what did you expect? I might reinvent myself to strangers, but to this day, as far as my family is concerned, I'm still the one most likely to set your house on fire.</p><p begin="00:09:24.41" data-timestamp="564.41">While I accepted my lowered expectations, Lisa fought hard to regain her former title. The wine shop was just a temporary setback and she left shortly after becoming the manager. Photography interested her and so she taught herself to use a camera. But those errors don't translate into 44,000 to 195,000 apologies from doctors. Starlee Kine says this may be changing, but very, very slowly.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:40:11.03" data-timestamp="2411.03">Five years ago, 39 year old Michael Lang had a stomach ache that was causing him a lot of pain. That night he checked into the local emergency room. The staff looked at him briefly and decided his condition wasn't too serious. The family says his mother talked to a nurse and requested an ultrasound, but that was rebuffed. Michael was given morphine for the pain and told he'd be able to go home the next morning.</p><p begin="00:40:32.99" data-timestamp="2432.99">Overnight, he died of an internal hemorrhage and blood around his heart. The hospital called Mike's parents and told them that his condition had worsened, but not that he had died. They got in their car and rushed over. This is Mike's sister, Roxanne.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:40:46.43" data-timestamp="2446.43">On the way there my parents start talking about taking him somewhere else, not realizing that he was already dead. And when they arrived at the hospital, no one was there to meet them and they took the elevator up to the second floor. And when the door opened, a lot of the nurses were standing there whispering. And when my mom looked at one of the nurses, she knew and she turned to my dad and said, he's dead, Ray.</p><p begin="00:41:16.15" data-timestamp="2476.15">And they ran down the hall to his room where Mike was lying in the bed with the IV still in his arm. It was hanging over the side and my dad tried to put it under the sheet, but was unable to bend it. The thing that they remember the most is going over to him and trying to hug him. And he was, you know, stiff and cold. That's their memory of their son.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:41:52.98" data-timestamp="2512.98">It's not just the fact that the doctors didn't catch what was wrong with their son, but the way they were treated by the hospital that haunts Roxanne's family. How they were disregard when they asked for an ultrasound. How they were allowed to drive over thinking Mike was still live. And then how no one was waiting for them with the tragic news once they got there.</p><p begin="00:42:09.19" data-timestamp="2529.19">In the weeks that followed, the things the hospital did just made it worse, as far as the family was concerned. When asked why they hadn't been more attentive to Mike, the hospital said they'd been busy that night with an emergency accident. They denied all responsibility for Mike's death.</p><p begin="00:42:23.39" data-timestamp="2543.39">The town in Wisconsin where this happened is small. Roxanne's parents go to the same church as the hospital's CEO. Roxanne says they never talk. It's awkward. Almost the only communication they've had from him was in a letter he sent after Mike's death.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:42:36.81" data-timestamp="2556.81">He basically said Mike didn't die of a medical error and we have no responsibility for his death at this hospital. We had several committees that investigated it and reviewed everything and we made no mistakes here. Basically, he said we didn't have any part in it. But still, no answers. Still no apologies.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:42:56.49" data-timestamp="2576.49">No?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:42:57.41" data-timestamp="2577.41">No. And the thing about the letter was is he addressed them by their first names, Ray and Betty. Dear Ray and Betty. To me, that was an insult when I read that. I was just like, how can you be so familiar with my parents when you can't even apologize for their son dying in your facility?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Landdeck</h4><p begin="00:43:20.07" data-timestamp="2600.07">Well, see, an apology, why do you apologize to somebody-- because you did something wrong?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:43:27.98" data-timestamp="2607.98">This is the CEO of the hospital, John Landdeck. He and Roxanne don't see the situation the same at all. For him it's like this-- the hospital wasn't at fault from Mike's death. An independent medical commission that looked into the case cleared them of wrongdoing. And besides, he's not even sure the hospital didn't apologize.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Landdeck</h4><p begin="00:43:44.46" data-timestamp="2624.46">To the extent that we failed to communicate our sympathy with them, that's bad. But you know, a family facing the sudden loss of an otherwise normal, healthy, young man doesn't always hear and see everything that's going on either. And those nurses might have said things and we might have and the doctor might have that they completely missed in their grief.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:44:12.64" data-timestamp="2652.64">Do you think when the family started saying stuff like, you know, there was a medical error, he died of a medical error. It was the hospital's fault, stuff like that that the hospital went on the defensive though?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Landdeck</h4><p begin="00:44:22.77" data-timestamp="2662.77">Well, we tried not to. I'm sure that everybody felt that way. If somebody says you're bad and you know you're not and the first thing is to set out to prove that you're right.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:44:34.46" data-timestamp="2674.46">This is really like any fight when someone asks for an apology. One side wants to prove it's not their fault, the other wants acknowledgment that their feelings are valid. There's a lot of talking past one another.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:44:45.38" data-timestamp="2685.38">Is part of the reason that you think you and your family and other families need to hear an apology so badly is because you're afraid that maybe when they don't say they're sorry that they don't actually feel sorry?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:45:01.00" data-timestamp="2701">Most definitely. I remember from Mike-- in Mike's situation-- is we just wanted somebody to publicly acknowledge the fact that he died and that that was an awful thing. You know, whether he died of an error or not, it was an awful thing that he died. And nobody was saying that.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:45:25.37" data-timestamp="2725.37">The reason doctors and hospitals don't apologize is actually pretty simple. It all comes down to malpractice insurance.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Sousa</h4><p begin="00:45:32.20" data-timestamp="2732.2">I think historically the party line has been gag orders.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:45:36.43" data-timestamp="2736.43">This is David Sousa, president of Medical Mutual Insurance Company.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Sousa</h4><p begin="00:45:40.59" data-timestamp="2740.59">As people like us, as an insurance carrier, have seen our society become more and more litigious, it has caused us to go to our physician insureds and to caution them, unfortunately, that anything and everything that they may say in the context of a medical error, can be used against them to prove their negligence if they are sued by that patient.</p><p begin="00:46:07.96" data-timestamp="2767.96">When those kinds of things started happening, we started counseling doctors, much to our chagrin, you need to not have these conversations with your patients. Or if you do have to have them, they've got to be almost sterile in nature so that the patient can't come back and use that against you if they try to sue.</p><p begin="00:46:31.65" data-timestamp="2791.65">Over time, that has had an incredible chilling effect on the ability of doctors to just openly communicate with their patients when something goes wrong.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:46:43.61" data-timestamp="2803.61">And you actually had these conversations with doctors where you would advise them to not say anything?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Sousa</h4><p begin="00:46:50.22" data-timestamp="2810.22">Repeatedly. Repeatedly. Absolutely.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:46:58.84" data-timestamp="2818.84">This is all changing though. The shift began back in the '80s when Lexington, Kentucky Veteran's Hospital lost a series of malpractice suits and decided to try an experiment.</p><p begin="00:47:08.33" data-timestamp="2828.33">The doctors were told that they had to disclose fully and immediately any medical error to patients. And also, apologize if it was appropriate.</p><p begin="00:47:15.68" data-timestamp="2835.68">In 10 years, the hospital went from having some of the highest legal costs in the state to some of the lowest. Meanwhile, the laws in certain states started changing so that when a doctor apologizes it can't be used against them in a lawsuit. There are now laws like that in nine states. Some apply to any kind of apology made in any civil suit. The new ones specifically apply to medical errors. Again, David Sousa from the insurance industry.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Sousa</h4><p begin="00:47:37.70" data-timestamp="2857.7">Now in our state here North Carolina, a statement made that is tantamount to an apology can not be used against that health care provider to prove negligence in a court room. That's huge. It really opens the door now for us to say, now not only do we believe it's the right thing to do, but now you're protected when you do it. And that's huge. That's just giant.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Dr. Albert Wu</h4><p begin="00:48:05.91" data-timestamp="2885.91">The apology. The apology. [LAUGHTER]</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:48:13.72" data-timestamp="2893.72">Tucked away in the basement of Johns Hopkins Medical School through a door that I thought was a fire exit, in a little room that reminded me of the smoking stairwell from college is a movie studio, an instructional movie studio. Dr. Albert Wu, an associate professor at John Hopkins, is shooting a training video here. It's called, &quot;Disclosing Errors and Adverse Outcomes. The Who, What, Where, When, How, and Why of Disclosure.&quot; Or as I like to call it, being a doctor means never having to say you're sorry. Unless something goes wrong, then you really should.</p><p begin="00:48:43.27" data-timestamp="2923.27">For the past 15 years, Dr. Wu has been researching how doctors handle medical mistakes. He's talked to patient about what they actually want to hear and then traveled around presenting his findings to doctors via the magic of PowerPoint presentation.</p><p begin="00:48:56.36" data-timestamp="2936.36">His video walks doctors through the process of telling their patient something went wrong, baby step by baby step.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Dr. Albert Wu</h4><p begin="00:49:02.12" data-timestamp="2942.12">Tips for disclosing. Before beginning, make sure the patient or family member is comfortable. Try not to put a desk or other barrier between you and the patient. Give the patient the opportunity to talk and ask questions about the situation. Do not ignore or dismiss strong emotions, even if you would prefer to do so.</p><p begin="00:49:24.66" data-timestamp="2964.66">If the patient is enraged acknowledge this by saying, I can understand why you are angry with me.</p><p begin="00:49:32.18" data-timestamp="2972.18">In the case of personal responsibility, express personal regret and make a sincere apology. You should say, I am sorry that I did this.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:49:42.22" data-timestamp="2982.22">And that's that. There's no complicated formula or magic combination of phrases. It literally comes down to teaching doctors how to say the actual words, &quot;I'm sorry.&quot;</p><p begin="00:49:52.63" data-timestamp="2992.63">Dr. Wu tells me that when he first started out, doctors didn't even have to tell their patients when they did something wrong, let alone apologize for it. A nurse was there with him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Dr. Albert Wu</h4><p begin="00:50:09.17" data-timestamp="3009.17">And the person at the head of the bed said, give this. What she intended me to do, I think, was for her to get maybe a milligram. Or maybe two. But not 10.</p><p begin="00:50:37.69" data-timestamp="3037.69">She had been breathing very rapidly [HEAVY BREATHING] and it was about as clean a cause and effect relationship as one could ever see. I gave her an overdose of morphine and she stopped breathing.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:50:52.20" data-timestamp="3052.2">The woman was rushed off to an emergency room and was ultimately OK. And I just sort of stood there with sort of that tingly feeling that starts in your spine and goes up to your neck. It really is a little bit like the walls are closing in on you. Unfortunately, this head nurse was standing in the corner with her arms folded and she looked at me and said, &quot;that was a lot of morphine.&quot; And we talked a little bit about what seemed to have happened and what might have been done differently. And then, in the end, she said, well, &quot;I don't think that anyone really needs to hear about this.&quot; And that was it.</p><p begin="00:51:58.97" data-timestamp="3118.97">So as far as I'm aware, the patient's attending physician never heard about what had happened, the patient certainly never heard what happened. You know exactly what I'm talking about.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:54:32.38" data-timestamp="3272.38">In the years since Roxanne's brother died, she has sat on panels with doctors and policymakers talking about the need to be honest with patients and admit their errors. And she's met with doctors who have been trained in this whole new way of apologizing. And she's glad for the effort, but says that a real apology isn't only about the words.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:54:48.83" data-timestamp="3288.83">The response that I get now when people find out, especially from health care that I lost a brother through medical error, they are in a hurry to say, I'm very sorry for your loss. But you can see it's that they've been taught that phrase. That that's the phrase they should use.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:55:16.33" data-timestamp="3316.33">How can you tell?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roxanne</h4><p begin="00:55:19.61" data-timestamp="3319.61">It's like the rush they need to get it out to me and this is the phrase that is used. And eventually, it's going to mean nothing to them because it won't be real anymore. It will be a programed response.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Starlee Kine</h4><p begin="00:55:53.81" data-timestamp="3353.81">And then there are some doctors who are simply never going to change.

  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.37" data-timestamp="0.37"> I was a reporter for years in the Chicago Public schools, covering all the different ways that people tried to fix and improve those schools. And one of the big debates-- not just among the parents and politicians, but inside the schools as well-- was how much could we expect teachers to actually accomplish? Like, how much could they do, really, with the students they were given?</p><p begin="00:00:24.04" data-timestamp="24.04">Some teachers definitely were the heroic we-can-do-better types. We need to try everything. They were trying all kinds of reforms and fixes to make their schools improve. Others shrugged.</p><p begin="00:00:35.70" data-timestamp="35.7">This population of kids, they would tell you, arrives with such deficits-- no books at home, violent neighborhoods, the kind of family problems that would distract anybody from school. These teachers would say, we're doing as well with them as anybody could. You know, if dozens of kids show up in your classroom each year with terrible reading ability and in need of remedial writing instruction, how exactly are you supposed to bring them up to grade level on the state standardized tests in the one year you have with them?</p><p begin="00:01:03.83" data-timestamp="63.83">One of the things that's been interesting about the teacher's strike in the Chicago schools is that it's actually gotten people talking about some very basic things in education, and things like these. Like for instance, one of the big issues is standardized testing. How much should teachers be evaluated on the test scores of their students? Where you stand on that one pretty much comes down to whether you think that teachers have a lot of power to raise those scores or not.</p><p begin="00:01:31.15" data-timestamp="91.15">There's an essay that's being published this Sunday in the <i>New</i> <i>York</i> <i>Times</i> by Alex Kotlowitz, a reporter who writes a lot about kids and poverty in Chicago. And he points out how much we expect from teachers, especially teachers in a school system like Chicago's where 87% of the students come from low income families. He writes that he's been reporting in a high school lately where the staff has transformed and improved the school in lots of ways.</p><p begin="00:01:55.63" data-timestamp="115.63">"And yet each day I spend there," he writes, "I witness one heartbreaking scene after another. A young girl who yells at one of the school's social workers, 'This is no way to live,' and then breaks down in tears. Because of problems at home, she's had to move in with a friend's family, and there's not enough food to go around. A young man has retreated into his shell having witnessed a murder over the summer.</p><p begin="00:02:15.93" data-timestamp="135.93">The stories are all too familiar. And yet somehow, we've come to believe that with really good teachers and longer school days and rigorous testing, we can transform their lives. We've imagined teachers as lazy, excuse-making quasi-professionals or, alternately, as lifesavers. But the truth, of course, is more complicated."</p><p begin="00:02:36.29" data-timestamp="156.29">I bring all this up here today because there is a new way to look at all this, a new body of science that's just starting to get discussed in a widespread way. It looks at what a difficult home life can do to the brain of a school kid-- literally to the biology of the brain-- and how it makes it difficult to learn.</p><p begin="00:02:55.82" data-timestamp="175.82">And this science suggests all kinds of things that schools could be doing that could improve teaching, get through to huge numbers of kids that teachers have a hard time getting through to today.</p><p begin="00:03:05.28" data-timestamp="185.28">As school starts up again, we thought here at the radio show that it would be nice to turn away from the political questions and the union questions and the money questions and all the regular stuff that people argue about when they argue about schools all the time and turn instead today for the beginning of school to something more optimistic and forward-looking, something that feels big and Earth-shaking and, I have to say, kind of exciting. That is what we're going to be talking about today.</p><p begin="00:03:31.61" data-timestamp="211.61">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: No These Things Will Not Be on the Final Exam</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:45.99" data-timestamp="225.99"> Act One-- No, These Things Will Not Be on the Final Exam. OK, so let's start with the most basic question, a question that is so big, it is almost embarrassing to ask out loud. What should kids be learning at school?</p><p begin="00:03:59.75" data-timestamp="239.75">You know, people argue about this. Schools are established pushing this approach or that one about the best way to teach. And I learned about this shift in the way that people are answering this question, this whole new set of theories and ideas that are in the air right now from this new book, this book that came out this month that is trying to document that shift and explain what these ideas are about.</p><p begin="00:04:20.15" data-timestamp="260.15">Some of these ideas have been around for years but are now backed up by new data and tied to research about what goes on in kids' brains. Some are new, based on new research.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:04:28.80" data-timestamp="268.8"> There are educators involved in this, psychologists, economists, neuroscientists. They're not all working together. But they're starting to make connections. And I feel like they're coming to a similar conclusion.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:40.99" data-timestamp="280.99"> This is Paul Tough. He's a reporter who writes about new approaches to educating children. He's been on our program now and then in the past. And he's the one who wrote this new book.</p><p begin="00:04:49.99" data-timestamp="289.99">He says that to understand this shift in thinking, you have to first understand where things were before the shift. Up until now, what's been emphasised in most schools is something called-- and jargon word here, but there's no way around this-- something they call cognitive development.</p><p begin="00:05:04.95" data-timestamp="304.95">And what "cognitive development" means is smarts the way that you think about smarts in school-- the stuff that gets measured on IQ tests and standardized math and reading achievement tests that kids take most places. And it's not like that stuff isn't important.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:05:18.91" data-timestamp="318.91"> Well, I'm definitely not saying that none of that stuff matters at all. I'm saying that we have put way too much emphasis on it, and especially in the early years. I think it's especially parents of very young kids who get really anxious that their kids need all of these skills and all of this information really early on. I think--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:35.68" data-timestamp="335.68">There's a whole industry that came out of that.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:05:37.38" data-timestamp="337.38">Exactly. I think it's part of these preschool classes, Junior Kumon, all of these flash cards and brain building toys and the right kind of videos and DVDs from early on to try to boost their cognitive skills at an earlier and earlier age.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:58.60" data-timestamp="358.6">So that's the old way of looking at this. What's our new understanding?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:06:03.84" data-timestamp="363.84">Where I think they're heading is this idea that what matters most in a child's success is not just cognitive skill. It's not just reading and math and IQ. It is this very different set of qualities.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:17.02" data-timestamp="377.02">And you write that the guy who's kind of at the heart of this way of thinking is an economist named Heckman, right?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:06:23.14" data-timestamp="383.14">Yeah, James Heckman. He's a Nobel Prize winning economist at the University of Chicago. If there's any one figure who is trying to pull together all of this research, it's him. He puts together these conferences where he invites psychologists and neuroscientists and economists, people who don't usually have much connection, to get together and talk about some of these ideas and some of these issues.</p><p begin="00:06:44.36" data-timestamp="404.36">The thing that really got Heckman started down this path was a study that he did about the GED.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:51.07" data-timestamp="411.07">The GED, of course, is the high school equivalency exam. If you didn't graduate from high school, you can take the GED. And if you pass, it counts the same as if you graduated.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:06:59.53" data-timestamp="419.53"> Well, I'll give you-- it's a personal anecdote. This was in the early 1990s.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:05.32" data-timestamp="425.32">When I invited Heckman into the studio to chat, he told me how blown away he was when he first encountered the GED. It was the early 1990s, like he said. He was in Texas doing a study about something else.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:07:15.71" data-timestamp="435.71">And they pointed to this classroom and said, "You realize that these people will be studying for their GEDs. And in a few weeks, maybe a few months, you can convert essentially high school dropouts, people who were even intermediate school dropouts, into high school graduates." And I said, "That's amazing."</p><p begin="00:07:37.56" data-timestamp="457.56">So I'm an economist. And that really started to surprise me. And as a result--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:44.15" data-timestamp="464.15">Wait, why was that surprising if what they were testing was just you've learned this information?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:07:49.30" data-timestamp="469.3">Well, it was surprising, you know, in a few months, you could actually teach people the knowledge that they would acquire in four years of high school.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:58.15" data-timestamp="478.15">Oh, I see.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:07:58.61" data-timestamp="478.61">Maybe a couple more. There are lots of things that kids are learning in this class. But in some ways, when I would sit in the One Goal classroom, it felt like this kind of ongoing group therapy session. I don't think they would want me to say that, because it sounds too--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:47:19.29" data-timestamp="2839.29">Touchy feely.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:47:19.71" data-timestamp="2839.71">Touchy feely. Yeah. But really, that is what makes a huge difference for these kids is learning these psychological skills. And when they get to college, that's what's going to make a difference.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Michelle Steffel</h4><p begin="00:47:30.67" data-timestamp="2850.67"> It's OK to be scared. It's OK to kind of stumble and fall along the way. And you should. It's not going to be easy. It shouldn't be easy. This is your life, OK? It's not going to be a bed of roses. OK?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:47:43.54" data-timestamp="2863.54"> One Goal's premise is it's going to be difficult to make up for how far behind these kids are in their cognitive skills, in traditional test scores and all that. When Kewauna first took a practice ACT test for college, she scored an 11, putting her in the bottom one percentile of everyone who took the ACT that year. After months of study, she said she went up one point.</p><p begin="00:48:05.10" data-timestamp="2885.1">But One Goal could teach her non-cognitive skills-- how to be resourceful, how to rebound from a setback, how to control herself and not get into fights-- plus concrete skills that would be useful, like how to present yourself, how to network. Kewauna never used to ask questions in class. She thought her questions were dumb. So she had to learn how to do that.</p><p begin="00:48:22.75" data-timestamp="2902.75">She ended up with an ACT score of 15, which, combined with a 4.1 grade point average in her junior and senior years, got her into college-- Western Illinois University in Macomb. She was a freshman last year.</p><p begin="00:48:34.12" data-timestamp="2914.12">Paul Tough has stayed in touch with her. And he says her training paid off. In fact, he says it's hard to imagine how she would have gotten to college and made it through freshman year without that training. Non-cognitive traits like grit and self-control are even more important in college than in high school.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:48:47.36" data-timestamp="2927.36">And then the other thing that I think has made a huge difference for her at college especially is this resourcefulness, is this sense that she has of like, "I'm starting from behind. I need to do everything. I need to get everything on this campus working for me," which I think is not a way that most kids think when they arrive on campus, and definitely not kids from the South Side of Chicago.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Kewauna Lerma</h4><p begin="00:49:09.09" data-timestamp="2949.09">The first thing I did when I first got to every class, I introduced myself. As long as they know who you are.</p><p begin="00:49:36.66" data-timestamp="2976.66">And I always, like after class or before class, I introduce myself. Ask them about their office hours. The United States used to have a higher percentage of its young people graduate from college than any other country. Now, we're not even close.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:50:23.83" data-timestamp="3023.83">We're still doing pretty decently in terms of the number-- the percentage of kids, the percentage of say 18-year-olds, who go to college. The problem is--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:50:32.52" data-timestamp="3032.52">Who enroll.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:50:33.24" data-timestamp="3033.24">Who enroll, right. Who just show up there on the first day of school. But we also have the highest dropout rate in the world.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:50:41.20" data-timestamp="3041.2">I'm just going to pause because that seems worth repeating. The United States has the highest college dropout rate in the industrialized world.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Paul Tough</h4><p begin="00:50:49.49" data-timestamp="3049.49">It's an issue that we haven't, even in education policy circles, we haven't paid any attention to before very recently. Nobody was really focusing on it. We're very focused on getting kids to college.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:51:02.08" data-timestamp="3062.08">Of 128 students who signed up for One Goal at the same time as Kewauna, 85% of them have made it through their freshman year and are now back in school this fall as sophomores. Of course, they're still not done. Nationally, only about 58% of kids who start college end up graduating in six years or fewer.</p><p begin="00:51:18.70" data-timestamp="3078.7">But remember, Kewauna and these other kids in One Goal are average Chicago public high school students. They're not a select group. So the 85% is especially impressive. A University of Chicago study a few years ago showed that only 8% of high school freshman in Chicago will get a four year degree from college-- 8%.</p><p begin="00:51:38.34" data-timestamp="3098.34">And of course, this is the hope that Heckman and other educators and researchers have for these non-cognitive skills, that programs like One Goal can teach them to kids like Kewauna, making those kids capable of seizing a better future for themselves.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:51:49.45" data-timestamp="3109.45">And this, I think, just changes the way we think about poverty and human opportunity and what can we do to open up more possibilities to more people.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:51:59.70" data-timestamp="3119.7">When James Heckman talks about this stuff-- you know, economists can be such a sober, cautious bunch-- he can sound positively Utopian.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:52:07.10" data-timestamp="3127.1">I think there's just a general question, which this whole research is about, which is, what are the determinants of human success? How fixed are these determinants? How much can you change them? How much can you bolster them? How much social policy can actually influence those? To me, that's extraordinarily interesting.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:52:27.27" data-timestamp="3147.27">It's so interesting when you talk about this, because I feel like there's this vision of human perfectibility. Like, oh, our goal is we're going to make people so they'll be more successful and happy. Most of us don't even think about that as a possibility, because that goal is so big.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:52:42.30" data-timestamp="3162.3">Well, but I think one of the reasons why people don't think it's a possibility is they think these traits are fixed at birth or fixed so early there's not much we can do about it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:52:50.41" data-timestamp="3170.41">Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>James Heckman</h4><p begin="00:52:50.72" data-timestamp="3170.72">And I think what we've learned is that these human capabilities can be shaped. And as an economist, what I like about it is that it has this possibility of reducing inequality, but not doing it through the standard mechanism of just handing out money and transfers from the rich to the poor. That's ancient.</p><p begin="00:53:10.25" data-timestamp="3190.25">The idea is you make the poor highly capable. That there really is a possibility of giving people more possibilities. That there really is the chance of improving their capabilities.</p><p begin="00:53:20.38" data-timestamp="3200.38">And I'm personally very excited by that. And a lot of the evidence comes together, whether it's neuroscience, psychology, economics. It's the confluence of these things. There are these happy times in science and social science and knowledge where different strands come together. And I think we're at such a time.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:45.76" data-timestamp="3225.76">James Heckman.

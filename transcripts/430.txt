  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.61" data-timestamp="0.61">Lindsey Dills is 24. She's from a place in southern Georgia called Glynn County, right on the coast. The nearest big town is a partly gentrified, mostly impoverished city called Brunswick. Population 16,000. Though I interviewed her in a prison way upstate.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:00:15.34" data-timestamp="15.34">OK I'm Lindsey Dills. This is Lee Arrendale State Prison. Before all this I was-- I actually was all-state for soccer five years in a row. I was expected to have a scholarship to college. A couple scouts had contacted my dad, my coach at my school.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:31.74" data-timestamp="31.74">But in her sophomore year of high school, Lindsey started smoking pot. She began working at this bar and restaurant, Spanky's, as a waitress, ran with an older crowd that she met at the restaurant. By senior year she was doing coke in a pretty serious way. She had a 21-year-old boyfriend, which at the time she thought was the coolest thing ever, though now she sees that one differently. Finally, senior year, she quit soccer, which of course, her dad and every other adult in her life tried to talk her out of.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:00:56.76" data-timestamp="56.76">The coaches were calling the house, telling my dad, &quot;Will you please talk to her?&quot; And probably part of it was part of the drugs, and I think part of it was that I just had done it for so long and I wanted to be-- I wanted to be a teenager. I wanted to go do stuff on the weekends, and I wanted to not have practice at 5 o'clock in the morning. And I didn't want to have to run three miles every other day.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:16.88" data-timestamp="76.88">And she says, in retrospect, she knows she wasn't coping well. Her home life had been pretty rough when she was little, before and after her parents split up. And now she was self-mutilating, cutting herself with a razor, smoking a lot of pot. And her parents tried to do the right thing and get her treatment for all that. She went into a program for a week or so, more than once. But here's where things took an unusual turn for Lindsey. October 2004, she was 17, her dad was out of town. And she forged two checks, one for $40, one for $60, on her dad's checking account. She cashed them at her job, spent some of the money on drugs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:01:49.29" data-timestamp="109.29">Well, he comes back from out of town and he does his balance his checkbook. He's a CPA, so he's on it. As soon as he gets home he knows that something's not adding up. And I don't-- I don't in a million years blame my dad for doing what he did. He was terrified. All he'd heard was horror stories about what happens to people when they were doing drugs. He didn't mess with drugs. My dad doesn't drink. In the 24 years I've been alive, I've never seen my dad drink alcohol. I never heard my dad curse.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:13.46" data-timestamp="133.46">She says he started randomly taking her to be drug tested. And told her that if she failed a drug test, then he'd go to the police about the two forged checks. Lindsey figured out ways to pass the test-- get somebody else's pee, or dilute her own. But finally she fails a test. And her dad followed through on his threat.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:02:29.90" data-timestamp="149.9">Two police officers come into my job and they're like, &quot;We're looking for Lindsey Dills.&quot; I was like, &quot;Let me get her for you.&quot; And I'm about to just leave. So I know exactly what's going on at this point when they say, &quot;We're looking for Lindsey Dills.&quot; I was like, &quot;Oh my god, he actually did it.&quot; I never thought he would do it.</p><p begin="00:02:45.71" data-timestamp="165.71">So I am arrested at this point. They slam me over the hostess stand. I'm at the front of restaurant. People are turning around. I'm mortified. I'm, like, 18. And everybody's just like, &quot;Oh [BLEEP].&quot; Because they know how my dad is. My dad used to call Spanky's to see if I had left work. He's be like, &quot;Is Lindsey there?&quot; Because I would be like, &quot;Oh, I'm working late.&quot;</p><p begin="00:03:04.55" data-timestamp="184.55">The sheriff's in there. He's one of the regulars at the bar. And the sheriff's standing there Like, &quot;Oh my god.&quot;</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:09.84" data-timestamp="189.84">Because you know the sheriff, because you've waited on him.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:03:11.97" data-timestamp="191.97">Right, I know the sheriff. And then there's a bond salesman there that, he's a regular. Him and the sheriff are real good friends. And so he finds out what my bond is, and he's going to bond me out, because he's familiar with me. Well, my dad calls him. Because I'm like-- my dad's like, &quot;I'm not going to bond you out.&quot; I was like, &quot;Well, I don't need you to bond me out. So and so's bonding me out.&quot; It's the worst thing I could have ever said. So my dad calls this man and begs him not to bond me out. He's like, &quot;Please don't. I'm trying to save her life.&quot;</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:37.74" data-timestamp="217.74">Her dad declined to be interviewed for the radio. But Lindsey's mom, Vikki Woodard, confirmed that he was hoping that this would be the intervention that would change everything. She had moved from Georgia to South Carolina a month before Lindsey forged the two checks and kept in touch by phone with what was happening. She says her thinking was, Lindsey had just turned 18. It was their last chance. After this--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vikki Woodard</h4><p begin="00:03:57.59" data-timestamp="237.59">She could do whatever she wanted. She could drop out of school. She could move out. We would have no influence over her whatsoever. And she was in a fast spiral down at that point. She was not going to school. Her father would call me and say, &quot;I can't get her out of the bed in the morning. She just won't get up.&quot; And so he rolled the dice.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:16.28" data-timestamp="256.28">He rolled the dice hoping that she would agree to a drug treatment program that he found, out of town, away from her friends and everybody who was influencing her to do drugs, and that she'd go there in exchange for dropping the charges. But Lindsey didn't want to go out of town to some drug treatment program that her daddy picked. And then the prosecutor put another option on the table. Drug court.</p><p begin="00:04:37.10" data-timestamp="277.1">Drug courts are all over the country. You've probably heard of them. They've been around since 1989 and they are a huge national success story. The idea is, you take nonviolent offenders whose crimes are caused by their drug addiction. And instead of prison time, you give them court supervised drug treatment. This accomplishes two things. It saves money, because fewer people are incarcerated. And studies show it actually helps people. It gets them off drugs, which means fewer repeat offenders.</p><p begin="00:05:02.28" data-timestamp="302.28">But if you enter a drug court program, it's serious business. Especially the drug court that happened to be in Lindsey's small town. Which is run by a judge that many people truly fear. The Chief Judge at the Glynn County Superior Court, Amanda Williams.</p><p begin="00:05:15.96" data-timestamp="315.96">The basics of the Glynn County drug court program are about as tough as they get. It takes two years to complete. And at the beginning, you're going to five meetings a week with drug court counselors, plus four AA or NA meetings a week, plus curfews and drug tests every week. If you finish the whole program, your record is wiped clean, but if you mess up and flunk out, you have a felony conviction on the original charge on your record and you serve the time for that conviction. So there's a real threat hanging over your head.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:05:43.04" data-timestamp="343.04">So Jason Clarke is my public defender at the time, and he's warning me. He's saying, &quot;Lindsey don't take drug court. I'm telling you, you won't make it.&quot; He's told me this like three or four times. I'm adamant. I'm saying, &quot;I'm taking drug court.&quot; Because all I know is I get to get out of jail. It's all I know. I don't know anything else about it. So I go to plead into drug court. My dad's in the courtroom. Judge Barton was on the bench that day instead of Judge Williams. She was gone. My dad's done Judge Barton's accounting for probably the past 15 years and they're very good friends. So he sees me come in and he pulls me up to the bench and he's like, &quot;Don't do this. Don't take drug court. Are you sure you want to do this?&quot; He asked me three or four times.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:19.57" data-timestamp="379.57">Judge Barton says this?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lindsey Dills</h4><p begin="00:06:20.43" data-timestamp="380.43">Judge Barton does this. Judge Barton's trying to talk me out of taking drug court, that I would not do well. I wouldn't make it. He didn't think it was what I should do.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vikki Woodard</h4><p begin="00:06:28.00" data-timestamp="388">And she was spiteful that day.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:30.85" data-timestamp="390.85">Again, Lindsey's mom, Vikki.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Vikki Woodard</h4><p begin="00:06:32.70" data-timestamp="392.7">She was like, &quot;You--&quot; And Lindsey's just got a little bit too much of her dad and I in her. She's very independent. She's like, &quot;You're not going to tell me what to do. You want me to do this? I'm doing this other thing.&quot; She clearly looked at her dad.

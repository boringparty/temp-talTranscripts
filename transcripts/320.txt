    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:01.84" data-timestamp="1.84"> OK, here's a simple way to measure how violent things have gotten in Iraq. Calling around this week to five English-speaking Iraqis. Every one of them knew somebody who's died in the violence since the US invasion. A doctor in Baghdad, who's active in politics, told me he knows over 300 people who've died. A guy in Babylon says it's pretty safe where he lives, but his cousin was shot by Americans when he got too close to them on the road. A medical student's cousins died from an IED exploding in the street. And he has friends who died too.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 1</h4><p begin="00:00:29.73" data-timestamp="29.73"> One of them was kidnapped. No one knows why. Ali, a bookstore owner in Baghdad that I talked to picked up a list of people he knows who've died. Friends, relatives neighbors.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 2</h4><p begin="00:01:02.77" data-timestamp="62.77"> The situation, no matter how much I don't talk about, it cannot described how it is.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:08.29" data-timestamp="68.29"> How often will you hear that a friend or a friend of a friend or a relative has died? Is it once a week?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 2</h4><p begin="00:01:15.44" data-timestamp="75.44"> Every day, believe me.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:17.60" data-timestamp="77.6"> No. Every day?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 2</h4><p begin="00:01:19.10" data-timestamp="79.1"> I swear. Just two hours ago a neighbor has been killed in our neighborhood. He was shot dead.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:28.93" data-timestamp="88.93"> Shot dead. How, why, where was he?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 2</h4><p begin="00:01:32.79" data-timestamp="92.79"> He was with his wife in his car, and some gunmen pulled over and just shot him and gunned him down because he's a Shiite.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:46.70" data-timestamp="106.7"> That's horrible.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 2</h4><p begin="00:01:48.11" data-timestamp="108.11"> I saw three similar incidents-- getting a man killed in front of his wife and two children. The oldest girl is about five years of age. And she was shouting, oh, daddy, please daddy, don't die. In front of my eyes. I don't know these people, but this happened in front of my eyes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Man 1</h4><p begin="00:02:08.81" data-timestamp="128.81"> It's like you're getting used to it. You hear people die. OK, people die.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:18.07" data-timestamp="138.07"> It's at the point now where if you go up to a dead body and try to clear it away, you're in danger of getting shot yourself. It's at the point now where Iraqis are getting their names, addresses, and phone numbers tattooed on their bodies. So if they're killed, their bodies will be identified and returned to their families.</p><p begin="00:02:34.20" data-timestamp="154.2">People I talked to said they're scared all the time. Everybody said that it was better when Saddam was in power. Not that they like Saddam. Mohaned, an architect, described the choice between Saddam and the current situation in this way.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mohaned</h4><p begin="00:02:47.32" data-timestamp="167.32"> They're two bad choices, I must say. Saddam's staying in power is not a solution.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:54.82" data-timestamp="174.82"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mohaned</h4><p begin="00:02:55.63" data-timestamp="175.63"> And there was no light at the end of the tunnel, so to speak. But today there is no tunnel.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:06.80" data-timestamp="186.8"> Seems like every day we hear these stories and numbers from Iraq. Car bombings, more deaths, sectarian killings. But today on our radio show, we try to understand what you would think would be one of the simplest facts about the war, once and for all.</p><p begin="00:03:18.74" data-timestamp="198.74">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Our program in three acts today. Now, in the second half of the show we have a very unusual recording of US forces trying to make things right with Iraqis, after the US accidentally killed 12 civilians. In the other acts of the show we talk about a number, and in one of the acts a small news story that nobody ever really seemed to take very seriously at the time. And what was really behind that story. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Truth, Damn Truth And Statistics</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:50.04" data-timestamp="230.04"> Act One. Truths, Damn Truths, and Statistics. Well, the number that we're going to talk about right now-- and we are going to talk about a number-- the number is how many civilians have died as a result of a war. Fact is, nobody is even pretending to have an accurate count. Iraqi Ministry of Health for a while, early on in the war, was compiling morgue figures from across the country and making them public each week. But that practice was stopped. There's also a privately run nonprofit web site called iraqbodycount.net, which keeps a running tally of confirmed deaths in Iraq. That's as opposed to an estimate of all the deaths in the country.</p><p begin="00:04:28.44" data-timestamp="268.44">And there have been two studies published in the British medical journal, <i>The</i> <i>Lancet</i>. One of the studies came out just a few weeks ago. The other came out two years ago. And both of these studies arrived at casualty numbers that are so high, they shocked many people.</p><p begin="00:04:40.95" data-timestamp="280.95">Right now, in this first act, we're going to return to a story that one of our producers, Alex Blumberg, did for our show a year ago about that first <i>Lancet</i> study. Later in the show, in act three, we're going to look at the study that just came out. Anyway, here's Alex.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:04:53.93" data-timestamp="293.93"> Everyone will tell you counting casualties in wartime is hard. First of all, you need to do something called a large-scale mortality survey. And second of all, you need to do it in the middle of a war zone.</p><p begin="00:05:04.63" data-timestamp="304.63">The first attempt ever made in Iraq was this Johns Hopkins University study published in <i>The</i> <i>Lancet</i>, a British Medical Journal, in late October 2004. A couple of days before the US presidential election. It concluded that probably a 100,000 Iraqis died as a result of the war. This figure was astonishingly high. 10 times higher than any other casualty estimates at the time.</p><p begin="00:05:26.07" data-timestamp="326.07">Because of this, and because the study itself got almost no traction in the press, I remember thinking at the time it came out that it was probably bogus and slanted. I'm guessing a lot of people, if they heard about the study at all, felt the way I did.</p><p begin="00:05:37.70" data-timestamp="337.7">But recently, in trying to find out how many Iraqis have died in the war, I've learned more about the <i>Lancet</i> study. And the more I learned about it, and the remarkable story of how it was done, the more likely it seems that the 100,000 figure is actually the best estimate and possibly low.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Les Roberts</h4><p begin="00:05:52.98" data-timestamp="352.98"> Before the Iraq study, the main thing I was known for-- and that I'd testified in front of Congress for-- was documenting how many people had died in the war in the Congo.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:06:03.32" data-timestamp="363.32"> This is Les Roberts, the lead author on the Lancet study. And one of a handful of scientists in the world who could be called an expert in counting war dead.</p><p begin="00:06:10.71" data-timestamp="370.71">In the Congo study he found that 1.7 million civilians had died from the war, a figure cited by Colin Powell when he was Secretary of State and Tony Blair on the floor of the British Parliament. Les has also done studies in Burundi, Rwanda and Sierra Leone.</p><p begin="00:06:23.46" data-timestamp="383.46">To a guy in Les Robert's line of work, the war in Iraq had a number of unique and interesting things that deserve study. The main thing that distinguished this war was that the military took unprecedented care to avoid civilian casualties. Almost 2/3 of the bombs dropped were precision guided, as compared to just 8% in the first Gulf War and 0% in World War II. They limited daytime strikes and avoided civilian infrastructure like power and sewer plants. Compare that to World War II, where American forces firebombed entire cities as part of the military strategy, killing up to 100,000 people in Tokyo alone. And upwards of half a million civilians in Europe.</p><p begin="00:06:54.23" data-timestamp="414.23">And you can see why George Bush called the Iraq War one of the most quote, &quot;humane military campaigns in history.&quot; The lesson here that often it's not bombs and bullets that kill people in war, it's the other things that happen when society falls apart. Clean water and medical supplies get scarce. In a lot of the studies he did in Africa, diarrhea killed more people than weapons did. Women can't get to the hospital to deliver their babies so infant mortality rates go up as well.</p><p begin="00:07:17.35" data-timestamp="437.35">It took Les a long time to get to Iraq and see if the same things were happening there. First, an Iraqi doctor who he'd planned to work with, died in an auto accident. Another social ill, by the way, that tends to increase during wartime. And then insurgent violence spiked.</p><p begin="00:07:30.88" data-timestamp="450.88">It wasn't until August of 2004, five months after he'd originally planned to go, that he finally landed in Amman, Jordan. And he called various statisticians and epidemiologists who looked at study.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:51:38.01" data-timestamp="3098.01"> Well, if anything, the methodology is sounder in this study than it was in the last study. A lot of the critiques that were leveled at the last study could not be leveled at this study.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:51:46.92" data-timestamp="3106.92"> For instance?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:51:48.00" data-timestamp="3108"> For instance, one of the biggest critiques of the last study was that it had a very wide confidence interval. And the first study, they went to only 988 households.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:52:08.10" data-timestamp="3128.1"> So the critiques that you've seen of this study, are there criticisms of this study or questions about this study that seem particularly compelling though?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:52:15.33" data-timestamp="3135.33"> Everybody I talk to said that if you're doing this in a lab, you would do it differently. But almost everybody agreed that given that they were doing this in a war zone, the methodology was as sound as it could possibly be. And it's hard to imagine that any sort of bias that crept into the methodology could have accounted for a several hundred thousand person overcount.</p><p begin="00:52:36.50" data-timestamp="3156.5">The fact of the number is so shocking to people who know about this stuff, that that's been the basis of criticism. And from groups that you would think would be sort of aligned with the <i>Lancet's</i> results. In other words, this group called Iraq Body Count. This is a group that's basically trying to do the same thing that these researchers are doing.</p><p begin="00:52:56.71" data-timestamp="3176.71">Since the beginning of the war, they've been documenting every news report of where civilians are reported killed. So they came out with a detailed response on their website, and it's very compelling to look at.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:53:52.22" data-timestamp="3232.22"> For instance.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:53:53.16" data-timestamp="3233.16"> So one of the things that they do is they dig into the data. With less than a tenth of them being noticed by any public surveillance mechanisms.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:54:11.91" data-timestamp="3251.91"> They're not showing up in the morgue. And they conclude that conservatively, if there are 600,000 people being killed by violence, there should be at least 800,000 people seriously wounded. But if you look at the hospital records all across Iraq, you don't see 800,000 people as having been treated for shrapnel wounds and legs getting blown off and all the things that you would expect to find for this level of violence.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:55:50.07" data-timestamp="3350.07"> And did anybody who you talked to have any explanation for how these numbers could be so high?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:55:56.58" data-timestamp="3356.58"> Well, there's two possibilities. Either people lied to them and told them that people in their household had died, and then somehow fabricated death certificates to support that. That seems impossible. Said that people died in these households when, in fact, they hadn't.</p><p begin="00:56:43.66" data-timestamp="3403.66">I don't think that happened.

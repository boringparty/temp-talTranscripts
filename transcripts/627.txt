  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.68" data-timestamp="0.68"> There's a story my mom used to like to tell. My older sister Randi was eight or so when this happened. Something came up at school that Randi didn't understand, and she came home and she asked my mom, where do babies come from? And my mom just felt like, great. This is great. She's bringing this up on her own. I don't have to sit her down in some awkward birds and bees conversation someday. We can get this out of the way now, when she's young. And she's really excited to tell her this stuff.</p><p begin="00:00:26.56" data-timestamp="26.56">And she explains the whole thing, where babies come from, what sex is. And the way my mom always used to tell this story, she said that she felt like, I am doing such a good job here. I am such a good mom today. And at the end of it, she asked Randi, OK, so that's everything. Do you have any questions? And then Randi pauses for a long moment and she asks, can birds really fly?</p><p begin="00:00:53.88" data-timestamp="53.88">And my mom realizes, of course, oh, she was not ready. She absorbed nothing. And sure enough, like a year passes and Randi comes back to her-- I don't remember why-- and asks her again, where do babies come from? And it's like the first time never happened. She remembered none of it.</p><p begin="00:01:10.34" data-timestamp="70.34">And I think of that story sometimes when there are debates over what is suitable for children. An I think there's a whole category of things that kids protect themselves from, like they just don't want to engage with that stuff. Adults don't need to keep them from it. But then there's other stuff, stuff that does seep into their world that they take to heart and they mull about and they make part of themselves. And the thing that's difficult is that it can be really hard to predict what those things are going to be.</p><p begin="00:01:42.02" data-timestamp="102.02">Today on our program, things that kids hear, things they see, things that happen to them that affect them in ways that they only understand later as adults. Like for instance, what it means to give little black kids toys that celebrate the Confederacy, or to drag teenage boys into the desert to watch atomic bombs go off, or to send kids who should be in elementary school over the border into the United States alone. How do you even quantify exactly what that does to a person? Well, today, we try to figure that out.</p><p begin="00:02:11.73" data-timestamp="131.73">From WBEZ Chicago, is <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>

  <h3>Act One: Stars & Bars & Bars</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:24.91" data-timestamp="144.91"> Act One, Stars &amp; Bars &amp; Bars.</p><p begin="00:02:29.64" data-timestamp="149.64">We got the idea for this week's show from this thing that happened to one of our producers, Neil Drumming, a realization that he came to in adulthood about a TV show that he'd watched as a kid. Here's Neil.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:02:40.32" data-timestamp="160.32"> The hit TV show <i>The</i> <i>Dukes</i> <i>of</i> <i>Hazzard</i> ran from 1979 to 1985. It starred John Schneider and Tom Wopat as Bo and Luke Duke, to rakish good old boys trying to stay on the right side of the law in rural Georgia. But if you ask any man my age who was a fan of the show, <i>The</i> <i>Dukes</i> <i>of</i> <i>Hazzard's</i> true star was a bright orange 1969 Dodge Charger nicknamed the General Lee. Every week on CBS, the General Lee would roar through country backroads, leaping over virtually everything, defying the laws of physics, and blowing little boys' minds across America.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:03:12.71" data-timestamp="192.71"> I had a General Lee.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:03:13.50" data-timestamp="193.5"> In like a Matchbox or Hot Wheels?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:03:15.44" data-timestamp="195.44"> No, I had a straight-up-- like the plastic joint.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:03:18.25" data-timestamp="198.25"> Oh, OK. Like a big-sized one.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:03:19.35" data-timestamp="199.35"> A big-sized one. You know, go around, drive on your dad's foot and he'd get angry. That size.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:03:27.05" data-timestamp="207.05"> This is one of my favorite rappers, Breeze Brewin. He's from the Bronx. Like me, he watched <i>The</i> <i>Dukes</i> <i>of</i> <i>Hazzard</i> faithfully as a boy. We both had <i>Dukes</i> <i>of</i> <i>Hazzard</i> lunchboxes. And we were both proud owners of miniature versions of the General Lee. Back then, it was probably one of the most coveted toys any kid in either of our neighborhoods could possess.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:03:45.39" data-timestamp="225.39"> I remember the bully of the neighborhood stole mine and tried to pretend it was his.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:03:48.76" data-timestamp="228.76"> I hate when that happens. This is mine, right? I'm like, no, it's definitely not.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:03:55.04" data-timestamp="235.04"> The General Lee was remarkable, not just because it was both fast and furious before that was a thing, but because its rooftop was emblazoned with the Confederate flag. As kids, black kids, pushing our mini American muscle along the carpets and concrete of New York City, neither Breeze nor I saw anything bothersome about that bold symbol up top. In fact, for us at the time, the rebel flag was just part of what made the car so damn cool.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:04:18.32" data-timestamp="258.32"> There was a kid in my neighborhood, Irish kid. It was a real car or it was a toy?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:04:36.02" data-timestamp="276.02"> No, he drove that bad boy. And you heard him coming down the block.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:04:40.53" data-timestamp="280.53"> You were just like, that car would be dope.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:04:41.85" data-timestamp="281.85"> That car would be dope if he had that flag on that bad boy.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Neil Drumming</h4><p begin="00:04:50.80" data-timestamp="290.8"> I got the idea to talk to Breeze about this because, in 2001, as part of his group the Juggaknots, he wrote and released a song called "Generally." I don't remember exactly when I heard the song, but by then it'd been over 15 years since <i>The</i> <i>Dukes</i> <i>of</i> <i>Hazzard</i> went off the air. I hadn't thought about the show or the car in ages.</p><p begin="00:05:10.59" data-timestamp="310.59">[MUSIC - "GENERALLY" BY THE JUGGAKNOTS]</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:05:21.71" data-timestamp="321.71"> I'm pretty sure that when I was in my 20s and heard "Generally," well, that was the first time I put two and two together and realized, oh wait. The flag on the roof, how the hell did I miss that?</p><p begin="00:05:36.62" data-timestamp="336.62">Listening to the song again recently, I thought maybe it was about Breeze himself coming to that same realization. He was actually inspired to write "Generally" a few years later, after he'd had kids of his own.</p><p begin="00:05:56.48" data-timestamp="356.48">One day, he took them on a shopping trip.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Breeze Brewin</h4><p begin="00:05:58.40" data-timestamp="358.4"> I was literally in Toys "R" Us, probably on Gun Hill Road, near Co-op City, or the one in Yonkers, on Central Ave, and kids losing their shh, looking for toys and me questioning, like, what's up with these toys? Staged houses and barns were splintered and scattered.</p><p begin="00:54:28.40" data-timestamp="3268.4">We never had to write reports, nor did anyone ask us what we saw because it turned out they were looking at us. They wanted to see how young men reacted to an atomic blast. Apparently, that's all they wanted to do. I was not selected because I was special. I had no need for qualifications, aside from being a 19-year-old boy.</p><p begin="00:54:57.77" data-timestamp="3297.77">Over the years of America's open-air atomic bomb testing in Nevada, a few thousand army men participated. It does not matter anymore that only feeble attempts were made by the government to find out how these experiences affected us. Lately, I've begun to realize that I am one of the last people living in America to have actually experienced close-up explosions of atomic bombs. We're all dying now, and most of us are already dead.</p><p begin="00:55:28.29" data-timestamp="3328.29">I'm not aware of anyone's health being affected by the blasts, but some years ago I did check with the Veterans Administration about the possible radiation dosage I received during my participation. I was informed that the radiation film badge that I wore throughout my four months at Desert Rock had been burned up in a government warehouse records fire in St. Louis some decades ago.</p><p begin="00:55:54.09" data-timestamp="3354.09">Now in my late years, when I can conjure that brief, surreal period of my youth, I try in vain to make some sense of it. In some ways, it has become my responsibility to at least recollect and tell how that great flash and blast permanently reached into my very young mind and heart, how those enormous sounds deadened my ears and still ring in them to this day, how the crush of shock waves sometimes buried us alive in our trenches. I feel it my duty to tell the reckless absurdity of it all.</p><p begin="00:56:35.80" data-timestamp="3395.8">In August, our president threatened to unleash fire and fury like the world has never seen. We keep threatening to unleash these bombs, and so I suspect that one day we will. Most of us have forgotten what we are capable of.

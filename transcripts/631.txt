  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.86" data-timestamp="0.86"> So a horse walks into a movie theater, gets his popcorn and a Diet Coke, and sits down in one of the few seats that are left. He realizes right away there's a cow sitting directly in front of him wearing this huge hat, totally inappropriate to wear indoors, one of the big, tall Stetson hats, completely blocking his view. So the horse taps the cow on the shoulder. Excuse me?</p><p begin="00:00:21.72" data-timestamp="21.72">Cow doesn't budge. No response at all. So again, the horse taps him on the shoulder. Excuse me? The cow is like ice. He's like a statue. So OK, one more time. He taps him on the shoulder. Hey, can you take off your hat? Cow turns around. And he's like, oh, my god, it's a talking horse.</p><p begin="00:00:46.15" data-timestamp="46.15">Not the greatest joke in the world. But what I love about that joke is that for once in the joke, the animals acknowledge that we should not be having animals talking in jokes. That's the actual punchline of the joke. That's the premise.</p><p begin="00:00:58.36" data-timestamp="58.36">We as human beings are so constantly blurring the line between animals and people in kids' stories, and movies, and the way we talk to our pets. We can't help ourselves. And we do it so much we even have jokes where the animals call us out on it.</p><p begin="00:01:13.12" data-timestamp="73.12">And I bring this up because that's our show today. Today in our program, we have two stories of human beings not just trying to blur the line between people and animals but trying to erase that line completely. From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us, people.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Monkey in the Middle</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:37.52" data-timestamp="97.52"> Act One, Monkey in the Middle. So there's some places where animals almost never go, places that are designed by humans for humans. This act ends up in a place like that, but it starts about as far from there as you can get. Dana Chivvis explains.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:01:55.14" data-timestamp="115.14"> Our story begins deep in the rainforests of Indonesia on an island called Sulawesi. A few years ago, the photographer David Slater traveled there from his home in England to photograph a troop of monkeys.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:02:05.21" data-timestamp="125.21"> I'd gone a whole day just following them into the thick of the forest, through the tangles and the vines and the muddy undergrowth and the cobwebs and the poisonous snakes.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:02:16.31" data-timestamp="136.31"> Oh, man.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:02:17.00" data-timestamp="137"> You name it, I had to go through it. And it was just so amazing just to be like a monkey. I was a monkey honestly. I had met monkeys before, like I said, all over the world. And I know a little bit about monkey etiquette and showing your teeth-- not to show your teeth, not to stare them in the face. Yeah, this is one of the secrets, actually, of being able to get good photography is making the animal relaxed.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:02:42.84" data-timestamp="162.84"> There was one shot David really wanted to get-- a full face portrait with a wide angle lens. But every time he got close to taking the shot, the monkeys would look away. So after two days of these near misses, he changed strategies.</p><p begin="00:02:57.42" data-timestamp="177.42">He put his camera on a tripod and threw down some cookies to try to entice the monkeys over. He was hoping they'd get curious about the camera and start pushing the button themselves. They were curious. They were not pushing the button. So David attached a cable with a button on the end of it to the camera. The monkeys liked that. They started playing with it, putting it in their mouths, fighting over it.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:03:21.45" data-timestamp="201.45"> And of course when it got pressed, they automatically looked into the lens. And the fact that it's going ch, ch, ch, ch, ch, it's monkey language.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:03:31.68" data-timestamp="211.68"> They fired off a few shots. David reviewed them. They weren't bad, a little out of focus. So he adjusted the settings and put the camera back. The monkeys were into the whole project by now.</p><p begin="00:03:43.42" data-timestamp="223.42">In fact, they were getting a little too excited. They almost knocked the tripod over. So David laid down on his stomach and stretched his arm out to steady the tripod with two fingers. A few other monkeys noticed their strange new friend lying full out on the floor and hopped onto his back.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:03:58.92" data-timestamp="238.92"> So I had monkeys on my back whilst I'm trying to keep my camera from being knocked, while some monkeys, or two or three monkeys, are trying to play with the button. And all of a sudden, I heard the camera going off. And I didn't dare. I knew that if I looked up at them, they would probably look away. So I let them click it, click it, click it. And I sort of very carefully looked up to see them pulling these amazing contorted faces, like howling mouths, and the smiling mouth, and more besides.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:04:28.02" data-timestamp="268.02"> The monkeys were taking selfies-- amazing, beautiful, weird selfies.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:04:33.71" data-timestamp="273.71"> I just wanted it to go on forever really.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:04:40.04" data-timestamp="280.04"> When David finally stood up and checked their work, he saw that the shots weren't just regular, old, everyday selfies. They weren't that guy at the gym taking pictures of his muscles in the mirror. These were next level, transcendentally good. They somehow escape the bounds of selfie and enter the realm of art.</p><p begin="00:04:57.65" data-timestamp="297.65">There was one shot in particular that caught David's eye. The monkey's face is very close to the camera looking straight into the lens. It's hair perfectly coiffed. Eyes friendly, big and round and amber. All of which are nice details, and details are important. But the focus of this selfie, the main event, is the monkey's self-satisfied grin-- teeth that are too big for its mouth with gaps between them, a real grin, like he knows he's smiling for a picture.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:05:26.90" data-timestamp="326.9"> I just saw it on the front cover of <i>National</i> <i>Geographic</i> or something like that. And I just thought, I need to get this to my agents.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:40.83" data-timestamp="340.83"> David and his agents put out a press release about the selfies. They thought they could grab people's attention with the photos and then use the opportunity to send a message about conservation. And also they wanted to sell the pictures, of course.</p><p begin="00:05:53.01" data-timestamp="353.01">These monkeys, crested black macaques, are considered to be critically endangered, which is just one step before extinct in the wild. They've lost 80% of their population in the last 40 years because of humans, which is why David went to Indonesia in the first place. He wanted to take photographs that would inspire us humans into caring more about these little monkeys, to stop killing them. The selfies didn't end up on the cover of <i>National</i> <i>Geographic,</i> but the British tabloids were interested. The <i>Daily</i> <i>Mail</i> was the first newspaper to publish the story about the selfies. This was in 2011.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:06:29.22" data-timestamp="389.22"> Within an hour, it was all over your country, Australia, Germany. I was getting emails everywhere. My inbox went crazy. People wanting to know the story, wanted to use the image. Literally, my phone was hot with calls.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:06:46.92" data-timestamp="406.92"> Over the next few months, the monkey selfies sold pretty well. David was licensing them to publications and selling prints. And then one day, he goes online to research the monkeys.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:06:57.27" data-timestamp="417.27"> I started to just search crested black macaque one day in the Google. And there was Wikipedia, top of the list as always. I clicked on that, and there's my image. And I thought, where have they got this from? Have they been to Caters News Agency and paid a license fee for it?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:07:14.58" data-timestamp="434.58"> They had not.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:07:15.45" data-timestamp="435.45"> So I sent them a letter. Sent them a letter saying, I think you're using this without a license fee. Could you show me one, or would you pay? They wrote back, saying, this image we believe is public domain.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:07:32.46" data-timestamp="452.46"> In other words, anyone could use it without David's permission, for free. To David, that was just stealing. He makes a living from selling his pictures. So it was really helpful to have one that was such a hit. But now, anyone could download it from Wikipedia and hang it on their wall or print it in their publication. Wikipedia's opinion is that information on the internet should be free. And David soon learned that they had decided the selfie was in the public domain--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>David Slater</h4><p begin="00:07:58.58" data-timestamp="478.58"> Because the monkey pressed the button.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:08:02.69" data-timestamp="482.69"> So the monkey was technically the creator of the photo. But in Wikipedia's assessment, monkeys can't own copyrights. Hence, the photo was free and fair for all to use. It belonged to the internet. David was baffled.

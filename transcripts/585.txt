  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.57" data-timestamp="0.57">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>. I'm Ira Glass. So one of the people who works on our radio show, her brother was going through a rough time. And he called their mom, kind of panicking. And the mom was like, you know, you just need to stop thinking about this stuff. And he was like, Mom, that is not possible.</p><p begin="00:00:17.56" data-timestamp="17.56">And she was like, that is totally possible. What you have to do is you have to take the bad thought and put it into a box in your head. Then, you have to take the box out of your head, and put it in a cupboard, and shut the cupboard.</p><p begin="00:00:33.34" data-timestamp="33.34">I love the completeness of that. It's like the box in the head was not enough, right? That image maybe worked for their mom when she was young. But then she was like, I have not extinguished the bad thoughts quite enough. I need more. I need a cupboard. Next time our producer and her brother were visiting her parents, she told me, the two of them were all just like, "don't open the cupboards!"</p><p begin="00:00:59.23" data-timestamp="59.23">Denial usually gets a bad rap. Compartmentalizing your feelings, keeping it all inside, pretending things never happened-- these are not signs of psychological health. What they're signs of is adulthood. If you do those things, basically, you're an adult because being an adult means sucking it up, getting on with it, going to work even if you have a cold because you're an adult. You do your job.</p><p begin="00:01:29.16" data-timestamp="89.16">I'm not complaining. In fact, today's entire show is a defense of not thinking about it, of not knowing-- a defense, in short, of ignorance. We have three very different stories. You can decide how convincing they are. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: What You Don't Know</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:50.97" data-timestamp="110.97">Act One, What You Don't Know. Lulu Wang has this case study of people not admitting the truth.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:01:59.37" data-timestamp="119.37">When my grandmother was diagnosed with stage 4 lung cancer, she was given three months to live. She was 80 years old, and my family, for reasons I'll get into in a minute, decided not to tell her. I was totally against this.</p><p begin="00:02:14.81" data-timestamp="134.81">When I heard the news, I wanted to talk with my grandmother, comfort her. I wanted to grieve with her in the way that seems natural when someone you love is dying. But my mom-- she's the one who called me to give me the bad news-- quickly informed me that what I wanted, that what I thought was the right thing to do, would not be permitted and that I would have no say in the matter. I was stunned.</p><p begin="00:02:40.68" data-timestamp="160.68">My grandmother, or Nainai, as I call her, lives in China, along with a lot of my other relatives. My parents and I live here in the United States. I'm really close to my Nainai. At 2 years old, I lived with her and my grandfather for a year so my mom could work. Nainai is 5 foot tall with a full head of permed, white hair. She's small, but when my Nainai walks into a room, everyone listens.</p><p begin="00:03:11.59" data-timestamp="191.59">I wasn't the only one who felt weird keeping this a secret. My dad thought about going to his mom and telling her she was dying.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lulu's Dad</h4><p begin="00:03:19.81" data-timestamp="199.81">But then what? I could not take care of her because I have my own family back in the United States, right? I have my job here. And then I won't be able to take the responsibility of being with my mom.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:03:36.05" data-timestamp="216.05">It's true. My dad wasn't the one on the ground looking after Nainai. He left China when he was just a teenager. His older brother has lived in Japan for 24 years. The person who was on the ground was Nainai's younger sister.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Little Nainai</h4><p begin="00:03:50.30" data-timestamp="230.3">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:03:51.55" data-timestamp="231.55"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:03:55.81" data-timestamp="235.81">This is her, my Nainai's sister, my great aunt. She looks almost identical to Nainai. In Chinese, I call her [CHINESE], which translates to Little Aunt Grandma. But I'll just call her Little Nainai for this story.</p><p begin="00:04:12.05" data-timestamp="252.05">Little Nainai lives in the apartment right upstairs from Nainai, and they're very close. She was the one who picked up the results of Nainai's physical. It's customary in China for doctors to give bad news to family members rather than giving it directly to the patient, especially if the patient is older. Nainai's doctor recommended that Nainai be immediately hospitalized for treatment.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Little Nainai</h4><p begin="00:04:33.58" data-timestamp="273.58">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:04:34.82" data-timestamp="274.82"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:04:38.37" data-timestamp="278.37">She's saying the doctor told her the cancer was very advanced. To not hospitalize her would be irresponsible. Of course, in order to hospitalize Nainai, it means they'd actually have to tell her she's sick.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Little Nainai</h4><p begin="00:04:51.70" data-timestamp="291.7">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:04:52.86" data-timestamp="292.86"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:05:00.23" data-timestamp="300.23">Little Nainai told the doctor that Nainai is too old, that she couldn't handle a blow like this. It's not just that Little Nainai didn't want to upset her sister with the news of her death. She actually believed that not telling her was a way to prolong her life. Knowing Nainai's personality, Little Nainai worried that her sister would get overwhelmed with fear and depression. She'd stop eating. She'd stop sleeping. She'd lose interest in life. The Chinese believe that mental and emotional health are completely linked to physical health.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Little Nainai</h4><p begin="00:05:32.15" data-timestamp="332.15">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:05:34.09" data-timestamp="334.09"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:05:35.07" data-timestamp="335.07">She tells me there's a Chinese joke. Two people go get a physical. One of them is healthy, and one of them has a terminal illness. The hospital gets the results mixed up. The healthy person gets a terminal prognosis and vice versa.</p><p begin="00:05:49.44" data-timestamp="349.44">So then the healthy person ends up dying while the sick person ends up living.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:05:53.66" data-timestamp="353.66">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:05:55.23" data-timestamp="355.23"></p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:05:58.72" data-timestamp="358.72">That's me asking Little Nainai, is that really a joke? It's not very funny.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Little Nainai</h4><p begin="00:06:02.85" data-timestamp="362.85">[SPEAKING CHINESE].</p><p begin="00:06:04.56" data-timestamp="364.56"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:06:06.04" data-timestamp="366.04">She assured me that it was. My family got a second opinion, and then a third. Same prognosis-- three months, maybe less. Little Nainai knew she needed a fake medical report to show Nainai, so she went to a print shop. They told her, we can't create a false medical record. If you want to make something up, we could photocopy it for you. Little Nainai whited out the real diagnosis and wrote down words that sounded vaguely medical but unalarming, something about the nine shadows.</p><p begin="00:06:41.83" data-timestamp="401.83">My family built on that lie with an even more elaborate one. We realized we had a problem. How are we all going to manage to see Nainai before she died? Family from three countries needed to say goodbye without letting Nainai know we were actually saying goodbye. This would require more than Wite-Out. My dad had an idea.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lulu's Dad</h4><p begin="00:07:04.56" data-timestamp="424.56">So I said, all right, your uncle, maybe we can just, you know, stage your son's wedding early, one year early.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:07:17.25" data-timestamp="437.25">Are you proud that you came up with this idea?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lulu's Dad</h4><p begin="00:07:19.41" data-timestamp="439.41">Well, you know, I'm the-- everybody considered me as the smartest in the family. So I mean, it's a show. I mean--</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:07:31.64" data-timestamp="451.64">This was our plan-- a giant goodbye party disguised as a giant wedding banquet.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Lulu's Dad</h4><p begin="00:07:37.76" data-timestamp="457.76">This is the lie that everybody agreed to lie.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Lulu Wang</h4><p begin="00:07:42.25" data-timestamp="462.25">And here's how it was going to go down. My Uncle Hai Bin's son, Liang-Liang, had recently married a Japanese woman.

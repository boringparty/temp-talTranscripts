But nothing appeared to be missing inside, except enough bread and sandwich meat to make a sandwich, the resident told police. The incident is similar to several other break-ins in the past few days, including one Wednesday in which a burglar drank a bottle of beer, and another Tuesday, in which a burglar prepared a turkey sandwich.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:56.77" data-timestamp="56.77">We think of crime as a kind of monolithic, menacing presence, a shadowy threat, lurking, faceless. But there are many kinds of crimes and many kinds of criminals. Take these crimes, ripped from today's headlines.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Matt Malloy</h4><p begin="00:01:10.72" data-timestamp="70.72">Crime blotter. The <i>Anacortes</i> <i>American</i>, Anacortes, Washington. A woman's persistence rewarded her with a shoplifting citation after she attempted to steal four 40-ounce bottles of beer, one at a time, from the Old Salt's convenience store on Skyline Way. The owner of the store told police that the woman, who appeared intoxicated to begin with, entered the store and went to the beer cooler, where she selected a 40-ounce Lucky Lager and left without paying. The owner quickly followed the woman and brought her back in the store. Undaunted by her apprehension, the woman again returned to the cooler, selected another Lucky, and again walked out, closely followed by the owner, who once again returned her to the store.</p><p begin="00:01:55.13" data-timestamp="115.13">When the owner went to her phone to call police, the persistent woman again jumped to the beer cooler, this time selecting a 40-ounce bottle of Milwaukee's Best, and started drinking down the brew as she left the store. The owner followed and once again returned the shoplifter to her store to wait for police.</p><p begin="00:02:16.09" data-timestamp="136.09">Apparently still a little parched, the woman returned to the cooler for a fourth time, and grabbed a 40-ounce bottle of St.  Ides. This time, however, she didn't bother leaving the store and chugged it down where she stood. The woman was cited for third degree theft, and then taken home and turned over to her husband.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:37.37" data-timestamp="157.37">Stories about crime, no matter how simple, are a kind of snapshot of someone's life and their problems at a certain moment. Through our crimes, we express who we are.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Matt Malloy</h4><p begin="00:02:49.17" data-timestamp="169.17">Crime blotter. The <i>Anacortes</i> <i>American</i>, Anacortes, Washington. Police arrested a 22-year-old Anacortes man Saturday morning, July 11, for causing a disturbance after he allegedly hurled potato salad at kids attending a festival, and, in the presence of a police officer, threatened to kill the operator of an inflatable obstacle course. The man told police he was upset because the festival, which started at 10:00 AM, was disrupting his sleep. The man was booked into Skagit County Jail on a charge of disorderly conduct.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:24.96" data-timestamp="204.96">Today on our radio program, stories about crime and the many varying reasons for committing crimes. Each of the people on today's show chose a crime that is particular to him or her. And it's actually impossible to imagine any of them committing the crimes that the others committed. When we choose to break the law, we choose carefully. It's as much a Rorschach test as anything in our lives.</p><p begin="00:03:47.84" data-timestamp="227.84">Act One of our program today, Your Good Girl's Gonna Go Bad, the story of the kind of criminal who somehow believes, through all her crimes, that she's a good person with good intentions. <div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Your Good Girl's Gonna Go Bad</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:30.71" data-timestamp="270.71">Act One, Your Good Girl's Gonna Go Bad. When she was 21, Julia Sweeney moved from Spokane, Washington, to Los Angeles to follow a dream, a dream so many people have, of landing a job in accounting. She had a fresh, new accounting degree from the University of Washington.</p><p begin="00:04:47.22" data-timestamp="287.22">But the dream accounting job that she had been promised at MGM Studios fell through. And she ended up working as an assistant bartender at a downtown hotel, meaning at various parties and events in the hotel, she sold tickets for drinks. And then customers would carry these tickets over to the real bartenders, who were all young, African American men, and not deemed trustworthy enough to handle cash themselves.</p><p begin="00:05:12.05" data-timestamp="312.05">Among other things, this turned out to be a bad piece of judgment on the part of hotel management. Julia estimates that she herself stole between $10,000 and $15,000 in cash in this job, though she started as an honest employee.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:05:26.35" data-timestamp="326.35">I started out very conscientiously. And there was some accounting involved, because you would write down the number of the first ticket and the number of the last ticket. And then you had to have the amount of money for the amount of tickets you sold. And I had--</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:39.04" data-timestamp="339.04">Did you actually think that at the time, well, there is some accounting involved in this? Was that a thought?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:05:42.58" data-timestamp="342.58">Yeah, actually I did. I thought, well, I like numbers. Now, I'm such a different person than the person I was who liked numbers, that it's hard for me to even believe I ever had that thought. But yes, at the time, I was looking forward to the number part of it. So I had three different kinds of tickets. I had beer and wine tickets, I had soft drink tickets, and I had mixed drink tickets. And they were like $2.75 and $2.50 and $1.25 or something. And they were different colors.</p><p begin="00:06:11.31" data-timestamp="371.31">And so I started selling these tickets. And it was just many, many hours of sitting at a card table. And people would come up, and you'd just make change and sell tickets. And well, to me, what started it was that they wouldn't pay for my parking at the hotel while I worked.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:28.72" data-timestamp="388.72">And I bet parking was really expensive.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:06:30.44" data-timestamp="390.44">Well, it was $3.00. But still, it made me mad that they wouldn't pay the $3.00 for the parking. So I justified taking $3.00 every time I worked because I thought they should pay for parking.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:46.39" data-timestamp="406.39">You felt like an injustice had been done. And you were just righting a wrong.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:06:50.65" data-timestamp="410.65">Yes.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:51.59" data-timestamp="411.59">And that set you on the path of crime.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:06:53.85" data-timestamp="413.85">Yes, it did. So I discovered that if you sold a ticket from inside the roll, for example, at the beginning of the roll, like if you pulled out the little cardboard insert that started the roll of tickets and pulled the ticket out of that part of it, that number on that ticket wouldn't be part of the numbers that you would be reporting. So I would just take one ticket from the middle and sell it. So I'd just take $2.75.</p><p begin="00:07:26.08" data-timestamp="446.08">But it did require me having to pocket the $2.75 when I was in the room counting the money with all the other cashiers. So you're kind of in front of people. So you have to be able to get that money in your pocket without them seeing. So I had to learn how to do that, which actually was very easy.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:44.78" data-timestamp="464.78">How do you do it?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Julia Sweeney</h4><p begin="00:07:46.81" data-timestamp="466.81">You just wait till no one's looking or just dumb things, like you cough or look the other way, and then you put $2.75 in your pocket. But then, I was working long shifts that were like eight hours. And I would get hungry. And I didn't have enough time to leave the hotel. And they didn't provide a meal. So I felt it was their responsibility to buy me lunch. So I thought, well, maybe I'll just take six more dollars' worth.</p><p begin="00:08:19.59" data-timestamp="499.59">OK, it's all a blur now, where it went from $6 to, oh, $500. But somehow, it just started increasing and increasing, because I started taking a little more and a little more. And then, things would happen like, oh, my car insurance would be due, and I was like $80 short.</p><p begin="00:08:43.88" data-timestamp="523.88">The thing that, looking back on it, is interesting to me is that I started getting really high from it. I would get a huge rush when I left the hotel and knew that I was safe with the money. And I can honestly say I've never really had that particular feeling of high again since I did that. And then I started doing really kind of nutty things where I would increase the danger, like I was flirting with getting caught or flirting with the danger of it.</p><p begin="00:09:13.87" data-timestamp="553.87">For example, I stopped parking at the hotel, and I purposely-- and this is like downtown LA, so it's a little scary and empty at night.

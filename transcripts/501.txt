  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.78" data-timestamp="0.78"> A friend in South Africa sent me the link to this article that is making the rounds there. It's an open letter, supposedly from the foreign media, to every man, woman, and child in their country.</p><p begin="00:00:12.14" data-timestamp="12.14">It begins, "Dear South Africa, please get the F of the way. Wait, that probably came out wrong. As you may have noted, we're back!" Meaning of course, that the foreign press has returned to South Africa to cover what is happening now with Nelson Mandela. I'll go back to the letter.</p><p begin="00:00:31.71" data-timestamp="31.71">"In the real world, far away from your sleepy backwater, news works on a 24-hour cycle, and you need to get out of the way. It's nothing personal. In fact, we couldn't do this successfully without you.</p><p begin="00:00:42.00" data-timestamp="42">In many cases, our footage is made more compelling by your presence. Specifically, we are fond of small black children praying and slash or singing in unison. Equally telegenic are the Aryan ubermensch blond kids, also praying slash singing, who help underscore the theme that Mandela united people of all races under a rainbow umbrella.</p><p begin="00:01:03.42" data-timestamp="63.42">Also very important, thematically speaking, are Mandela's successors. We very much like the idea that your ex-president was quote, 'one of a kind', and that despite his best efforts, the current batch of idiots proved that he was an exceptional presence. Sui generis. And we don't have to worry about someone else like him coming along in Africa ever again.</p><p begin="00:01:22.27" data-timestamp="82.27">We enjoy your leaders' bumbling ways, their daft nonsequiturs. This story would lack a tragic arc without Jacob Zuma." That of course, is the current president of the country. "May he keep on keeping on."</p><p begin="00:01:35.34" data-timestamp="95.34">The letter continues. "Then there's the Mandela family. Really, where would we derive our soap operatic undertones if it weren't for the infighting and blinged-up brashness of that clan? We love subtly implying that a saint sired a generation of professional shoppers, no-goodniks.</p><p begin="00:01:51.40" data-timestamp="111.4">In our biz we call that irony. Makes for great copy. In fact, we love everything about the country that doesn't live up to Mandela's legacy.</p><p begin="00:01:59.03" data-timestamp="119.03">We will take every opportunity to mention how everything you do flies in the face of everything Mandela would have wanted for his people, how you're basically a nation of underachieving screw ups. All of this is fantastic. We thank you profusely for your individual and collective contributions to this essential storyline, and urge you to keep squandering your potential."</p><p begin="00:02:20.46" data-timestamp="140.46">Can I say what I love about this? It was written by a journalist named Richard Poplak for the South African news service <i>Daily</i> <i>Maverick,</i> which you can find online.</p><p begin="00:02:28.74" data-timestamp="148.74">What I love about this is how it doesn't just take us foreigners inside how a South African might see our news coverage and the tone of that coverage, it also reveals a world of utterly justified resentments that I guess maybe we could have suspected, but we didn't really know about. It is an insider's view totally.</p><p begin="00:02:50.02" data-timestamp="170.02">Well, today on our radio show, we have stories from three radically different locations. We dive into three very different worlds for the insider's view that only insiders have.</p><p begin="00:03:00.70" data-timestamp="180.7">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life,</i> distributed by Public Radio International. I'm Ira Glass. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>
</div>
<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Weeds of Discontent</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:15.29" data-timestamp="195.29">Act One, The Weeds of Discontent. What we have now is a recording of a very unusual conversation that came about in an unusual way.</p><p begin="00:03:24.00" data-timestamp="204">There's this filmmaker named Eugene Jarecki who made a documentary about prisons and the criminal justice system called <i>The</i> <i>House</i> <i>I</i> <i>Live</i> <i>In</i>. And he's been taking it around the country and showing it in prisons, kind of Johnny Cash style, for inmates and corrections staff.</p><p begin="00:03:39.39" data-timestamp="219.39">One of our producers, Brian Reed, went to one of those screenings, which is where this conversation happened. Here's Brian.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:03:45.09" data-timestamp="225.09"> The screening I saw was at Joseph Harp Correctional Center, a medium security prison in Lexington, Oklahoma. About 35 inmates, most of them in for drug related crimes or with a history of drug use, sat waiting in the visiting room in chairs facing a projector screen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Prison Guard</h4><p begin="00:03:59.36" data-timestamp="239.36"> Once we get to where we're getting ready to start the movie, if you're here, you're staying.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:04:02.98" data-timestamp="242.98"> More than a dozen staff members came to watch the movie too, including the warden and some state officials from the Oklahoma Department of Corrections. And before the screening started, what did these drug offenders think the movie was going to be about?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Prisoner 1</h4><p begin="00:04:14.63" data-timestamp="254.63"> Rehab.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Prisoner 2</h4><p begin="00:04:16.61" data-timestamp="256.61"> Probably about some drugs and how to prevent yourself from doing some drugs.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Prisoner 3</h4><p begin="00:04:21.13" data-timestamp="261.13"> How drugs is bad for you. How it mess up your skin and your body. Make you age quicker too.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:04:27.62" data-timestamp="267.62"> But these guys were way off base. <i>The</i> <i>House</i> <i>I</i> <i>Live</i> <i>In</i> is about how drugs are bad for them, but not in the way they were expecting.</p><p begin="00:04:35.62" data-timestamp="275.62">Because it argues that the war on drugs has completely failed. That it's made law enforcement less effective and overwhelmed prisons. That's it's a self-perpetuating system which preys on poor, uneducated people. A system where politicians win elections by being tough on crime, a multi-billion dollar industry and entire towns thrive off the business of incarceration, and laws trap people with few opportunities in a cycle of committing crimes.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Ogletree</h4><p begin="00:04:58.62" data-timestamp="298.62"> People don't realize, for example, that when you arrest a young black man and he goes to jail, when he gets out of the prison, he can't get a job in most places because of his record.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:05:08.67" data-timestamp="308.67"> This is Charles Ogletree, a professor at Harvard Law School.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Charles Ogletree</h4><p begin="00:05:11.37" data-timestamp="311.37"> If he wants to go back to school to go to college, he's ineligible by law for certain grants. He can't get certain health care benefits. His family that was the centerpiece of his life, if they're living in public housing, they can't take him in. He can't vote!</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:05:25.94" data-timestamp="325.94"> The film has interviews with people throughout the criminal justice system. Inmates and their families, but also judges, police officers, and prison staff. Almost all of them say, in some way or another, that the system needs to be fixed.</p><p begin="00:05:38.60" data-timestamp="338.6">And what made it even more interesting for this audience in Oklahoma is that several people from the Oklahoma Department of Corrections are in the movie. And a couple of them were at the screening, watching it with the prisoners. Like Mike Carpenter, who oversees security for Oklahoma's medium security prisons.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mike Carpenter</h4><p begin="00:05:53.53" data-timestamp="353.53"> I am very much a law and order kind of guy. I would rather have 10 police cars than 1 soup kitchen if you give me the choice. But sometimes I think that we need to be smarter about what those police officers are actually out there doing.</p><p begin="00:06:10.10" data-timestamp="370.1">I think sometimes we have people doing a whole lot of time for not very much crime. It's almost like they're paying for our fear, instead of paying for their crime.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:06:20.94" data-timestamp="380.94"> So when the screening was over, some of the inmates were surprised that prison staff had even shown them this at all. One guy said it was exhilarating to hear corrections officers say things like, "We're jailing too many people. We're locking them up for too long."</p><p begin="00:06:34.42" data-timestamp="394.42">So we had this idea. We thought the film might prompt a conversation that prisoners and guards don't normally get to have about the prison system. And Oklahoma corrections authorities agreed to give us a room where I could sit down prisoners and guards and they could talk to each other one on one.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:06:49.56" data-timestamp="409.56"> Who do you want to talk to? Who do you want to talk with?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Antwaun Wells</h4><p begin="00:06:52.48" data-timestamp="412.48"> I want to talk to Dooley and Shelock.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:06:54.55" data-timestamp="414.55"> OK. Why do you want to talk to them?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Antwaun Wells</h4><p begin="00:06:58.72" data-timestamp="418.72"> My own personal opinions.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Brian Reed</h4><p begin="00:07:01.52" data-timestamp="421.52"> This is Antwaun Wells. He's 25, serving the third year of a nine year sentence for shooting a guy during an argument and also for stealing TVs from a Target and then stabbing a security guard in the arm on the way out of the store.</p><p begin="00:07:14.76" data-timestamp="434.76">It's not his first time in prison. He'd been in before. One of the staff members he picked-- Shelock-- wasn't around. But Dooley was.</p><p begin="00:07:23.50" data-timestamp="443.5">Dooley is Lt. Cecil Dooley.

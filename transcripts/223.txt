    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.70" data-timestamp="0.7">Hi, it's Ira Glass. Now, of course, Craigslist has taken the place of a lot of this classified advertising, putting the entire newspaper business in peril. So in just seven years, this has turned into a look at what once was. Here's the show.</p><p begin="00:00:23.83" data-timestamp="23.83">Marilyn Tanious has been working in the classifieds for 27 years at five different newspapers. And when she reads the ads, she sees things that you and I don't. The other day, she was sitting in her office at the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Sun-Times</i> with two of her colleagues and with me, flipping through the paper and telling stories. She talked about the gun ads that Timothy McVeigh used to take out in the <i>Arizona</i> <i>Republic</i>, back when she worked there, long before he blew up the Federal Building in Oklahoma. Or there was the time that Marilyn's own mother caught a thief when some stuff that had been stolen from one of their neighbors showed up in the For Sale ads. Then Marilyn turned the page and spotted this ad in the used car section.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marilyn Tanious</h4><p begin="00:01:01.39" data-timestamp="61.39">OK, here's an ad. Here's an ad that tells me something. OK, I'm going to read this ad to you. And I'm going to see, what do you think it tells you. OK. "Volvo, '92, a four-door sedan, automatic, mint condition in and out, 37K, Texas car, exclamation point, properly maintained and serviced, mechanically perfect, needs nothing. Must see to believe, respect, and appreciate, $5,850," and the phone number.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:27.57" data-timestamp="87.57">She looks at the three of us like, well, isn't it obvious? Then she points at the ad.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marilyn Tanious</h4><p begin="00:01:33.74" data-timestamp="93.74">This line, "must see to believe, respect, and appreciate," this person doesn't want to sell this car. You must see to respect?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:00.19" data-timestamp="120.19">Marilyn says that, when you read your newspaper, you've got the news part of up front, which gives you the public life of the city.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Marilyn Tanious</h4><p begin="00:02:05.58" data-timestamp="125.58">You have the sports. You have the entertainment. You have politics. But if you read the classifieds, it's really about individual, personal lives.</p><p begin="00:02:13.06" data-timestamp="133.06">It's, "My children are grown up, do I really need the piano anymore?" It's, "I'm really broke. I have to sell my possessions to live." I think you really get a sense of people's personal lives.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:28.79" data-timestamp="148.79">With that in mind, a few weeks ago, we the staff of this radio program, took one Sunday edition of the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Sun-Times</i>, and that same week's edition of the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Reader</i>, which is this weekly paper that we wanted to use, because of the musicians classifieds and the personal ads that they run. And then we hit the streets, tracking down the ads. For instance, "New, never worn wedding dress, tulle beaded bodice, $750 or best offer." There are several wedding dresses and a bunch of wedding rings for sale in the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Sun-Times.</i></p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman</h4><p begin="00:02:58.74" data-timestamp="178.74">Well, the bottom line was he didn't want to marry me anymore. You know me, but you don't care.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:49.03" data-timestamp="229.03">The same weekend, two hours after talking to her, we followed up on this ad, "Agoraphobics In Motion, A-I-M, meets weekly in Lakeview East. And not only that--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman 2</h4><p begin="00:05:10.77" data-timestamp="310.77">She would write in a lot of these books, "Given to me by my dear brother." And then it's dated November 21, 1980.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Woman 2</h4><p begin="00:05:46.33" data-timestamp="346.33">I mean, the inscriptions in these, "There was a vacant spot in my heart. Her mother.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:04.01" data-timestamp="364.01">In this one day of classifieds, there are all the people trying to adopt babies, and all the formerly adopted babies who are now adults looking for their parents. "Dan Earler, ?] if your belongings are not removed from 6123 South Archer Road, they will be discarded." And there are prayers. There are lots of prayers, every day in the newspaper.</p><p begin="00:06:37.62" data-timestamp="397.62">Well, from WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i>, distributed by Public Radio International. We use the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Sun-Times</i> from Sunday, August 25, and the same week's edition of the <i>Chicago</i> <i>Reader.</i> The classifieds are a public space where intensely private thoughts are being expressed all the time, much like this radio program. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Act One: Lost And Found Ads</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:12.73" data-timestamp="432.73">OK, maybe this is a cheap move, but we're going to start our radio program today with the most poignant possible elements for any story. They are the lost and found column and a puppy. Here's the ad.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Leo</h4><p begin="00:07:25.57" data-timestamp="445.57">Stolen dog, black, toy poodle named Isis. Stolen 7/27/02 from the Armitage-Kinzie area. Call 312-719-3669.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:36.10" data-timestamp="456.1">The production manager for our show, Todd Bachmann, who normally is not on the air, but who helps us get our show on the air, wanted to get in on the action this week. And he was the one to check out this ad.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Todd Bachmann</h4><p begin="00:07:46.74" data-timestamp="466.74">When I saw this ad, the thing that hooked me was the word "stolen." The toy poodle wasn't lost, like most missing dogs are, but abducted. Leo, the author of this ad, was working in his garage while Isis played in the fenced yard just outside.

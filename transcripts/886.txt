<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Both/And</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:05:38.49" data-timestamp="338.49"> It's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> Today's episode, Blackout. Other places have had their internet cut off during a war. Iran is not the first. But this isn't something that's being imposed on the country from the outside. It's being done by the leadership of the country to its own citizens while they're being attacked-- in this case by us, the United States, and Israel.</p><p begin="00:06:01.05" data-timestamp="361.05">Listening to these messages that people sent us, you really get the feeling for how total a blackout is. I had the thought, listening over and over, that every part of what they're saying is not supposed to be heard, even basic things like, what are people doing every day? A blackout is not a censorship that goes line by line. It is wholesale redaction of people's lives, and sometimes deaths.</p><p begin="00:06:27.81" data-timestamp="387.81">Here's what it took for people to get these messages out of Iran. They had to borrow or pay for a VPN or satellite connection, wait for it to actually work. Starlink connections in Iran keep getting shut down. They had to not get caught. Iranians have been imprisoned for speaking with foreign media. It sometimes took days to get these voice memos out of the country to us, often short messages, a minute at a time so the files were small enough to get through.</p><p begin="00:06:56.42" data-timestamp="416.42">The messages came from a whole range of people, from different parts of Iran, different ages, professions, political views, and also different experiences. Every time I got one of these messages, I stopped what I was doing to listen all the way through. So I want to play them for you the way I heard them, one person after another. We have edited them but tried to leave them at a length where you can really hear what people have to say as they go about their day. I'm going to play the messages in four sections. We could call them acts.</p><p begin="00:07:25.78" data-timestamp="445.78"></p><p begin="00:07:26.66" data-timestamp="446.66">So here we go, Act One, Both/And. In these first few messages, people talk about the dual experience of living both in a war and at the same time in an internet blackout, both dragging on and on, 63 days and counting as I record this now. First voice memo, Negin. She's in her early 40s and lives in Tehran with her boyfriend, Ameen.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Negin</h4><p begin="00:07:52.98" data-timestamp="472.98"> After breakfast, I got ready to go to work, got into my car, and drove onto the highway. Then suddenly, Ameen called and said, Negin, they're bombing Tehran. That was pretty much my last call. All internet lines were suddenly cut off.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:08:09.46" data-timestamp="489.46"> This message and all the messages you're going to hear in the show is being read by an actor. To protect everyone's identity in this show, we've changed people's names, and we've asked Iranian and Iranian American actors outside of Iran to listen to the messages and then record what people said and how they said it onto their phones as voice memos. Some of the original messages were recorded in English. Most were translated from Persian. Once or twice, we've changed a small phrase for clarity. OK, back to Negin.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Negin</h4><p begin="00:08:42.30" data-timestamp="522.3"> And then they started hitting the East of Tehran, these strange, terrifying sounds. We were really not prepared for suddenly being in the middle of the highway and hearing sounds like that. Some people, they just parked their cars and they got out. The deadline was evening in the US, 3:30 in the morning in Iran.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Anushirvan</h4><p begin="00:38:24.05" data-timestamp="2304.05"> Part of me didn't really want to take Trump seriously, but he is the president of the most powerful country in the world, so there is that. In Iran's time, it would be around 3 and 1/2 AM, if I'm not mistaken. I had charged my phone, my laptop, my portable power bank, not that they were going to last too long anyway, and had my essentials ready to go in case of emergency or if I decided to move back to my hometown, since being so high up in my current apartment, no electricity would also mean no water pump, not to mention no elevator.</p><p begin="00:38:55.26" data-timestamp="2335.26">I took a shower. And I think it was around 2:00 AM when I decided to leave it up to fate and sleep and wake up to either the unthinkable or to a war-torn country. And, yes, by unthinkable, I mean the ceasefire, as I had no idea what it would look like. I woke up automatically around 5:00 AM, grabbed my phone, and saw my dad's text notification that there was a ceasefire. I stared into the message a good couple minutes, stone faced, before going back to sleep.</p><p begin="00:39:27.46" data-timestamp="2367.46">To make all the damages caused by the war worth it, we would have to have a regime change. But now it looks like we just got the shorter stick on both ends. It brought nothing but more death and misery, not to mention it further entrenched the regime. This was one of the dumbest wars in history, to be honest.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Sarah</h4><p begin="00:39:44.22" data-timestamp="2384.22"> Last night, I was awake until maybe 12:30 or so. And there's a hallway in our house that has no windows around it, no glass. So that's where we sleep at night when they bomb. We go there to take shelter. And I was trying to sleep, but I was constantly checking the internet to see what was happening, where they were saying what. And I just had a feeling that there was going to be a ceasefire.</p><p begin="00:40:09.22" data-timestamp="2409.22">So I couldn't sleep. But I finally did fall asleep. And then the next morning, my partner told me that there had been a ceasefire. And to be honest, my feeling when I read that news was just very, very, very full of rage, especially the things that I read. And I mean, I honestly don't know how much of it is propaganda and how much of it is reality. But the things I read, that Iran is even earning money from The Strait of Hormuz during the ceasefire. Because we, the people of Iran, know that that money earned from there is not going to be spent on us. So we're just thinking that, well, we're just going to have a richer IRGC, and they're going to commit crimes even more easily. And I'm really, really afraid for the kids who are in prison, the kids who received death sentences, because I know they're going to be persecuted terribly. Executions are going to increase.</p><p begin="00:41:06.66" data-timestamp="2466.66">I think we're going to have even more severe crackdowns. Probably any kind of social movement of any form, which I honestly find unlikely after the January massacres, but they'll face an even greater massacre. I feel like we're the main losers of this war, the people of Iran. Because practically nothing has changed.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:41:32.82" data-timestamp="2492.82"> One voice memo was short and to the point, from Khabat, a carpenter. He's Kurdish, an ethnic minority in Iran, living in the western part of the country.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Khabat</h4><p begin="00:41:42.50" data-timestamp="2502.5"> People are really frustrated with Trump. Wasn't he supposed to keep the pressure on until the regime surrendered? Why is he going to negotiate now? He was supposed to finish them off, not talk to them.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:41:56.58" data-timestamp="2516.58"> One last person-- Arta. He runs a tech company in Isfahan. Arta's voice memos came in last. I listened after hearing all of the others. And it helped me understand something I hadn't before about where things might be headed in Iran. Arta was deeply unsettled by people around him who were furious that President Trump did not follow through on his threats, especially a promise Trump made on social media to bomb Iran back to the Stone Age.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Arta</h4><p begin="00:42:25.79" data-timestamp="2545.79"> You might not believe it, but the morning the ceasefire was announced, I actually called a friend. And he picked up the phone, saying, what is this? What a mess. Why did they just let it go? I mean, believe it or not, there is a segment of society who, in my opinion, probably lack a realistic view of what returning to the Stone Age actually entails, because maybe they don't really know what it's like to live without electricity in a fifth or sixth or 10th floor apartment.</p><p begin="00:42:54.75" data-timestamp="2574.75">But whether it is out of intense rage or just pure, sheer ignorance, many people prefer that we go back to the Stone Age and have our energy infrastructure be completely wiped out, as long as the Islamic regime went down with it.</p><p begin="00:43:09.87" data-timestamp="2589.87">So there's one group completely upset and furious, but then there's the complete opposite, the diehard loyalists ready to sacrifice their lives for the Islamic Republic. The situation is truly, it's extremely terrifying.</p><p begin="00:43:23.63" data-timestamp="2603.63">And for me, who's slightly in the middle-- I completely oppose the Islamic regime. But my red lines are the destruction of my country's infrastructure and the loss of my loved ones. And aside from that, if I'm being real, I'd give my own life to see the Islamic regime gone. But I do hold these two red lines.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:43:42.51" data-timestamp="2622.51"> There's another red line Arta has held for a while now. He tells this story. And this is where I felt like, oh, that is a clue, not just about the blackout and what's happening now, but something the Iranian regime seems to have been building up for years now.</p><p begin="00:43:58.11" data-timestamp="2638.11">Before the war, over a year ago, Arta was offered something he calls a white line. It's also called a white SIM card. White SIM cards are a way for Iranian officials and political leaders and regime-aligned journalists to bypass internet restrictions and access much more of the global internet than the general population ever has access to.</p><p begin="00:44:19.43" data-timestamp="2659.43">So even when there's an internet blackout or delays, if you have a white SIM card, you can get online. Arta had a contact who said they could get him a white card, a white line. But back then, Arta refused.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Arta</h4><p begin="00:44:32.71" data-timestamp="2672.71"> Many of my friends and former colleagues around me had white lines, and I knew it. But I resisted. I felt this is an undeniable right that all Iranian people should have, and I didn't want to use this special privilege. I wanted to stand alongside everyone else, living with the same hardships and struggles and VPNs and staying on this side, the side of the ordinary people.</p><p begin="00:44:58.63" data-timestamp="2698.63">You know what it's like? It's like using a special privilege just to have the right to eat food while the rest of your society-- your friends, your family, your siblings-- can't afford to eat meat. But because of your privilege, you do. Does that make sense? If I'm being real, I condemned a lot of my friends who used that kind of privilege. I would actually-- half jokingly, I would tell them, oh, shut up, bro, you have a white line.</p><p begin="00:45:27.59" data-timestamp="2727.59">We would actually talk amongst ourselves, saying, did you see that guy had a white line too, that guy who pretended to be so aligned with the people? And I was one of the proud ones. Many of my friends told me, we thought you definitely had one, but you didn't. I held my head high.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:45:45.19" data-timestamp="2745.19"> Then this war and this blackout, the longest ever. And Arta got a new offer, a new service approved by the state called Internet Pro. This would be like a white line or a white SIM card, but it would cost money, and it would give Arta a way to access the global internet. Arta is supporting his extended family. He had staff depending on him. They're an IT company. They couldn't work without the internet. So he did it. He signed up.</p><p begin="00:46:14.07" data-timestamp="2774.07">This was a guy who, before the war, would not even use the domestic messaging apps in the state-run intranet. He did not want to be surveilled. Now, to get Internet Pro, Arta was required to provide his national ID number, phone number, and the names and details of everyone he works with who would be using the internet. He handed all of that in.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Arta</h4><p begin="00:46:36.44" data-timestamp="2796.44"> Do I feel secure? No, not at all. Listen, I firmly believe their monitoring over me has increased significantly. I have absolutely no sense of security.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:46:48.20" data-timestamp="2808.2"> Surveillance and submission are the price of access. The blackout wasn't lifted when the ceasefire was declared, and it hasn't been lifted since. It now seems more and more possible that Iran will not return the internet back to the way it was before the war, ever.</p><p begin="00:47:05.00" data-timestamp="2825">And instead, there's a new plan-- tiered internet access, internet as a privilege granted by the state for people who support the state, or are at least willing and able to pay a state-sanctioned company for access. It turns the internet from a public place where anyone can show up and speak, listen, argue, spread conspiracy theories, find the best tweezers, to an internet that is more like an airport lounge, a silver elite, gold-plated, platinum loyalty program.</p><p begin="00:47:40.52" data-timestamp="2860.52">This is more and more common around the world, governments limiting internet access and sometimes going to full blackout. The whole thing has a real "coming to a country near you" vibe.</p><p begin="00:47:52.04" data-timestamp="2872.04">When I first listened to these voice memos from Iran, they felt to me like a rare document of a rare time, people living their lives in this temporary moment through a war and a blackout. But I think of the voice memos differently now. I think it's possible they're documenting a massive, frightening change.</p><p begin="00:48:23.44" data-timestamp="2903.44">That story was produced by Diane Wu.</p></div>  </div>

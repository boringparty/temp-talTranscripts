  <h3>Prologue</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.58" data-timestamp="0.58">The way I always heard the story was that Van Halen had something in the contract that they used when they toured that said that everywhere that they went, in every city, in every dressing room on their tour, there had to be a bowl of M&amp;Ms, and that the brown M&amp;Ms had to be removed. It's kind of a well-known story, I think. And the way that I understood it is that it showed what divas rock stars could be, that any whim that they had would have to be met, no matter how petty. You hate brown M&amp;Ms? Poof! They will cease to exist in your world.</p><p begin="00:00:32.47" data-timestamp="32.47">And then a couple of years ago, we had this band, They Might Be Giants, on our radio show. And by the way, you're listening to <i>This American Life</i> from WBEZ Chicago, distributed by Public Radio International. Anyway, we had this band on the show. And I got to know them a little bit. And I had never talked to a touring rock musician about that story. And I remember John Flansburgh saying to me, no, no, no, no, no. I had the meaning of the story totally wrong.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:00:53.83" data-timestamp="53.83">I think there was only one &quot;no,&quot; Ira.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:56.75" data-timestamp="56.75">This is John. I asked him to come and talk about this with me again today, here on the radio. He told me that the music industry name for what we were discussing was the contract rider.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:01:07.40" data-timestamp="67.4">The thing that the average rock fan doesn't realize is that, in the itinerant life of somebody in a rock band, they're relying on a promoter-- probably a different promoter every day-- to give them everything. And a contract rider is basically the entire show from beginning to end. I mean, you're talking about personnel. You're talking about the PA. So a lot of it's very prosaic stuff. People really focus on the dressing room stuff, but actually most of it is just making sure that there's literally enough electricity in the venue so that the show doesn't end after 10 minutes.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:46.88" data-timestamp="106.88">And this, Flansburgh says, was what was so ingenious about the brown M&amp;Ms. Van Halen had this huge setup with lots of gear, and if the local promoter didn't carefully read the contract rider, stuff could collapse. It could be dangerous. So the brown M&amp;Ms were like the canary in the coal mine. The contract rider said the brown M&amp;Ms were not supposed to be there. If they were there, look out.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:02:06.49" data-timestamp="126.49">You know, it was a very clever way to make sure that all the specifics of his contract rider were going to be met, including technical requirements, safety requirements, all the things that David Lee Roth is probably more worried about than his actual M&amp;M needs.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:24.07" data-timestamp="144.07">Yeah. Actually, in his autobiography, he writes this. I found this on snopes.com. He explains the M&amp;Ms this way. David Lee Roth writes, &quot;Van Halen was the first band to take huge productions into third level markets.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:02:37.44" data-timestamp="157.44">Tertiary markets is the word we use in the business.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:39.48" data-timestamp="159.48">Tertiary markets. &quot;We'd pull up with nine 18-wheeler trucks full of gear in places where the standard was three trucks max. And there were many, many technical errors, whether it was the girders couldn't support the weight, or the flooring would sink in, or the doors weren't big enough to move the gear through. The contract rider read like a version of the Chinese Yellow Pages, because there was so much equipment, and so many human beings to make it function. So just as a little test, in article number 126, in the middle of nowhere was, quote, 'There will be no brown M&amp;Ms in the backstage area upon pain of forfeiture of the show with full compensation,' end quote.&quot;</p><p begin="00:03:13.93" data-timestamp="193.93">So, he writes, &quot;When I would walk backstage, if I saw a brown M&amp;M in that bowl, well, line check the entire production, guaranteed you're going to arrive at a technical error. They didn't read the contract. Guaranteed, you'd run into a problem. Sometimes it would threaten to destroy the whole show. Sometimes literally life threatening.&quot;</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:03:31.54" data-timestamp="211.54">Wow.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:39.64" data-timestamp="219.64">Now, when I emailed you to see if you wanted to come on the radio and talk about this, you said, &quot;Oh, that's really a coincidence.&quot; Because you just spent your whole day yesterday working on your contract rider?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:03:50.14" data-timestamp="230.14">Basically, a couple days ago, I was looking at the contract rider, which was 25 pages long. And I realized it was this crazy, Frankenstein document that-- there was some really odd, vestigial stuff. I mean I actually found-- we have all these personnel requirements for loaders, and electricians, and fly riggers, and all these people. There's 30 people that the promoter is going to hire on our behalf, and they have very specific job descriptions. But in only half of them did we require that they be sober.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:23.55" data-timestamp="263.55">Wait, your contract for some of them says specifically they have to be sober?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:04:26.95" data-timestamp="266.95">It was such a hodgepodge that we had, in some cases-- What had happened is that we had had a bunch of loaders that had come in from another show the night before that had ended at 5:00 in the morning. And they came to our show at 7:00 in the morning to, literally, do another show. And they all got drunk in the couple of hours in between.</p><p begin="00:04:46.48" data-timestamp="286.48">So in our contract rider, we said the loaders have to be sober. But unfortunately, the way a contract reads, it looks like you're kind of implying that everybody else can be drunk. And no one had ever thought to cross it out.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:01.75" data-timestamp="301.75">Is there an M&amp;Ms clause in your contract?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:05:05.70" data-timestamp="305.7">There are some-- It's such a personal thing. It's like asking somebody what's in their medicine chest. There are no M&amp;Ms on our-- We have, like, hummus and tabouli. You would think it was Sarah McLachlan the way our contract rider reads.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:21.03" data-timestamp="321.03">I mean more, is there an M&amp;Ms clause, is there a thing in your contract that you put in there to be sure that people read the contract?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:05:27.88" data-timestamp="327.88">I think the first line of our contract is the promoter needs to call our tour manager when he gets this rider. That's basically just getting good communication going, rather than bullying and threshold tests, is the way we do it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:43.45" data-timestamp="343.45">And so if you don't get the call, you know, all right--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>John Flansburgh</h4><p begin="00:05:45.42" data-timestamp="345.42">Yeah, yeah. And believe it or not, oftentimes they don't call. They've got other things to do.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:59.55" data-timestamp="359.55">Well, today on our radio show we have stories of the fine print. We dive into the fine print in places where the fine print really, really matters. Act one of our show, Chana Joffe-Walt explains how the fine print may be the biggest obstacle to getting rid of our acne. Act two, David Rakoff brings us the fine print that could fix what therapy fails to. Act three, Nancy Updike has the story of fine print happening at a place you may never have expected it. Act four, Susan Burton rereads the fine print that changed her childhood. Stay with us.</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>ACT ONE: ONE PILL TWO PILL, RED PILL BLUE PILL</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:06:41.44" data-timestamp="401.44">Act One. One Pill Two Pill, Red Pill Blue Pill. Let's start today in a place that is swimming in fine print, the insurance business. President Obama said during the health care debates that if we all understood our health care, it would save money.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>President Obama</h4><p begin="00:06:57.12" data-timestamp="417.12">If there's a blue pill and a red pill, and the blue pill is half the price of the red pill and works just as well, why not pay half price?</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:09.21" data-timestamp="429.21">And the problem with the fine print is that it makes it hard to tell which pill is the red one and which one is the blue one, as Chana Joffe-Walt explains.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:07:17.61" data-timestamp="437.61">Ted Serra is in the middle of a war. It's been going on for many years and involves billions of dollars. A lot of us are in this war. And like Ted, we don't even know it. He stumbled onto the battlefield because he's got pimples. Pimples and a card.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ted Serra</h4><p begin="00:07:34.51" data-timestamp="454.51">It is called the Solodyn Patient Access Card.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Chana Joffe-Walt</h4><p begin="00:07:38.48" data-timestamp="458.48">What's it look like?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Ted Serra</h4><p begin="00:07:39.47" data-timestamp="459.47">And it looks like a little credit card.

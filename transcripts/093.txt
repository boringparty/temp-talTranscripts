And he writes the first lyric love poems ever written.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Klein</h4><p begin="00:01:53.98" data-timestamp="113.98">He says before this moment, one lives in a state of distraction. And the idea of this moment, the bolt of lightning, gets repeated in literature and song and theater all through the Renaissance, up through the 19th century, and into the present day, today. This is what we talk about, when we talk about love.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Klein</h4><p begin="00:03:04.94" data-timestamp="184.94">In ancient Greek, the Greeks had two verbs for seeing.   <h3>Act One: Before And After</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:31.80" data-timestamp="451.8">Act One, Before and After. As a kid growing up on the East Coast, in the 1970s, for me, the most romantic songs in the world-- and this is kind of a corny thing to admit now, years later with retrospect-- the most romantic songs in the world were Bruce Springsteen's. Songs about falling in love in run-down beach towns on the Jersey Shore, songs about hitting the road together to, you know, who knows where.</p><p begin="00:07:55.78" data-timestamp="475.78">But once Bruce hit a certain age, he stopped writing songs about that moment when lightning flashes between people, when their eyes meet.</p><p begin="00:08:03.03" data-timestamp="483.03">And he wrote a series of songs where basically every song takes place long after the couple fell out of love. And the narrator, the singer of these songs, is remembering that exhilarating moment of falling in love, and then describing the moment he's in now, the drudgery of his marriage now. And he's laying these two moments side by side, trying to make sense of how one of them has any connection in the world to the other. How one of them led to the other.</p><p begin="00:08:28.57" data-timestamp="508.57">Well, this next story is something like that. It's by Richard Bausch.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Richard Bausch</h4><p begin="00:08:32.70" data-timestamp="512.7">It's exactly 20 minutes to midnight on this, the eve of my 70th birthday. And I've decided to address you, for a change, in writing, odd as that might seem. I'm perfectly aware of how you're going to take the fact that I'm doing this at all, so late at night with everybody due to arrive tomorrow in the house, still unready.</p><p begin="00:08:50.96" data-timestamp="530.96">I haven't spent almost five decades with you without learning a few things about you that I can predict and describe with some accuracy. Though I admit that, as you put it, lately we've been more like strangers than husband and wife. Well, so if we are like strangers, perhaps there are some things I can tell you that you won't have already figured out about the way I feel.</p><p begin="00:09:11.63" data-timestamp="551.63">Tonight, we had another one of those long, silent evenings after an argument. Remember? Over pepper.</p><p begin="00:09:19.38" data-timestamp="559.38">We had been bickering all day, really, but at dinner, I put pepper on my potatoes, and you said that about how I shouldn't have pepper because it always upsets my stomach. I bothered to remark that I used to eat chili peppers for breakfast, and if I wanted to put plain old ordinary black pepper on my potatoes, as I had been doing for more than 60 years, that was my privilege.</p><p begin="00:09:39.64" data-timestamp="579.64">Writing this now, it sounds far more testy than I meant it. But that isn't really the point. In any case, you chose to overlook my tone. You simply said, John, you were up all night the last time you had pepper with your dinner. I said, I was up all night because I ate green peppers. Not black pepper, but green pepper. A pepper is a pepper, isn't it? You said.</p><p begin="00:10:06.04" data-timestamp="606.04">And then I started in on you. I got, as you call it, legal with you, pointing out that green peppers are not black pepper. And from there we moved on to an evening of mutual disregard for each other that ended with your decision to go to bed early.</p><p begin="00:10:19.88" data-timestamp="619.88">The grandchildren will make you tired, and there's still the house to do.   <h3>Act Three: Without</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:39:29.46" data-timestamp="2369.46">Act Three, Without. Donald Hall says that when he first got together with his wife, Jane Kenyon, the earliest years were not so very intense. They didn't have that big lightning moment. It was the '70s, he says, by way of explanation. They saw each other while they also saw other people. And then they slowly decided that they had no interest in the other people. And then they got married. And then, three years into the marriage, they moved to a farm in New Hampshire that had been in Donald's family for generations.</p><p begin="00:39:55.03" data-timestamp="2395.03">And it was only then, Donald Hall says, that they entered their most intimate time together. They got closer every year of their marriage, he says, and the closest time of all between them came at the end of their marriage, when after 22 years together, Jane Kenyon got leukemia and he took care of her.</p><p begin="00:40:09.68" data-timestamp="2409.68">During this time, he started to document what was happening between them in his writing. And his newest book of poems, called <i>Without,</i> is all work he began during her illness and after her death in 1995. The poems include sad moments and funny moments, and a million little details of everyday life during a life-threatening crisis. In one poem, Jane stares at the mirror at her bald head and her face, swollen from the drugs that she's taking, and she declares, I am Telly Savalas.</p><p begin="00:40:34.98" data-timestamp="2434.98">In another, Donald asked, why were they not contented four months ago? Because Jane did not have leukemia? A year hence, would he question why he was not contented now? Therefore, he was contented.</p><p begin="00:40:48.58" data-timestamp="2448.58">He writes about the dog Gus reacting to their illness, about Jane trying to finish her own book of poems called <i>Otherwise</i>, and about small moments when they both realize once again, all over again, what's happening to them.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:41:00.31" data-timestamp="2460.31">Alone together a moment on the 22nd anniversary of their wedding, he clasped her as she stood at the sink, pressing into her backside, rubbing his cheek against the stubble of her skull. He gave her a ring of pink tourmaline with nine small diamonds around it. She put it on her finger, and immediately named it Please Don't Die.</p><p begin="00:41:25.68" data-timestamp="2485.68">They kissed and Jane whispered, timor mortis conturbat me. That last bit, of course, is Latin and Jane was not a Latin scholar, but that's a refrain line of a wonderful medieval Scots' poem about dead poets, and it translates more or less, the fear of death shakes me.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:41:49.39" data-timestamp="2509.39">Usually when we think about romance, the moment that we think about is the beginning of the romance as the most intense time. And in fact one of the things that happens in these poems is it's just the two of you going through a time that's just as intense.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:42:05.64" data-timestamp="2525.64">Oh, more intense. More intense. Really, we had a very good marriage. We were very fortunate. We loved the same things. And then we began to be sick. I had cancer a couple of times and then Jane had leukemia, beginning at the age of 46 and dying at 47. Jane was 19 years younger than me. But when you think you're going to lose what you love, when you know it's probably going to happen, then the sweetness of the intensity and the pain of the intensity is greater than ever, I do think</p><p begin="00:42:43.74" data-timestamp="2563.74">I think it was beautiful. I often say that obviously the worst thing in my life is Jane dying. But one of the best things in my life was taking care of her.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:42:53.83" data-timestamp="2573.83">There's a line in one of the poems actually, where you say, he felt shamed to understand that he would miss the months of sickness and taking care.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:43:01.95" data-timestamp="2581.95"> And I had that thought literally. It's all right to tell lies in books, but this was not a lie. I had that thought and wrote down something like that line the day before we found out that she was going to die.</p><p begin="00:43:16.82" data-timestamp="2596.82">I was optimistic. And I thought, with guilt, I'll be sorry when I can't take care of her anymore.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:43:24.06" data-timestamp="2604.06">So explain this poem, &quot;Last Days,&quot; to me.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:43:26.22" data-timestamp="2606.22">Well &quot;Last Days&quot; is the poems that I wrote or began to write during the last days of her life.</p><p begin="00:43:33.19" data-timestamp="2613.19">One day in April we had her blood checked, which we did once a week, and it was just fine. And a week later, the leukemia had come back and there was nothing to do. She was going to die.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:43:47.95" data-timestamp="2627.95">This is after also she went through very intensive chemotherapy and radiation treatment, a bone marrow transplant--</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:43:54.31" data-timestamp="2634.31">Everything. Yeah, 15 months of a lot of suffering. So this was what we came home with the doctor saying, it might be a month. But it actually took just 11 days. And here are poems out of those 11 days.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:44:07.65" data-timestamp="2647.65">OK.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:44:09.28" data-timestamp="2649.28">Home that afternoon, they threw her medicines into the trash. Jane vomited. He wailed, while she remained dry-eyed, silent, trying to let go. At night, he picked up the telephone to make calls that brought a child or a friend into the horror.</p><p begin="00:44:30.92" data-timestamp="2670.92">The next morning, they worked choosing among her poems for <i>Otherwise</i>, picked hymns for her funeral, and supplied each other words as they wrote and revised her obituary.</p><p begin="00:44:43.04" data-timestamp="2683.04">The day after, with more work to do on her book, he saw how weak she felt and said, maybe not now. Maybe later. Jane shook her head. Now, she said. We have to finish it now.</p><p begin="00:44:58.22" data-timestamp="2698.22">Later, as she slid exhausted into sleep, she said, wasn't that fun? To work together. Wasn't that fun?</p><p begin="00:45:11.79" data-timestamp="2711.79">He asked her, what clothes should we dress you in when we bury you?</p><p begin="00:45:17.64" data-timestamp="2717.64">I hadn't thought, she said.</p><p begin="00:45:19.84" data-timestamp="2719.84">I wondered about the white salwar kameez, he said, her favorite Indian silk they bought in Pondicherry a year and a half before, which she wore for best or prettiest afterward.</p><p begin="00:45:32.51" data-timestamp="2732.51">She smiled. Yes, excellent, she said. He didn't tell her that a year earlier, dreaming awake, he had seen her in the coffin in her white salwar kameez.</p><p begin="00:45:49.23" data-timestamp="2749.23">They talked about their adventures, driving through England when they first married, and excursions to China and India. No more [BLEEP]ing.</p><p begin="00:46:24.33" data-timestamp="2784.33">Incontinent three nights before she died, Jane needed lifting onto the commode. He wiped her and helped her back into bed. At 5:00 he fed the dog and returned to find her across the room, sitting in a straight chair. When she couldn't stand, how could she walk? He figured she would fall and called for an ambulance to the hospital. But when he told Jane, her mouth twisted down and tears started.</p><p begin="00:46:54.59" data-timestamp="2814.59">Do we have to? He canceled. Jane said, Perkins, be with me when I die.</p><p begin="00:47:06.47" data-timestamp="2826.47">Dying is simple, she said. What's worst is the separation. When she no longer spoke, they lay alone together, touching, and she fixed on him her beautiful enormous round, brown eyes, shining, unblinking, and passionate with love and dread.</p><p begin="00:47:29.83" data-timestamp="2849.83">One by one, they came, the oldest and dearest, to say goodbye to this friend of the heart. At first she said their names, wept and touched, then she smiled. Then turned one mouth corner up. On the last day, she stared silent goodbyes, with her hands curled and her eyes stuck open.</p><p begin="00:47:56.93" data-timestamp="2876.93">Leaving his place beside her, where her eyes stared, he told her, I'll put these letters in the box. She had not spoken for three hours. And now Jane said her last words, OK.</p><p begin="00:48:13.13" data-timestamp="2893.13">At 8:00 that night, her eyes open as they stayed until she died, brain-stem breathing started. He bent to kiss her pale cool lips again and felt them one last time gather and purse and peck to kiss him back.</p><p begin="00:48:32.79" data-timestamp="2912.79">In the last hours, she kept her forearms raised with pale fingers clenched at cheek level, like the goddess figurine over the bathroom sink. A sharp, almost sweet smell began to rise from her open mouth.</p><p begin="00:49:12.83" data-timestamp="2952.83">That's a line from her, by the way. She wrote a wonderful poem called Gettysburg. And she says a sharp, almost sweet smell began to rise from his open mouth. And nobody has noticed that yet. I'm ripping her off as she dies. She wouldn't mind. She'd be amused.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:49:35.24" data-timestamp="2975.24">Do you feel like going through the process of revising and finishing this book, and publishing it, has kept you suspended in that period of mourning for her longer than perhaps you would've otherwise?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Donald Hall</h4><p begin="00:49:49.98" data-timestamp="2989.98">No, I don't think so.

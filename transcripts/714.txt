  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:08.99" data-timestamp="8.99"> Me, I'm not crazy about the beach. It's hard to think of a less comfortable place to read a book than flat on a blanket under the scorching sun. Going in the waves-- totally fine for a little bit, but, like, all day? And like many people over 40, I have no desire to ever be seen in a bathing suit by anyone ever for the rest of my life.</p><p begin="00:00:32.74" data-timestamp="32.74">But know how many times I've been to the beach this summer? OK, there were two quickie day trips and a seven-day beach vacation. I don't like the beach, but, I've been. A bunch.</p><p begin="00:00:55.80" data-timestamp="55.8">And why? Well, because people I love love the beach. Hating the beach during summer-- and I believe I can say this with authority from personal experience. Hating the beach in summer is like being a Jew at Christmas. You can try to sit it out, but it's just too big. At some point, you're going to drink some eggnog.</p><p begin="00:01:18.23" data-timestamp="78.23">And you know what? When I go to the beach, I try-- I really try-- to get into it, to have a nice time, to appreciate the waves, and the sand, and the heat. And I think about the many hundreds and thousands of years that families have brought their kids to the water's edge to play pretty much exactly the same dumb way that we play in the sand and waves today. How many ways are there to do that anyway? And you know, some people really love it so much.</p><p begin="00:01:42.17" data-timestamp="102.17">A while back, here on our radio show, we also had this article about somebody like that. At the time, he was a 66-year-old lifeguard who was suing New York state for age discrimination. And I just want to pause on that for a second-- a 66-year-old lifeguard.</p><p begin="00:01:56.52" data-timestamp="116.52">All of us here on our staff, we had no idea that could even exist. We all thought lifeguarding is something you do when you're in high school, maybe a couple years after, into your 20s. Like, who's still lifeguarding at 66?</p><p begin="00:02:07.66" data-timestamp="127.66">And then it was even more of a question when we realized that the lifeguard in the story, he has another job. He's a lawyer. He's a working lawyer. His name is Roy Lester. He's a bankruptcy attorney. He's got his own firm on Long Island. And then he lifeguards every weekend in the summer. And can I just say, today on our program we have stories about people like him-- people who love the beach, not people like me.</p><p begin="00:02:33.12" data-timestamp="153.12">From WBEZ Chicago, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass. And let's just get right to it with Act One.</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Act One: Grapes of Wrath</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:49.22" data-timestamp="169.22"> Act One, the Grapes of Wrath. Basically the deal is that they tried to make him wear a Speedo. He refused. He lost his job. Here's Dana.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:03:07.88" data-timestamp="187.88"> If you asked Roy, why are you still lifeguarding at 66, he barely understands the question. His best man at his wedding was a lifeguard.</p><p begin="00:03:23.15" data-timestamp="203.15">Their kids grew up playing together on the beach while they were on duty. I never took it quite that seriously. The idea of giving up the summer was something I just couldn't do.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:03:48.83" data-timestamp="228.83"> He's not alone. Lifeguarding at Jones Beach is such a thing that a former lifeguard made a film about it. It's called <i>Jones</i> <i>Beach</i> <i>Boys.</i> Roy insisted I watch it. I did. It was awesome. Here's my favorite song from it.</p><p begin="00:04:10.79" data-timestamp="250.79">[MUSIC PLAYING]</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Singer</h4><p begin="00:04:11.26" data-timestamp="251.26"> (SINGING) We're going for the rescue and getting to the victim.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:04:16.89" data-timestamp="256.89"> "We're going for the rescue and getting to the victim." I never really appreciated how thrilling lifeguarding is until Roy talked about rescues. We were sitting in his law office.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roy Lester</h4><p begin="00:04:26.81" data-timestamp="266.81"> The exhilaration of a good rescue is unlike anything you've ever had. And you don't get that. I sit here, and I shuffle papers. I wouldn't call it exciting. I wouldn't call it rewarding.</p><p begin="00:04:42.89" data-timestamp="282.89">But this is-- you're actually accomplishing something. You're up there, and all of a sudden you're going out in the water, and the rest of world is behind you. There's nothing else except between you getting from your stand to that victim. That's the only thing, and it's great. It's a great feeling.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:09.56" data-timestamp="309.56"> How many people do you think you've rescued in your career?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roy Lester</h4><p begin="00:05:12.77" data-timestamp="312.77"> Over 1,000.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:13.93" data-timestamp="313.93"> 1,000?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roy Lester</h4><p begin="00:05:14.82" data-timestamp="314.82"> Yeah. You have to remember, there were times we would have 40 rescues in an hour.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:19.83" data-timestamp="319.83"> What? Why? What? Why?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roy Lester</h4><p begin="00:05:22.61" data-timestamp="322.61"> Because you have people that come down to Jones Beach who really don't know about swimming. So-- especially when you have a current. And you can get a very strong current at Jones Beach.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Dana Chivvis</h4><p begin="00:05:35.45" data-timestamp="335.45"> 1,000 rescues, that's way more rescues than David Hasselhoff did on <i>Baywatch.</i> I figure two rescues per episode, 10 years on the air, Roy would still beat him by 560 rescues. Which is to say, Roy is one of the lifeguardiest lifeguards there is. He had two wins at the national lifeguard competition. He served as an expert lifeguard witness in court cases.</p><p begin="00:06:00.53" data-timestamp="360.53">And all was well in his happy lifeguarding world until the Speedo mishegoss began in 2007. Here's what happened. If you're a lifeguard at Jones Beach, you have to take a physical fitness test every year to prove that you're still able to do the job. It includes a speed test in a pool. You have to swim 100 yards in a minute 20, which is actually pretty fast. A lot of these guys train all year for it.</p><p begin="00:06:25.01" data-timestamp="385.01">For 15 years, Roy took the swim test in his preferred swimsuit, a pair of jammers. They look like bike shorts without the butt cushion. If you're watching the Olympics right now, all the male swimmers are wearing them. They're tight, and they go down to just above the knee.</p><p begin="00:06:39.96" data-timestamp="399.96">But when Roy showed up for the test in 2007, he was told no jammers. His bosses at the office of parks and recreation said, you can only do the test in one of the official Jones Beach lifeguard swimsuits, which means you have three choices-- board shorts, trunks, or Speedo.</p><p begin="00:06:57.28" data-timestamp="417.28">Board shorts and trunks are loose, so nobody really takes a swim test in them because they create more drag and slow you down. So in effect, state officials were saying to Roy, you have to take the test in a Speedo. Roy said, no way. I won't do it. And he hasn't been a lifeguard at Jones Beach since.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Roy Lester</h4><p begin="00:07:12.98" data-timestamp="432.98"> It was one of those feelings like, am I making the right decision? Our father was a figure of authority, while Paul is more of a playmate.</p><p begin="00:51:26.51" data-timestamp="3086.51">When we went to the beach as children, on or about the fourth day our father would say, "Wouldn't it be nice to buy a cottage down here?" We'd get our hopes up, and then he would bring practical concerns into it. They weren't petty. Buying a house that will eventually get blown away by a hurricane probably isn't the best way to spend your money, but still we wanted one desperately.</p><p begin="00:51:48.83" data-timestamp="3108.83">I told myself when I was young that one day I would buy a beach house, and then it would be everyone's, as long as they followed my Draconian rules and never stopped thanking me for it. Thus it was on Wednesday morning, midway through our vacation, Hugh and I contacted a real estate agent named Phyllis, who took us around to look at available properties. On Friday afternoon, we made an offer on an oceanfront cottage not far from the one we were renting. Before sunset, our bid was accepted. I made the announcement at the dinner table and got the reaction I had expected.</p><p begin="00:52:23.32" data-timestamp="3143.32">"Now wait a minute," my father said. "You need to think clearly here."</p><p begin="00:52:28.02" data-timestamp="3148.02">"I already have," I told him.</p><p begin="00:52:29.50" data-timestamp="3149.5">"OK, then how old is the roof? How many times has it been replaced in the past 10 years?"</p><p begin="00:52:34.38" data-timestamp="3154.38">"When can we move in?" Gretchen asked.</p><p begin="00:52:37.14" data-timestamp="3157.14">Lisa wanted to know if she could bring her dogs, and Amy asked what the house was named.</p><p begin="00:52:43.07" data-timestamp="3163.07">"Right now it's called Fantastic Place," I told her, "but we're going to change it." I used to think the ideal name for a beach house was the Ship Shape. Now, though, I had a better idea. "We're going to call it the Sea Section."</p><p begin="00:53:02.18" data-timestamp="3182.18">My father put down his hamburger. "Oh, no, you're not."</p><p begin="00:53:05.33" data-timestamp="3185.33">"But it's perfect," I argued. "The name's supposed to be beachy, and, if it's a pun, all the better."</p><p begin="00:53:10.91" data-timestamp="3190.91">I brought up a cottage we'd seen earlier in the day called Dune Our Thing, and my father winced. "What about naming it Tiffany?" he said.</p><p begin="00:53:22.85" data-timestamp="3202.85">Our silence translated to, let's pretend we didn't hear that.</p><p begin="00:53:26.75" data-timestamp="3206.75">He picked his hamburger back up. "I think it's a great idea-- the perfect way to pay our respects."</p><p begin="00:53:32.50" data-timestamp="3212.5">"If that's the case, we could name it after Mom," I told him, "or half after Mom and half after Tiffany. But it's a house, not a tombstone, and it wouldn't fit in with the names of the other houses."</p><p begin="00:53:40.52" data-timestamp="3220.52">"Aw, baloney," my father said. "Fitting in-- that's not who we are. That's not what we're about."</p><p begin="00:53:46.07" data-timestamp="3226.07">Paul interrupted to nominate the Conch Sucker.</p><p begin="00:53:50.15" data-timestamp="3230.15">Amy's suggestion had the word "seaman" in it, and Gretchen's was even dirtier.</p><p begin="00:53:54.62" data-timestamp="3234.62">"What's wrong with the name it already has," Lisa asked.</p><p begin="00:53:57.08" data-timestamp="3237.08">"No, no, no," my father said, forgetting, I think, that this wasn't his decision. A few days later, after the buyer's remorse had kicked in, I'd wonder if I hadn't bought the house as a way of saying, see, it's just that easy-- no hemming and hawing, no asking to look at the septic tank. Rather, you make your family happy and iron out the details later.</p><p begin="00:54:23.27" data-timestamp="3263.27">The cottage we bought is two stories tall and was built in 1978. It's on proper stilts and has two rear decks, one above the other, overlooking the ocean. It was rented to vacationers until late September, but Phyllis allowed us to drop by and show it to the family the following morning, after we checked out of the house we'd been staying in.</p><p begin="00:54:45.17" data-timestamp="3285.17">A place always looks different-- worse, most often-- after you've made the commitment to buy it. So while the others raced up and down the stairs, claiming their future bedrooms, I held my nose to a vent and caught a whiff of mildew. The sale included the furniture, so I also made an inventory of the Barcaloungers and massive TVs I would eventually be getting rid of, along with the shell-patterned bedspreads and cushions with anchors on them.</p><p begin="00:55:12.03" data-timestamp="3312.03">"For our beach house, I want to have a train theme," I announced. "Trains on the curtains, trains on the towels-- we're going to go all out."</p><p begin="00:55:21.87" data-timestamp="3321.87">"Oh, brother," my father moaned.</p><p begin="00:55:27.37" data-timestamp="3327.37">We sketched a plan to return for Thanksgiving. And after saying goodbye to one another, my family splintered into groups and headed off to our respective homes. There had been a breeze at the beach house, but once we left the island the air grew still. As the heat intensified, so did the general feeling of depression.</p><p begin="00:55:48.02" data-timestamp="3348.02">Throughout the '60s and '70s, the road back to Raleigh took us past Smithfield and a billboard on the outskirts of town that read, "Welcome to Klan Country." This time we took a different route, one my brother recommended. Hugh drove, and my father sat beside him. I slumped down in the back seat next to Amy, and every time I raised my head I'd see the same soybean field or low-slung cinder block building we'd seemingly passed 20 minutes earlier.</p><p begin="00:56:17.57" data-timestamp="3377.57">We'd been on the road for a little more than an hour when we stopped at a farmer's market. Inside an open air pavilion, a woman offered complimentary plates of hummus served with a corn and black bean salad, so we each accepted one and took seats on a bench. 20 years earlier, the most a place like this might have offered was fried okra. Now there was organic coffee and artisanal goat cheese. Above our heads hung a sign that read "Whispering Dove Ranch." And just as I thought that we might be anywhere, I noticed that the music piped through the speakers was Christian, the new kind, which says that Jesus is awesome.</p><p begin="00:56:59.15" data-timestamp="3419.15">Hugh brought my father a plastic cup of water. "You OK, Lou?"</p><p begin="00:57:04.76" data-timestamp="3424.76">"Fine," my father answered.</p><p begin="00:57:09.71" data-timestamp="3429.71">"Why do you think she did it?" I asked as we stepped back into the sunlight. For that's all any of us were thinking, had been thinking since we got the news. Musn't Tiffany have hoped that whatever pills she'd taken wouldn't be strong enough and that her failed attempt would lead her back into our fold? How could anyone purposefully leave us, us of all people?</p><p begin="00:57:34.31" data-timestamp="3454.31">This is how I thought of it, for though I've often lost faith in myself, I've never lost faith in my family in my certainty that we are fundamentally better than everyone else. It's an archaic belief, one I haven't seriously reconsidered since my late teens, but still I hold it. Ours is the only club I'd ever wanted to be a member of, so I couldn't imagine quitting. Backing off for a year or two was understandable, but to want out so badly that you'd take your own life?</p><p begin="00:58:08.97" data-timestamp="3488.97">"I don't know that it had anything to do with us," my father said. But how could it have not? Doesn't the blood of every suicide splash back on our faces?</p><p begin="00:58:24.87" data-timestamp="3504.87">At the far end of the parking lot was a stand selling reptiles. In giant tanks were two pythons, each as big around as a fire hose. The heat seemed to suit them, and I watched as they raised their heads, testing the screened ceilings. Beside the snakes was a low pen corralling an alligator with its mouth banded shut. It wasn't full grown, but perhaps an adolescent, around 3 feet long, and grumpy looking. A girl had stuck her arm through the wire and was stroking the thing's back while it glared, seething. "I'd like to buy everything here just so I could kill it," I said.</p><p begin="00:59:08.12" data-timestamp="3548.12">My father mopped his forehead with Kleenex. "I'm with you, brother."</p><p begin="00:59:15.83" data-timestamp="3555.83">When we were young and would set off for the beach, I'd look out the window at all the landmarks we drove by-- the Purina silo on the south side of Raleigh, the Klan billboard-- knowing that when we passed them a week later I'd be miserable. Our vacation over, now there'd be nothing to live for until Christmas. My life is much fuller than it was back then, yet this return felt no different. "What time is it?" I asked Amy.</p><p begin="00:59:41.99" data-timestamp="3581.99">And instead of saying, "Who cares?" she said, "You tell me. You're the one with the watch on."</p><p begin="00:59:48.89" data-timestamp="3588.89">At the airport a few hours later, I picked sand from my pockets and thought of our final moments at the beach house I'd bought. I was on the front porch with Phyllis, who had just locked the door, and we turned to see the others in the driveway below us. "So is that one of your sisters?" she asked, pointing to Gretchen.</p><p begin="01:00:07.90" data-timestamp="3607.9">"It is," I said. "And so are the two women standing on either side of her."</p><p begin="01:00:13.40" data-timestamp="3613.4">"Then you've got your brother," she observed. "That makes five. Wow. Now, that's a big family."</p><p begin="01:00:21.68" data-timestamp="3621.68">I looked at the sunbaked cars we would soon be climbing into, furnaces every one of them, and said, "Yes. It certainly is."</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="01:00:44.70" data-timestamp="3644.7"> David Sedaris. This story appears in his book, <i>Calypso,</i> and in his forthcoming collection, <i>Best</i> <i>of</i> <i>Me.</i> We want to share the phone number for the National Suicide Prevention Hotline. That number is 1-800-273-8255. Again, 1-800-273-8255.</p><p begin="01:01:08.45" data-timestamp="3668.45">[MUSIC - "DAY AT THE BEACH" BY ATLANTIC THRILLS]</p><p begin="01:01:11.93" data-timestamp="3671.93"></p></div>  </div>

They live in Maine.</p><p begin="00:00:43.20" data-timestamp="43.2">The thing that they wanted more than anything was to get a dining room table. <div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Run On Sentence</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:00.26" data-timestamp="420.26">Act 1, Run On Sentence. So lots of us have things about ourselves that we keep to ourselves that we assume people would see us differently if they knew about. And Mike Anderson had something like that, one thing about him in particular that he kept a secret, until one day it came out. Jessica Lussenhop explains.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Jessica Lussenhop</h4><p begin="00:07:20.19" data-timestamp="440.19">Mike Anderson was 36 years old, married, a suburban father of four. He owned a contracting business and built his family's modest, three-bedroom house in St. Louis from the ground up. He volunteered at church on the weekends and coached his son's football team. That changed last July. He was at home alone with his two-year-old daughter while his wife was away on a business trip.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Mike Anderson</h4><p begin="00:07:39.84" data-timestamp="459.84">I was sleeping. I was awoken. It was about 6 o'clock in the morning, woken by knocking at the door. And it was unusual knocking. It was the consistent knocking, the hard knocking. So I knew something was going on.</p><p begin="00:07:49.35" data-timestamp="469.35">So I just stood at the top of the stairs for a moment. And finally I said, who is it? I'm in my boxers. And they said, marshals. Open it up, or it's coming down. Open up the door.</p><p begin="00:07:57.78" data-timestamp="477.78">As soon as I opened up the door, it was a small army. I mean, it was about eight of them. They had the shields. They had the helmets. And we only ate that once a year.</p><p begin="00:42:12.91" data-timestamp="2532.91">So why did we suddenly stop feeling free enough to change the landscape that way? And so the idea of solving the Meat Question this way, this is sort of where the beautiful poetry of it is. It was this key that unlocked all these other feelings of optimism and ambition and sort of stick-to-itiveness that America wanted to feel again. Because it was a way of saying, we have the smarts and the courage to solve this in a bold way.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:42:43.98" data-timestamp="2563.98">What about, though, the other reaction, which is sort of like, yuck. That's disgusting. I'm not going to eat hippo meat. Yuck.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jon Mooallem</h4><p begin="00:42:52.26" data-timestamp="2572.26">Right. There was a lot of that. There was a kind of campaign to nudge people past that. You had this guy, WN Irwin, at the Department of Agriculture who was helping Burnham and Duquesne promote it. Apparently he had a supply of hippopotamus jerky that he offered to reporters here and there in his office.</p><p begin="00:43:11.70" data-timestamp="2591.7">And I found this story in the <i>Washington</i> <i>Post.</i> He had actually given it to a <i>Washington</i> <i>Post</i> reporter as he showed the <i>Washington</i> <i>Post</i> reporter a photo of that very hippo being skinned in Africa by a crowd of men. And that reporter liked it. "Toughness is only skin deep," is what he wrote.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:43:28.58" data-timestamp="2608.58">So obviously, the state of Louisiana is not covered in hippo ranches. We don't have hippo bacon on the menus of fancy restaurants. So what happened? What got in the way of this vision?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jon Mooallem</h4><p begin="00:43:42.91" data-timestamp="2622.91">Yeah. That's a really good question. What got in the way of this vision? Frankly, a lot of the problem was administrative. They're all writing letters to each other all summer, all three men, trying to get this new food supply society up and running, which was going to be a lobbying firm to promote the idea in the next session of Congress. And I said to my editor on the story, I said, I'm starting to get the impression that if they had had email, this might have actually happened.</p><p begin="00:44:12.71" data-timestamp="2652.71">It's just so infuriating. Duquesne writes to Broussard and says, what can I do for you? But Broussard is in South America. And then when he comes back, he gets the flu. And so you have back and forth with his secretary.</p><p begin="00:44:25.65" data-timestamp="2665.65">Or Burnham is traveling in Mexico and can't be reached. Or someone misses a letter. They never got the letter. So then they write back two weeks later, I never received your reply to my last letter. So it got really messy and sloppy.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Alex Blumberg</h4><p begin="00:44:40.27" data-timestamp="2680.27">You tell this story at the very end of-- there's this scene at the very, very end of this piece that you wrote, where Frederick Burnham is now an older man. It's 1944.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Jon Mooallem</h4><p begin="00:44:52.50" data-timestamp="2692.5">Yeah. 34 years after all this. At this point, he's a very distinguished man. He's become very wealthy. And he was preparing his papers for posterity at both Stanford and Yale. I guess that's how you know you've had an impressive life, when both Stanford and Yale libraries want your archives.</p><p begin="00:45:09.93" data-timestamp="2709.93">And he finds this speech that he had given to a civic organization in Pasadena in 1910. And he reads over the speech. And there he is in 1910, 34 years earlier, making the case to the general public of why this is going to work, why it's necessary, and just urging these laypeople to understand that what he calls the complacent belief in the unending plenty of our natural wealth was now so totally false. It was so obvious that wasn't true anymore.</p><p begin="00:45:42.64" data-timestamp="2742.64">But that here he was, humbly with this idea. He's assuring them. He's saying, the idea made sense. It makes sense. But it's going to require working against what he calls overwhelming difficulties and the loud guffaws of the ignorant to make it a reality.</p><p begin="00:45:57.83" data-timestamp="2757.83">And here he is, he reads this over. And still, he takes a pen and he writes in this very shaky, kind of old-man hand, he writes, "The facts are still unrefuted." And he signs it "FRB, 1944." And then he sends it off to the archives, which is where I found it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:46:24.23" data-timestamp="2784.23">Jon Mooallem talking to Alex Blumberg, who these days you may have heard on the <i>StartUp</i> podcast. You can read Jon's full account of the American hippopotamus, including everything about the crazy rivalry between Fred Burnham and Fritz Duquesne that we were not able to get into, at theativist.com. It's also available on amazon.com as an ebook.</p></div>  </div>

  <h3>Act Four: Start Me Up</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:46:45.53" data-timestamp="2805.53">Act 4, Start Me Up. When comedian Tig Notaro was in sixth grade, she took this music class, and it was the kind of class where they would talk about Beethoven and The Who. And they would play instruments, and they read books. She described what happened in that class in front of a live audience at Largo in Los Angeles.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Tig Notaro</h4><p begin="00:47:02.35" data-timestamp="2822.35">It was so much fun. I just-- I loved it. I was a huge Beatles and Rolling Stones fan. And at the end of every class, the teacher would let one kid play their favorite song for the whole class to hear. And I always brought in Beatles and Rolling Stones albums.</p><p begin="00:47:21.06" data-timestamp="2841.06">And the coolest kid in our entire school, JD-- of course-- he came up to me after school one day, and he said, if I bring in my dad's Rolling Stones album, will you tell me a cool song to play? I said, absolutely. No doubt.</p><p begin="00:47:47.04" data-timestamp="2867.04">Next day, he brought in the Rolling Stones album <i>Let</i> <i>it</i> <i>Bleed.</i> I looked it over. I said, this song. Play this one. And I pointed at "You Can't Always Get What You Want."</p><p begin="00:48:02.27" data-timestamp="2882.27">He said, are you sure? I was like, I couldn't be more positive. This is the coolest song that you could possibly play. And he was like, OK.</p><p begin="00:48:14.03" data-timestamp="2894.03">So the teacher asked if anyone had a song. And JD raised his hand. She called him up to the front. He pointed out that song. And at that point, this is what the entire classroom heard.</p><p begin="00:48:32.95" data-timestamp="2912.95">[MUSIC - "YOU CAN'T ALWAYS GET WHAT YOU WANT" BY  THE ROLLING STONES]</p><p begin="00:48:49.71" data-timestamp="2929.71">JD was furious. He's looking at me like, what is this? And I'm like frantically signing, no, no, no, this is an amazing song. It gets better.</p><p begin="00:49:03.14" data-timestamp="2943.14">[MUSIC - "YOU CAN'T ALWAYS GET WHAT YOU WANT" BY  THE ROLLING STONES]</p><p begin="00:49:05.13" data-timestamp="2945.13"></p><p begin="00:49:24.04" data-timestamp="2964.04">And then the bell rang. Nobody got to hear any more of that song. And the coolest kid in school just marched down there, grabbed his record, and turned to me and was like, thanks a lot, and then just bolted out of the classroom. I was like, no! It gets better! And I guess you can't always get what you want.</p><p begin="00:49:50.52" data-timestamp="2990.52">[APPLAUSE]</p><p begin="00:49:52.95" data-timestamp="2992.95">Thank you so much.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:49:55.37" data-timestamp="2995.37">Tig Notaro, her latest comedy albums and her podcast, <i>Professor</i> <i>Blastoff</i>, are at her website, tignation.com.</p><p begin="00:50:01.82" data-timestamp="3001.82">[MUSIC - "YOU CAN'T ALWAYS GET WHAT YOU WANT" BY  THE ROLLING STONES]</p></div>  </div>

<div class="act" id="act5">
  <h3>Act Five: Happy Accident</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:50:19.62" data-timestamp="3019.62">Act 5, Happy Accident. Etgar Keret always had a good relationship with his dad, except for one thing. This story is true. It all happens in Israel, where Etgar lives, and was read for us in English translation by actor Michael Chernus.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Michael Chernus</h4><p begin="00:50:34.39" data-timestamp="3034.39">"30 years I'm a cabbie," the small guy sitting behind the wheel tells me. "30 years and not one accident." It's been almost an hour since I got into his taxi in Beersheba, and he hasn't stopped talking for a second.</p><p begin="00:50:47.47" data-timestamp="3047.47">Under different circumstances, I would tell them him to shut up, but I don't have the energy for that today. Under different circumstances, I wouldn't shell out 350 shekels to take a taxi to Tel Aviv. I would take the train. But today I feel that I have to get home as early as I can.</p><p begin="00:51:02.78" data-timestamp="3062.78">I spent last night at Ichilov Hospital with my wife. She had a miscarriage and was bleeding heavily. We thought it would be OK, till she passed out. It wasn't until we got to the emergency room that they told us that her life was in danger and gave her a blood transfusion.</p><p begin="00:51:18.85" data-timestamp="3078.85">Three days before that, my dad's doctors told me and my parents that the cancer at the base of his tongue, which had been in remission for four years, was back. And the tumor was at such an advanced stage that the only way to fight it was to remove his tongue and larynx. The oncologist said she didn't recommend having the surgery, but my dad said that he was for it.</p><p begin="00:51:41.05" data-timestamp="3101.05">The oncologist told him that the operation would leave him seriously handicapped, unable to speak or eat. "At my age," my dad said, "all I need are my heart and my eyes so I can enjoy watching my grandchildren grow."</p><p begin="00:51:54.98" data-timestamp="3114.98">When we left the room, the doctor whispered to me, "Try to talk to him." She clearly doesn't know my dad.</p><p begin="00:52:04.61" data-timestamp="3124.61">The taxi driver repeats, for the 100th time, that in 30 years, he hasn't had a single accident and that all of a sudden, five days ago, his car kissed the bumper of the car in front of him, traveling at 20 kilometers per hour. When they stopped and checked, he saw that except for a scratch on the left side of the bumper, the other car hadn't really been damaged at all. He offered the other driver 200 shekels on the spot. But the driver insisted that they exchange insurance information.</p><p begin="00:52:33.96" data-timestamp="3153.96">The next day, the driver, a Russian, asked him to come to a garage. And he and the owner, probably a friend of his, showed him a huge dent all the way on the other side of the car and said the damage was 2,000 shekels. The cab driver refused to pay, and now the other guy's insurance company was suing him. "Don't worry, it'll be OK," I tell him, in the hope that my words will make him stop talking for a minute.</p><p begin="00:52:59.62" data-timestamp="3179.62">"How will it be OK?" he complains. "They're going to screw me. Those bastards are going to squeeze the money out of me. You see how unfair it is? Five days I haven't slept. Do you get what I'm saying?"</p><p begin="00:53:11.21" data-timestamp="3191.21">"Stop thinking about it," I suggest. "Try thinking about other things in your life, happy things."</p><p begin="00:53:18.83" data-timestamp="3198.83">"I can't," the cab driver groans and grimaces. "I just can't."</p><p begin="00:53:23.89" data-timestamp="3203.89">"Then stop talking to me about it," I say. "Keep on thinking and suffering, but just don't tell me about it anymore, OK?"</p><p begin="00:53:30.98" data-timestamp="3210.98">"It's not the money," the taxi driver continues, "believe me. Yesterday I went with my son to the graves of the Sadikim. We bought blessings for 1,800 shekels, and I didn't mind paying. It's the injustice that gets me."</p><p begin="00:53:42.10" data-timestamp="3222.1">"Shut up," I say, finally losing it. "Just shut up for a minute!"</p><p begin="00:53:47.79" data-timestamp="3227.79">"What are you yelling for?" the cab driver asks, insulted. "I'm an old man. It's not nice."</p><p begin="00:53:53.22" data-timestamp="3233.22">"I'm yelling because my father is going to die if they don't cut his tongue out of his mouth," I continue to yell. "I'm yelling because my wife is in the hospital after a miscarriage." The driver is silent for the first time since I got into his taxi. And now I'm suddenly the one who can't stop the stream of words.</p><p begin="00:54:09.38" data-timestamp="3249.38">"Let's make a deal," I say. "Get me to an ATM, and I'll take out 2,000 shekels and give it to you. In exchange, it'll be your father who has to have his tongue removed and your wife who's lying in a hospital bed getting a blood transfusion after a miscarriage."</p><p begin="00:54:23.27" data-timestamp="3263.27">The driver is still silent, and now so am I. I feel a little uncomfortable for having shouted at him but not uncomfortable enough to apologize. To avoid his eyes, I look out the window. We missed the exit to Tel Aviv. I tell him that politely-- or I shouted angrily, I don't recall anymore.</p><p begin="00:54:44.71" data-timestamp="3284.71">He tells me not to worry. He doesn't really know the way, but in a minute, he'll find out. A few seconds later, he parks in the right lane of the highway after managing to convince another driver to stop. He starts to get out of the taxi to ask for directions to Tel Aviv.</p><p begin="00:55:01.66" data-timestamp="3301.66">"You'll kill us both," I tell him. "You can't stop here."</p><p begin="00:55:04.67" data-timestamp="3304.67">"30 years I'm a cabbie," he tosses back at me as he gets out of the taxi. "30 years and not one accident."</p><p begin="00:55:11.61" data-timestamp="3311.61">Alone in the cab, I can feel the tears rising. I don't want to cry. I don't want to feel sorry for myself. I want to be positive like my dad. My wife is fine now, and we already have a wonderful son. My dad survived the Holocaust.</p><p begin="00:55:29.88" data-timestamp="3329.88">That's not just the half-full glass, it's an overflowing one. I don't want to cry, not in this taxi, not next to this driver that I yelled at. The tears are welling up and will soon begin to flow.</p><p begin="00:55:46.27" data-timestamp="3346.27">Suddenly I hear a crashing boom and the sound of windows breaking. The world around me shatters. A silver car veers across the next lane, completely smashed.</p><p begin="00:55:56.80" data-timestamp="3356.8">The taxi moves too, but not on the ground. It floats above it, towards the concrete wall on the side of the road. After it hits, there's another bang. Another car must have hit the taxi.</p><p begin="00:56:17.15" data-timestamp="3377.15">A second before the ambulance drives away, they load the taxi driver into it. Deep in my heart, I was hoping they'd send us in separate ambulances, but it just wasn't my day. The driver, looking revitalized and happy, lights a cigarette. The paramedic wears a yarmulke and tells me I was very lucky. An accident like that with no deaths is a miracle.</p><p begin="00:56:37.93" data-timestamp="3397.93">"The minute you're discharged from the hospital," he says, "you should run to the nearest synagogue and give thanks for still being alive." My cellphone rings. It's my dad. He's only calling to ask how my day at the university was and whether the little one is asleep yet.</p><p begin="00:56:52.56" data-timestamp="3412.56">I tell him that the little one is sleeping and my day at the university was great. And Shira, my wife, is fine, too. She just stepped into the shower.</p><p begin="00:57:01.78" data-timestamp="3421.78">"What's that noise?" he asks.</p><p begin="00:57:04.37" data-timestamp="3424.37">"An ambulance siren," I tell him. I try to sound casual. "One just passed by on the street."</p><p begin="00:57:14.62" data-timestamp="3434.62">Once, five years ago when I was in Sicily with my wife and son, I called my dad to ask how he was. He said everything was fine. In the background, a voice on a loudspeaker was calling a Dr. Shulman to the operating room.</p><p begin="00:57:28.43" data-timestamp="3448.43">"Where are you?" I asked.</p><p begin="00:57:31.29" data-timestamp="3451.29">"In the supermarket," my dad said without a moment's hesitation. "They're announcing on the loudspeaker that someone lost her purse." He sounded so convincing when he said that, so confident and happy.</p><p begin="00:57:42.64" data-timestamp="3462.64">"Why are you crying?" my dad asks now from the other side of the line.</p><p begin="00:57:47.63" data-timestamp="3467.63">I force myself to smile, hoping he can sense it, too. "It's nothing," I say as the ambulance stops next to the emergency ward and the paramedic slams the ambulance doors open. "Really, it's nothing."</p><p begin="00:58:02.07" data-timestamp="3482.07"></p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:58:09.20" data-timestamp="3489.2">Michael Chernus, reading a story by Etgar Keret. Etgar's most recent book is <i>Suddenly</i> <i>a</i> <i>Knock</i> <i>on</i> <i>the</i> <i>Door.</i></p><p begin="00:58:16.29" data-timestamp="3496.29">[MUSIC - "HOW ARE YOU DOING" BY THE LIVING SISTERS"]</p></div>  </div>

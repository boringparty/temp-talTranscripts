  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:00:00.55" data-timestamp="0.55"> Rebecca remembers exactly when she learned the astonishing truth. And guess who the tooth fairy was." I said, "oh my god, who was it? I have to know." And she said, "my dad. My dad is the tooth fairy."</p><p begin="00:00:29.32" data-timestamp="29.32">And I remember running home after school and telling my mom, "mom, I know who the tooth fairy is," and declaring it as if I had grown up that I knew who the tooth fairy was. And she said, "oh, well, who is the tooth fairy?" And I turned to her and I said, "Rachel's dad is the tooth fairy. Ronnie Loeberfeld is the tooth fairy."</p><p begin="00:00:58.23" data-timestamp="58.23">And she said, "I can't believe you know. It's totally secret. You can't let anyone else know.</p><p begin="00:01:04.05" data-timestamp="64.05">But you're right, Ronnie is the tooth fairy, and he works really hard. And it's a secret, so you can't let anyone else know. He is the tooth fairy, but you can't let anyone else know." And from that day on, Ronnie Loeberfeld was the tooth fairy, and all of my notes under my pillow were signed "Love, Ronnie Loeberfeld."</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:01:26.86" data-timestamp="86.86"> Now, in his day job, what did Ronnie Loeberfeld do?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rebecca</h4><p begin="00:01:31.48" data-timestamp="91.48"> I think he did something in finance. He was either an accountant or a stockbroker. He worked next to a Stop &amp; Shop in Massachusetts, in Newton. Had dark hair, wore a suit, and I definitely had images of his driving his Volvo around the Boston area and delivering the tooth fairy treats.</p><p begin="00:01:57.15" data-timestamp="117.15">I remember wondering what it was like for Rachel to know that her dad was the tooth fairy, and definitely being a little envious that her dad had this special job and this special power, and that he had this whole other interesting life, where my dad just came home from work, and that was it.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:14.16" data-timestamp="134.16"> So when you would actually run into Ronnie Loeberfeld, what was it like for you? How would you act?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rebecca</h4><p begin="00:02:20.01" data-timestamp="140.01"> I tried to act cool. I didn't want to-- you know, it's like if you're starstruck but you don't want them to know that you're star struck?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:25.41" data-timestamp="145.41"> Just like meeting a celebrity?</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rebecca</h4><p begin="00:02:26.79" data-timestamp="146.79"> Exactly. You downplay it. You try not to mention it, but you definitely check them out twice and look at them when they walk away. Like, oh my god, you're the tooth fairy.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:37.56" data-timestamp="157.56"> But you knew enough to play it cool.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Rebecca</h4><p begin="00:02:38.83" data-timestamp="158.83"> I knew enough to play it cool. I said, hey, how are you doing. What's for dinner? How am I getting home tonight? Are my parents going to pick me up if they call?</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:02:49.71" data-timestamp="169.71"> You did play it cool.</p></div><div class="host"><p begin="00:02:55.21" data-timestamp="175.21"> One interesting question in all this. Why did both girls come to what seems like the least likely conclusion from the evidence in front of them, of a parent swapping money for a tooth under a pillow? Well, Alison Gopnik studies how children think, and she says, of course it's logical for a seven-year-old to conclude that her own father might be the tooth fairy.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alison Gopnik</h4><p begin="00:03:14.32" data-timestamp="194.32"> Children understand that their parents, for instance, are powerful in all sorts of ways that make them very different from children. Now, from a child's point of view, knowing where those powers begin and end is pretty tricky. I mean, think about all the things that your parents can do that you can't do, and think about the fact that there isn't any obvious explanation about why your father can use a Visa card, for instance.</p></div><div class="interviewer"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:36.34" data-timestamp="216.34"> Yeah.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alison Gopnik</h4><p begin="00:03:36.95" data-timestamp="216.95"> Which is something that you can't do. The power to be a tooth fairy isn't all that much more impressive.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:03:49.78" data-timestamp="229.78"> There's a certain kind of story that kids tell, like the Ronnie Loeberfeld story, where they look at something going on around them, observe it carefully, think about it logically, how one thing connects to the next, and then come to conclusions that are completely incorrect. Therapist Eileen Goldman in Texas tells this story about a little girl on an airplane.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Eileen Goldman</h4><p begin="00:04:07.87" data-timestamp="247.87"> And she was about four years old, and her very first flight. That had been her experience at airports watching airplanes take off-- they do get smaller.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:04:31.64" data-timestamp="271.64"> These stories are like jokes, and they're also like poems, I think because there's this "a-ha" quality to them. And part of what makes these so satisfying as stories is that the logic in them is perfectly reasonable and, at the same time, completely and utterly wrong.</p><p begin="00:04:51.56" data-timestamp="291.56">We at <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life</i> love these stories, and so today we bring you a full hour of them. From WBEC Chicago and Public Radio International, it's <i>This</i> <i>American</i> <i>Life.</i> I'm Ira Glass.</p><p begin="00:05:02.45" data-timestamp="302.45">Today on our show, Kid Logic, our show in four acts. Act One, Baby Scientists with Faulty Data. Act Two, The Game Ain't Over Till the Fatso Man Sings. In that act we hear one boy's explanation of how love works.</p><p begin="00:05:17.93" data-timestamp="317.93">Act Three, Werewolves in Their Youth. <div class="act" id="act1">
  <h3>Act One: Baby Scientists With Faulty Data</h3>
  <div class="act-inner">
    <div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:05:45.54" data-timestamp="345.54"> Act One, Baby Scientists with Faulty Data.</p><p begin="00:05:49.20" data-timestamp="349.2">30 years ago, psychologists and scientists believed that babies could not think at all, that they were irrational and illogical, self-centered little balls of need and want. What science has learned in the last 30 years is that this is not true, that children are observing the world and thinking about it and coming to logical conclusions from the day they're born.</p><p begin="00:06:09.46" data-timestamp="369.46">And Alison Gopnik and two of her colleagues decided to summarize a lot of this research in a book. They called it <i>The</i> <i>Scientist</i> <i>in</i> <i>the</i> <i>Crib,</i> meaning that babies are like little scientists. They argue that when a small baby sits in a highchair and drops a spoon onto the floor over and over and over for mom or dad to pick up, what the baby is doing essentially is running a little baby-sized experiment.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alison Gopnik</h4><p begin="00:06:31.17" data-timestamp="391.17"> Because it turns out that babies are very interested in gravity, and how gravity works. The fact that things fall down and not up is not obvious to babies. And it turns out another thing they're very interested in is human beings and how they work. We are actually the lab rats. They're actually doing experiments on us to see how we tick.</p><p begin="00:06:51.60" data-timestamp="411.6">So when you play "drop the spoon," you get two for the price of one. You get an experiment about gravity, you get a little physics tutorial, and you get a psychology tutorial. You can see about how that person will do something over and over again.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:08.69" data-timestamp="428.69"> While kids think with the same logic that adults use and apply that logic just as rigorously, there are certain things that they simply do not know and take a while to figure out. Up to six or seven years old, for instance, it's not exactly clear to anyone what is imaginary and what is not, or if wishing for something can make it come true.</p></div><div class="subject"><h4>Alison Gopnik</h4><p begin="00:07:28.64" data-timestamp="448.64"> There's a wonderful experiment about this, actually, that Paul Harris in England did, where he got children to imagine that something was in a box. So he would say, OK, now here's this box. We're going to open it up. We're going to close it.</p><p begin="00:07:43.19" data-timestamp="463.19">Now let's imagine that there's a puppy in this box, or else let's imagine that there's a monster in the box. And he asked the children, is there really a monster in the box? Is there really a puppy in the box? They said, no, they were just imagining it.</p></div><div class="host"><h4>Ira Glass</h4><p begin="00:07:56.33" data-timestamp="476.33"> Then the researcher would walk out of the room, leaving the box behind with the child, and then something funny would happen. The kids who were told to imagine a puppy in the box would go over and peek inside the box, just to check.
